# Q19.4 - Work, raising one end of a chain

**The question (Final Exam 1, item 13, 5 points).**
"A chain lying on the ground is $10\text{ m}$ long and its mass is $80\text{ kg}$.
How much work is required to raise one end of the chain to a height of $6\text{ m}$?"

**The answer.** $W = 144g$ joules $= 1411.2$ J, taking $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$.

**What to type into Möbius:** `1411.2`

## 1. What this question is really testing

Q19.1, Q19.2 and Q19.3 all handed you a function and asked you to integrate it.
This one does not.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Build an integrand from a physical slice** | Q19.5 (spring), Q19.8, Q19.9, and every "work" or "force" question |
| Choose a slicing variable and state the origin | the same, plus any centre-of-mass or fluid-force question |
| Notice that different slices move different distances | the single idea this question exists to test |
| Keep weight and mass apart | Q19.5, where the units flip to imperial and $g$ must vanish |
| Sanity-check against a trivial bound | every applications-of-integration question |

**The one sentence.**
Nothing is handed to you as a function to integrate; you have to build the integrand out of a picture, and the picture's whole content is that a chain raised at one end has slices that travel *different* distances.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2a. Work is a product, and the integral is there because one factor varies

For a constant force $F$ moving an object a constant distance $d$:

$$W = F \cdot d$$

The integral appears the moment *either* factor stops being constant.
There are exactly two shapes, and confusing them is the commonest structural error in the whole topic:

| Shape | What varies | The integral | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Variable force** | $F$ changes with position $x$ | $W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$ | a spring, a leaky bucket |
| **Variable distance** | each *piece* of the object moves a different distance | $W = \int (\text{weight of a slice})\times(\text{rise of that slice})$ | a chain, a cable, a tank of water |

Q19.4 is the second shape.
Q19.5 is the first shape.
They are both "work", and they are not the same calculation.

### 2b. The slice method, in four moves

> 1. **Set up a coordinate.** Draw the *final* picture, put an origin somewhere convenient, and name the variable.
> 2. **Cut.** Take a slice of thickness $d(\text{variable})$ and write its **weight**.
> 3. **Move it.** Read off, from the picture, how far *that particular slice* travelled.
> 4. **Add.** $W = \int (\text{weight of the slice}) \times (\text{rise of the slice})$, over the range of the variable.

Move 3 is where the question is won or lost.
Moves 1, 2 and 4 are bookkeeping.

### 2c. Weight versus mass, the classic lost mark

This is invisible to every numerical check, because a wrong answer that is off by a factor of $9.8$ still looks like a number.

| The stem gives you | What it is | Do you multiply by $g$? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $80$ kg, $8$ kg/m, "mass" | **mass** | **yes** - weight $=$ mass $\times\, g$ |
| $500$ N, $78.4$ N/m | **weight** (a force) | **no** - $g$ is already inside it |
| $2$ lb/ft, $50$ lb, "weighs" | **weight** (imperial) | **no** - the pound is a force |
| $3$ slugs, $12$ kg/m$^3$ | **mass** | **yes** |

Q19.4 says **mass**, in kilograms.
So $g$ belongs in the integrand, and the linear *weight* density is

$$\lambda g = \frac{80\text{ kg}}{10\text{ m}}\times 9.8\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2} = 78.4\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}}$$

Omitting $g$ gives $144$, which is exactly the answer divided by $9.8$.
Q19.5 is the mirror image: it is stated in foot-pounds, so inserting $g$ there is the same mistake running the other way.

### 2d. The constants carry units too

The $10$ is $10$ metres, the $80$ is $80$ kilograms, the $6$ is $6$ metres, and the $80/10$ is $8$ kilograms **per metre**.
A student who reads them as bare numbers has no way to notice when a conversion is needed.
Redoing the whole problem in grams and centimetres gives $1.4112\times 10^{10}$ erg, which converts back to exactly $1411.2$ J - an independent arm that catches a conversion applied to only one of the two factors.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. Decide which shape you are in: **variable force** or **variable distance**.
> 2. If variable distance: draw the **final** configuration, not the initial one.
> 3. Pick a slicing variable and **write down where the origin is**. The integrand depends on that choice; the answer does not.
> 4. Write the weight of one slice. Check whether the stem gave mass or weight.
> 5. Write the rise of that slice, **as a function of the slicing variable**, from the picture.
> 6. Integrate over the range of the variable that actually indexes slices of the object.
> 7. Check units, then check the trivial bounds.

### The classify table

| Cue in the stem | Shape | Slicing variable | Rise |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "raise one end of a chain/rope to height $h$" | distance | height $y$, or arclength $s$ | $y$, or $h-s$ |
| "haul up a hanging cable" | distance | depth $d$ below the top | $d$ |
| "lift the whole thing $h$ metres" | neither, it is a product | none | constant $h$ |
| "pump the water out over the rim" | distance | height $y$ of the layer | $(\text{rim}) - y$ |
| "stretch a spring", "compress" | force | extension $x$ | not applicable |
| "the bucket leaks" | force | height $y$ | not applicable |

## 4. Q19.4 worked four ways

Set $L = 10$ m, $M = 80$ kg, $H = 6$ m, $g = 9.8$ m/s$^2$, and $\lambda = M/L = 8$ kg/m.

Because $H < L$, only part of the chain leaves the ground.
The final picture is: **$6$ m of chain hanging vertically over the heights $0$ to $6$, and $4$ m still lying on the ground.**
That splits the mass $48$ kg moving, $32$ kg not moving.

### Route A - slice by HEIGHT, origin on the ground

Let $y$ measure height upward from the ground, $0 \le y \le 6$.
In the final picture the hanging part occupies exactly those heights, so the slice now sitting at height $y$ has weight $\lambda g\,dy$.
It started on the ground, so **it rose $y$**.

$$W = \int_0^{6} \lambda g\, y\,dy = \lambda g\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^{6} = 78.4 \times 18 = 1411.2\text{ J}$$

This is the route to write on the paper.

### Route B - slice by ARCLENGTH, origin at the raised end

Now index the chain by $s$, the distance along the chain from the end you are lifting, $0 \le s \le 10$.
This changes both the variable and the origin, so it is a genuinely independent derivation rather than the same algebra rearranged.

After the lift, the material point at arclength $s$ sits at height $H - s$ while $s \le 6$, and back on the ground for $s > 6$.
Everything started at height $0$, so the rise is $\max(H-s,\,0)$:

$$W = \int_0^{10} \lambda g \max(6-s,0)\,ds = \lambda g\int_0^{6}(6-s)\,ds = \lambda g\left[6s - \frac{s^2}{2}\right]_0^{6} = 78.4 \times 18 = 1411.2\text{ J}$$

Note that the integral now runs over the **whole** chain and the last $4$ m contributes zero, which is the picture stating "that part never moved".

**The two routes agree to the last bit of floating point**, not merely to a tolerance.
That is the strongest available check on a modelling question, because it tests the setup rather than the arithmetic - a wrong choice of "which slice travels how far" would not survive being re-derived from a different origin.

### Route C - slice by arclength from the GROUNDED end

A third origin, for free.
Let $u$ be the distance from the end that never leaves the ground.
That end is $4$ m from where the chain starts to rise, so the point at $u$ ends at height $\max(u-4,\,0)$:

$$W = \int_{4}^{10}\lambda g\,(u-4)\,du = 78.4\times 18 = 1411.2\text{ J}$$

Three origins, one answer.

### Route D - the centre-of-mass shortcut

Only the top $6$ m moves.
That piece is uniform, so its centre of mass sits at its midpoint and rises from $0$ to $3$ m:

$$W = (\text{moving mass})\times g \times (\text{rise of its centre of mass}) = 48 \times 9.8 \times 3 = 1411.2\text{ J}$$

**This shortcut is valid here**, and it is worth knowing exactly why, because it is not valid in general.
It works whenever the rise is a *linear* function of the mass coordinate, since then the average rise over the moving mass equals the rise of the average position.
Both of this question's conditions hold: the density is constant and the rise is $y$ itself.

Two guard rails, both checked numerically:

- It **still works** on a chain of variable density, provided the rise is still linear - because the centre of mass is defined by exactly that weighting.
- It **fails** the moment the rise stops being linear. Replacing the rise $y$ with $y^2/6$ makes the true work $940.8$ J while the shortcut returns $705.6$ J.

And there is a much more likely way to break it, described in section 9: applying it to the **whole** $80$ kg instead of the moving $48$ kg.

### Route comparison

| Route | What it costs | When to use it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A - slice by height** | one line of setup, one integral | **use this one under pressure** |
| B - slice by arclength | same integral, more thought about the limits | use it as a check, or when the stem is phrased along the chain |
| C - arclength from the far end | an extra subtraction to get the limits right | rarely worth it |
| D - centre of mass | no integral at all | as an eight-second check, never as the written method |

Route A wins because the coordinate you pick is the one the final picture already draws for you.
Route D is the fastest but it is the one that punishes a careless reading, so use it to confirm a number you obtained another way.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

These are the sentences that earn the method marks, in order.

> Let $y$ be the height above the ground, measured upward, with the origin at ground level.
> The chain has linear mass density $\lambda = 80/10 = 8$ kg/m, so a slice of thickness $dy$ has weight $8g\,dy = 78.4\,dy$ N.
> Since $6 < 10$, the chain ends up with $6$ m hanging over the heights $0$ to $6$ and $4$ m still on the ground.
> The slice that finishes at height $y$ began on the ground, so it is raised through a distance $y$.
> Hence $dW = 78.4\,y\,dy$ and
> $$W = \int_0^{6} 78.4\,y\,dy = 78.4\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^6 = 78.4(18) = 1411.2\text{ J}.$$

Four things in that paragraph do work:
naming the origin, dividing mass by length, saying which part of the chain moves, and saying how far the slice at $y$ travelled.
The integral is the fifth line and the easiest one.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The eight-second checks, in the order they catch things

| Check | On this question | What it catches |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Units** | (kg/m)(m/s$^2$)(m)(m) $=$ J | a missing or doubled $g$, a dropped $dy$ |
| **Tight bracket** | $0 < W < 48g(6) = 2822.4$ | the largest class of errors, see below |
| **Trivial bracket** | $0 < W < 80g(6) = 4704$ | a wrong density, a doubled $g$ |
| **Which mass moves** | $48$ kg moves, $32$ kg does not | applying anything to the full $80$ kg |
| **Mass or weight** | the stem says kg, so $g$ is required | the $9.8$ factor, in either direction |

**The tight bracket is the strongest single check available here, and that was measured rather than guessed.**
Nothing can move further than $6$ m, and nothing but the top $48$ kg moves at all, so $W$ must be under $48 \times 9.8 \times 6 = 2822.4$ J.
Against the seventeen candidates in section 9's census, the tight bracket rejects **nine**, the trivial bracket rejects **four**, and a positivity check rejects **none**.
It is also a strict superset: everything the trivial bracket catches, the tight bracket catches too.

### Where the answer sits in its range

$1411.2$ is **exactly $30$ percent** of the trivial upper bound $4704$, and **exactly half** of the tight bound $2822.4$.
Neither of those is a coincidence.
The first is $H/(2L) = 6/20$, and the second is the statement that a uniform chain's average rise is half its maximum rise.
So on any version of this question, the answer is half of "the moving mass through the full height" - which makes that ratio a genuine check rather than a numerological one.

### The check that is inert here, and why you should know that

Work, as this question asks for it, is a positive quantity, and every plausible wrong answer is positive too.
So the "an amount must be positive" repair that rescues Q19.1 does **nothing** on Q19.4.
This matters because Q19.1 and Q19.3 both reward that repair and Q19.2 punishes it; Q19.4 is the third behaviour, where it neither helps nor harms.
Read section 9 before trusting a sign check on any question in this category.

### Numbers worth memorising

| Quantity | Value |
| ---: | :--- |
| $\lambda$, linear mass density | $8$ kg/m |
| $\lambda g$, linear weight density | $78.4$ N/m |
| mass that moves | $48$ kg |
| mass that does not | $32$ kg |
| rise of the moving centre of mass | $3$ m |
| $\int_0^6 y\,dy$ | $18$ |
| **the answer** | **$1411.2$ J $= 144g$** |
| trivial upper bound | $4704$ J |
| tight upper bound | $2822.4$ J |
| weight now hanging, $\lambda g H$ | $470.4$ N |
| the same chain raised to $10$ m | $3920$ J |

That last row is worth its own note: $470.4$ N is exactly $dW/dH$ at $H=6$.
The marginal cost of raising the end one more metre is the weight currently hanging, which is a good physical check that the model is behaving.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read this as **structure**, not as thirteen facts.
There are only two shapes in the whole topic (variable force, variable distance), and only three questions to answer for any of them: what indexes the slices, what does one slice weigh, and how far does that slice go.
Every family below is those three answers filled in differently.

Every value in every table was computed twice - once from a closed form, once from a formula-blind midpoint sum that knows only the slice picture.

### Family 0 - one end raised, with $H \le L$ (this question)

**Trigger:** "raise one end to a height of $h$", and $h$ is no bigger than the object's length.
**Tool:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda g h^2$.
**Why:** the part still on the ground contributes nothing, and $L$ never enters.

| $L$ | $M$ | $h$ | $W$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $10$ m | $80$ kg | $6$ m | $1411.2$ J |
| $10$ m | $80$ kg | $4$ m | $627.2$ J |
| $12$ m | $60$ kg | $5$ m | $612.5$ J |

**The structural fact:** $W$ is independent of $L$ except through $\lambda$.
Halve the length while keeping the density and the answer does not move.

### Family 1 - one end raised, with $H \ge L$

**Trigger:** the target height is at least the chain's length, so the whole chain leaves the ground.
**Tool:** $W = \lambda g\left(\tfrac{1}{2}L^2 + L(h-L)\right)$.
**Why:** once the chain is clear, every further metre lifts the *entire* weight, so the cost becomes linear.

| $h$ | $W$ | note |
| ---: | ---: | :--- |
| $10$ m | $3920$ J | the two regimes meet here |
| $15$ m | $7840$ J | $3920$ for the lift plus $3920$ for the extra $5$ m |
| $20$ m | $11760$ J | linear from here on |

**The structural fact:** below $L$ the graph of $W$ against $h$ is a parabola; at $h=L$ it becomes a straight line, and the two pieces join smoothly because the slope $\lambda g h$ saturates at $\lambda g L = $ the whole weight.

### Family 2 - a uniform hoist, where every slice moves the same distance

**Trigger:** "lift the whole chain $6$ m", "raise the object to a height of...".
**Tool:** $W = Mgd$, a product with no integral.
**Why:** the rise is constant, so it comes out of the integral and what is left is the total mass.

Lifting all $80$ kg through $6$ m is $4704$ J - which is exactly Q19.4's trivial upper bound, because that is what "every slice travels the maximum distance" means.
**This family is the commonest wrong answer to Family 0**, and telling the two apart is the entire skill.

### Family 3 - a hanging chain hauled to the top

**Trigger:** "a cable hangs from the top of a building", "pull all the rope up".
**Tool:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2}\lambda g L^2$, with the slice at depth $d$ below the top rising $d$.
**Why:** it is Family 0 with $h = L$, drawn upside down.

| $L$ | $M$ | $W$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $10$ m | $80$ kg | $3920$ J |
| $30$ m | $45$ kg | $6615$ J |

**The structural fact worth carrying:** hauling up a hanging $10$ m chain and raising a grounded $10$ m chain's end to $10$ m are **the same number** by two completely different pictures.

### Family 4 - weight per unit length given, so no $g$ anywhere

**Trigger:** "weighs $2$ lb/ft", "a $50$ lb cable", any imperial force unit.
**Tool:** $W = \tfrac{1}{2}w h^2$ with $w$ the weight per length.
**Why:** the pound is already a force; $g$ is inside it.

| $w$ | $h$ | $W$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $2.5$ lb/ft | $6$ ft | $45$ ft-lb |
| $3$ lb/ft | $8$ ft | $96$ ft-lb |

This is the family that makes the weight-versus-mass distinction of section 2c cost a mark rather than merely being pedantry.

### Family 5 - variable linear density

**Trigger:** "the density at a distance $x$ from one end is ...".
**Tool:** the same integral, with $\lambda$ inside it rather than outside.
**Why:** nothing about the slice argument changes; only one factor stops being constant.

Worked instance: a $6$ m chain of density $(2+x)$ kg/m at distance $x$ from the raised end, that end taken to $6$ m so the whole chain hangs.
The slice at $x$ rises $6-x$:

$$W = \int_0^6 (2+x)g(6-x)\,dx = g\int_0^6 (12 + 4x - x^2)\,dx = 72g = 705.6\text{ J}$$

The chain's total mass is $30$ kg, and a *uniform* $30$ kg chain of the same length gives a different answer - which is the check that the variable density is actually doing work in the integral.

### Family 6 - a rope plus a payload

**Trigger:** "a bucket of mass $m$ is lifted on a rope weighing ...".
**Tool:** $W = mgh + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho g h^2$, one term from Family 2 and one from Family 0.
**Why:** the payload's rise is constant, the rope's is not.

| Piece | Value |
| :--- | ---: |
| $10$ kg bucket raised $12$ m | $1176$ J |
| $0.5$ kg/m rope, same lift | $352.8$ J |
| total | $1528.8$ J |

**The structural fact:** work is additive over the parts of the system, which is what lets you split a hard problem into two easy families.

### Family 7 - a leaky bucket

**Trigger:** "the bucket leaks at ... while it is raised".
**Tool:** $W = \int_0^h F(y)\,dy$ with $F$ the *weight at height $y$*.
**Why:** this is the **variable-force** shape, not the variable-distance one. The whole object travels the whole distance; it is the force that changes.

A bucket whose mass is $(20 - 0.5y)$ kg at height $y$, raised $10$ m, costs $175g = 1715$ J - less than the $1960$ J it would cost if nothing leaked.

**This family is the reason section 2a exists.**
Family 7 and Family 0 produce structurally identical-looking integrals from opposite physical arguments, and writing the right integral for the wrong reason is how a student passes this question and fails the next one.

### Family 8 - pumping liquid out of a tank

**Trigger:** "pump the water over the rim", "empty the tank".
**Tool:** $W = \int \rho g A(y)\,(\text{rim} - y)\,dy$.
**Why:** the slice is a horizontal layer, so its weight involves the cross-sectional **area**, which may itself vary with $y$.

A cylinder of radius $2$ m and depth $5$ m, full of water, pumped over the rim: $W = 490000\pi \approx 1.5394\times 10^6$ J.
A cone of the same height, narrowing downward, costs less - because more of its water starts near the rim.

This is Family 0's argument with one extra factor, and it is the standard next question after this one.

### Family 9 - a spring, which is Q19.5

**Trigger:** Hooke's law, "stretch beyond its natural length".
**Tool:** $F(x) = kx$, $W = \int_a^b kx\,dx$.
**Why:** variable force again, and $x$ measures extension, not position.

Q19.5's own numbers: $20$ ft-lb stretches the spring $1$ ft, so $k = 2(20)/1^2 = 40$ lb/ft, and stretching it $2$ ft costs $\int_0^2 40x\,dx = 80$ ft-lb.
Note that **no $g$ appears anywhere**, because the pound is a force - the mirror image of Q19.4.

### Family 10 - a general variable force

**Trigger:** "a force of $F(x)$ acts along ...".
**Tool:** $W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$, with no modelling at all.
**Why:** the integrand is handed to you, so this is really a Q17-style definite integral wearing a physics costume.

$F(x) = 3x^2 + 2$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ gives $72$.

### Family 11 - the question run backwards

**Trigger:** "how high must the end be raised for the work to be ...", "find the density given the work".
**Tool:** solve $\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda g h^2 = W$ for the unknown.
**Why:** the relation is invertible on $[0, L]$ because $W$ is strictly increasing there.

For Q19.4's chain, $1000$ J corresponds to $h = \sqrt{2000/78.4} = 5.0508$ m.
Always check the answer lies in $[0,L]$; a target above $3920$ J has no Family 0 solution at all and needs Family 1.

### Family 12 - a difference of works

**Trigger:** "how much *additional* work", "raise it a further ...".
**Tool:** $W(h_2) - W(h_1)$.
**Why:** work is a difference of a state function, so the intermediate history does not matter.

Going from $6$ m to $8$ m costs $1097.6$ J - which is **more** than the first $2$ m cost ($156.8$ J), because there is more chain hanging by then.
That monotone increase is a good structural check.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs a mark.

1. **Treating every slice as if it travelled the full $6$ m.**
   This is Family 2's formula on Family 0's question, and it gives $4704$ J.
   It is the single commonest wrong answer in this topic, and it is precisely the trivial upper bound - so it is the one error the bracket check cannot mistake for a right answer.
2. **Applying the centre-of-mass shortcut to the whole $80$ kg.**
   $80 \times 9.8 \times 3 = 2352$ J.
   The average rise of $3$ m is correct; the mass it is applied to is not, because $32$ kg never left the ground.
   This is the seductive one, because every individual step in it is true of *something*.
3. **Omitting $g$.**
   $144$ J, exactly the answer over $9.8$.
   Invisible to any check that does not look at units.
4. **Inserting $g$ where the stem already gave a weight.**
   The same mistake in reverse, and the one waiting on Q19.5.
5. **Integrating to $L$ instead of $H$.**
   $3920$ J, which is the answer to a different and perfectly sensible question (Family 1 at $h=L$).
   Nothing about the number looks wrong.
6. **Dropping the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ from $\int y\,dy$.**
   $2822.4$ J, exactly double.
7. **Using $M$ where $\lambda$ belongs**, or dividing by $H$ instead of $L$.
   $14112$ J and $2352$ J respectively.
8. **Measuring the rise as $6-y$ instead of $y$.**
   This one **hides**; see below.

### The census, and which errors hide

Seventeen candidate wrong answers were enumerated, each derived from a named mistake rather than typed, with no point excluded as degenerate.

| Mistake | Value |
| :--- | ---: |
| every slice travels the full $6$ m (whole chain) | $4704$ |
| lifted mass times full length | $4704$ |
| $g$ inserted twice | $13829.76$ |

**Exactly one candidate hides**: measuring each slice's rise downward as $6-y$ rather than $y$ returns $1411.2$ J, the correct answer, from an incorrect picture.
It hides because $\int_0^6 y\,dy = \int_0^6 (6-y)\,dy = 18$ - the rise is symmetric about the midpoint of the interval, so reversing it changes nothing.
That symmetry breaks the moment the interval does not start at zero, which is why Drill D3 and Family 5 are worth doing: they remove the hiding point.

**One more is a near-miss rather than a hider:** using $g = 9.81$ gives $1412.64$, which is $1.44$ J away.
That is not an error of method, and if the paper's own convention is $9.81$ it is the right answer.

**The magnitude pattern.**
Twelve candidates are larger than the truth and four are smaller.
Every single one is **positive**, so the positivity repair that rescues Q19.1 and Q19.3 rejects **zero** of them.
Four values are hit by more than one distinct mistake, and $2822.4$ is hit by **three** - it is exactly twice the answer, so the constant-distance-on-the-lifted-part error, the dropped-$\tfrac12$ error and a bare factor-of-two slip all land on it.
Nine of the seventeen candidates sit on a value that some other mistake also produces, which means "two methods agreed" is weak evidence on this question unless the two methods used different origins.

### Which pattern this is: neither Q19.1's nor Q19.2's

The queue has now measured three behaviours of the same repair:

| Question | The repair | What it does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Q19.1 | "an area is positive" | rescues all three wrong candidates |
| Q19.2 | "an area is positive" | produces a confident **wrong** answer |
| Q19.3 | "an amount is positive" | rescues the one sign error, harmlessly |
| **Q19.4** | "work is positive" | **inert - it rejects nothing at all** |

So Q19.4 is a fourth case, and it changes what you should reach for.
On an area or net-change question the sign is the cheap discriminator; on a work question there is no sign to check, and **the magnitude bracket is the only cheap check that discriminates**.
That is the single most transferable sentence on this page.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
Is a WORK question in front of you?
|
+-- Does the WHOLE object move the SAME distance?
|      YES -> W = (total weight) x (distance).  No integral.        [Family 2]
|      NO  -> continue
|
+-- Is it the FORCE that varies, with one object on one path?
|      YES -> W = int F(x) dx over the path.        [Families 7, 9, 10]
|      NO  -> it is the DISTANCE that varies; slice it.
|
+-- SLICE:  pick a variable, STATE the origin
|      chain raised at one end      -> y = height,  rise = y,   0..h
|      chain hanging, hauled up     -> d = depth,   rise = d,   0..L
|      liquid pumped out            -> y = layer,   rise = top - y
|
+-- WEIGHT of one slice
|      stem gave MASS (kg)      -> multiply by g
|      stem gave WEIGHT (N, lb) -> do NOT multiply by g
|
+-- W = int (weight of slice) x (rise of slice)
|
+-- CHECK
|      units      -> must come out in J (or ft-lb)
|      which mass actually moves -> is it all of it?
|      bracket    -> 0 < W < (moving weight) x (max rise)
|      halfway    -> a uniform chain gives exactly half that bound
|
+-- PREVIEW the Mobius box.  Number only, no unit suffix.
```

**What Q19.3 contributes to this page.**
Its section 2a establishes that the units of the integrand times the units of the differential must equal the units of the answer, and that the stem's constants carry units of their own.
That is the gate reused in section 2c and 2d here, with the difference that Q19.3's constants were already in litres per minute, whereas Q19.4's are in kilograms and need $g$ to become a force.

### What was verified, and how

- The answer was derived **three** times from three different origins (height from the ground, arclength from the raised end, arclength from the grounded end) and once more by a centre-of-mass argument, and all four agree to the last bit of floating point.
- The definite integral was evaluated by exact antiderivative, by exact rational arithmetic (with $g = 49/5$, giving $W = 7056/5$ exactly), by composite Gauss-Legendre, by tanh-sinh quadrature, and by composite Simpson.
- **The observed quadrature order on this integrand is unmeasurable**, and that is reported rather than papered over: $\lambda g y$ is linear, Simpson is exact on cubics, and every error on the ladder is pure roundoff. Exactness was asserted instead, which is a stronger statement than a converged order. The instrument was then demonstrated on controls - a smooth one converging to $4.00$, a cusp one converging to $5/3$ - with $n$ chosen off every multiple that aligns with a corner.
- Route B's integrand *does* have a corner, at $3/5$ of its interval, and Simpson's measured order there is **$2$, not $4$**, on any $n$ that misses the corner, while every $n$ divisible by $10$ reports a spurious zero error.
- The units were checked as a **separate dimensional gate** with seeded rejections: forgetting $g$, doubling $g$, dropping $dy$, and an inhomogeneous sum were each confirmed to be rejected before the gate's acceptance of the joule was trusted. The whole problem was then re-integrated in cgs units as an independent arm.
- The answer was confirmed strictly inside both trivial bounds, at exactly $30$ percent of the range.
- Seventeen wrong answers were enumerated and classified; one hides, one is a near-miss, and the rejection counts for each check were derived from the candidate list rather than typed.
