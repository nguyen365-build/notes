## 2. The six things you must have cold

### 2.1 What a fractional power means when the base is negative

$x^2 + 2x = x(x+2)$ is **negative** for every $x$ strictly between $-2$ and $0$.
So on the middle of the interval you are raising a negative number to the power $2/3$, and you have
to know which reading is intended.

| Reading | Formula | Value at $x=-1$ (base $=-1$) | Real on $(-2,0)$? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cube the root | $\left(u^{1/3}\right)^2$ | $(-1)^2 = 1$ | yes |
| Root the cube (used here) | $\left(u^{2}\right)^{1/3}$ | $1^{1/3} = 1$ | yes |
| Naive floating point | `(-1.0)**(2/3)` | a complex number | no |

Both correct readings agree, and both give $|u|^{2/3}$.
**An even numerator kills the sign.**
That is why $f(x) \ge 0$ everywhere and why the minimum can be exactly $0$.

Contrast Q15.1(a), where the exponent $5/3$ has an **odd** numerator and the sign survives:
$(-1)^{5/3} = -1$.

### 2.2 The derivative, and its domain

Write $u = x^2+2x$, so $u' = 2x+2 = 2(x+1)$.

$$f(x) = u^{2/3}, \qquad f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}u^{-1/3}u' = \frac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}}$$

The domain of $f$ is all of $\mathbb{R}$.
**The domain of $f'$ is not.**
The cube root in the denominator is zero exactly when $x^2+2x = 0$, so

$$\operatorname{dom}(f') = \mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,\,0\}$$

Writing the derivative's domain down is the step that produces the answer.
If you only ever solve $f'(x)=0$ you get $x=-1$ and you never see the other two points at all.

### 2.3 Why a cusp is a critical point

The definition of a critical point of $f$ on an interval is

> $c$ is a **critical point** if $c$ is in the domain of $f$ and either $f'(c)=0$ or $f'(c)$ does not exist.

The second clause is not a technicality bolted on for completeness.
Fermat's theorem says that an interior extremum forces $f'(c)=0$ **only if $f'(c)$ exists**.
Where $f'$ does not exist, Fermat's theorem says nothing at all, so the point cannot be ruled out and
must be carried as a candidate.

At $x=0$ this function has its **minimum**, and $f'(0)$ does not exist.
A method that only collects zeros of $f'$ is not a slightly incomplete method here.
It is a method that cannot see the answer.

### 2.4 How to FIND the points where $f'$ fails to exist

You do not hunt for them by looking at a graph.
You read them off the derivative you already wrote down.

| Where to look | What triggers it | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A **denominator** of $f'$ | it vanishes at some $x$ in the interval | $\dfrac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}}$ at $x=0,-2$ |
| A **fractional power** $g(x)^{p}$ with $0<p<1$ | $g(x)=0$ | $x^{2/3}$, $x^{1/3}$, $(x-4)^{4/5}$ |
| An **even root** $\sqrt[2k]{g(x)}$ | $g(x)=0$, and the domain also ends there | $\sqrt{x}$ at $x=0$ |
| An **absolute value** $\lvert g(x)\rvert$ | $g(x)=0$ and $g'$ is nonzero there | $\lvert x\rvert$, $\lvert x^2-4\rvert$ |
| A **piecewise** definition | at each seam | any split-rule function |

**The discriminator for a fractional power, and it is worth memorising.**
For $g(x)^{p}$ with $g(c)=0$ and $g'(c)\ne 0$:

- $p < 1$: $f'$ blows up at $c$, so $c$ is a critical point of the **undefined** kind.
- $p \ge 1$: $f'$ is finite at $c$ (and usually zero), so $c$ is an ordinary critical point or not one at all.

Here $p = 2/3 < 1$, so both zeros of the base are critical points.
This is measured, not asserted: the harness probes six exponents ($1/3$, $2/3$, $1$, $4/3$, $5/3$, $2$)
and the slope blows up on exactly the two with $p<1$.

### 2.5 Cusp, corner, vertical tangent: three different things

All three are points where $f'$ fails to exist, all three are critical points, and only one of them
is a cusp.
`MATH265_Review.md` gets this wrong three times and right twice, so do not settle it from the notes.
Settle it from the two **one-sided** limits of $f'$.

| Species | $\lim_{x\to c^-}f'(x)$ | $\lim_{x\to c^+}f'(x)$ | Picture | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **corner** | finite $L_1$ | finite $L_2 \ne L_1$ | a kink with two visible slopes | $\lvert x\rvert$ at $0$: $-1$ and $+1$ |
| **vertical tangent** | $+\infty$ | $+\infty$ (same sign) | the curve stands up and keeps going | $x^{1/3}$ at $0$ |
| **cusp** | $-\infty$ | $+\infty$ (opposite signs) | the curve comes to a spike | $x^{2/3}$ at $0$ |
| smooth | $L$ | the same $L$ | nothing to see | $x^{4/3}$ at $0$ |

**Classification here, from the one-sided limits, not from a picture.**
Near $x=0$ the numerator $4(x+1)\to 4$, a fixed positive number, and the denominator
$3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}$ approaches $0$ **through positive values from the right** and **through negative
values from the left**, because $x(x+2)$ changes sign at $0$.
Therefore

$$\lim_{x\to 0^-}f'(x) = -\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0^+}f'(x) = +\infty$$

Opposite signs, both infinite.
**It is a cusp.**
Numerically, $f'(-10^{-9}) \approx -1058.27$ and $f'(+10^{-9}) \approx +1058.27$, and both grow
without bound as the step shrinks.

The same computation at $x=-2$ gives $-\infty$ on the left and $+\infty$ on the right, so $x=-2$ is a
cusp too on the natural domain.
Inside $[-2,3]$ only its right branch exists, so within the exam's interval $x=-2$ behaves as a
one-sided vertical rise at the left end.
It is on the candidate list anyway, because it is an endpoint.

### 2.6 The centred difference quotient is a trap here

The reflex check "compute $\frac{f(c+h)-f(c-h)}{2h}$ and see if it is near zero" is what most people
reach for, and on this function it gives the **wrong answer with total confidence**.

| $h$ | centred quotient at $x=0$ | right one-sided | left one-sided |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $10^{-3}$ | $5.291337\times10^{-3}$ | $+15.879301$ | $-15.868719$ |
| $10^{-6}$ | $5.291337\times10^{-5}$ | $+158.740158$ | $-158.740052$ |
| $10^{-9}$ | $5.291338\times10^{-7}$ | $+1587.401052$ | $-1587.401051$ |
| $10^{-12}$ | $5.290647\times10^{-9}$ | $+15874.010520$ | $-15874.010520$ |

The centred quotient reads as $5.29\times10^{-5}$ while the true one-sided slopes are past $158$ in
both directions, an understatement by a factor **above one million**.
It does this because the two one-sided errors are near mirror images and cancel in the subtraction.
On a symmetric **corner** it returns exactly $0$; on an asymmetric corner it returns the **average**
of the two slopes; at a **cusp** it returns something tiny and meaningless.
Three behaviours, one method, and none of them is the derivative.

**Test one-sided slopes separately, always.**
And notice the divergence rate, because it is a useful fingerprint: the one-sided quotient here grows
like $h^{-1/3}$, so shrinking $h$ by $1000$ multiplies the quotient by exactly $10$.
The closed form is $\left(h^2+2h\right)^{2/3}/h \to 2^{2/3}h^{-1/3}$, and $2^{2/3}=1.5874010520$,
which is where the digits $15874$ above come from.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Check the hypotheses.** $f$ continuous on $[a,b]$, the interval closed and bounded. The Extreme Value Theorem then guarantees both extremes are attained. It locates neither.
> 2. **Fix the reading of the function.** If a fractional power can meet a negative base, decide now whether the sign survives. Even numerator: it does not. Odd numerator over odd denominator: it does.
> 3. **Differentiate, and write the derivative's DOMAIN next to it.** Not just the formula.
> 4. **Collect the zeros.** Set the numerator of $f'$ to zero.
> 5. **Collect the non-existence points.** Set the denominator of $f'$ to zero, and add any absolute-value seam or piecewise join. Keep only those inside $[a,b]$.
> 6. **Add both endpoints.** Unconditionally. They are not optional and they are not "usually not it".
> 7. **Evaluate $f$ at every candidate.** Not $f'$. Not the second derivative. Just $f$.
> 8. **Largest is the maximum value, smallest is the minimum value.** Report values and locations as two separate answers, and list every location if there is a tie.
> 9. **Classify only if asked.** Corner, cusp or vertical tangent comes from the two one-sided limits of $f'$, never from $f''$ and never from a picture.

The classify table for step 5:

| $f'$ near $c$ | left limit | right limit | name | still a candidate? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| finite, disagreeing | $L_1$ | $L_2$ | corner | yes |
| infinite, same sign | $\pm\infty$ | $\pm\infty$ | vertical tangent | yes |
| infinite, opposite signs | $\mp\infty$ | $\pm\infty$ | **cusp** | yes |
| finite, agreeing | $L$ | $L$ | $f'$ exists after all | only if $L=0$ |

Note the last row.
Q15.1 recorded that `MATH265_Review.md` calls $f'(0)$ undefined on a function where it is in fact
defined, so "the formula looks bad at $c$" is not the same claim as "$f'(c)$ does not exist".
Check the limits.

## 4. Q15.5 worked three ways

### Route A: the closed-interval method (the one to use)

**Step 1.** $f(x)=(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ is a composition of a polynomial with the continuous map
$t\mapsto \sqrt[3]{t^2}$, so $f$ is continuous on the closed bounded interval $[-2,3]$.
EVT: both extremes are attained.

**Step 2.** $x^2+2x<0$ on $(-2,0)$, and $2/3$ has an even numerator, so
$f(x)=\left|x^2+2x\right|^{2/3}\ge 0$ everywhere.

**Step 3.**
$$f'(x)=\frac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}},\qquad \operatorname{dom}(f')=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,0\}$$

**Step 4.** Numerator zero: $4(x+1)=0 \Rightarrow x=-1$.
Check the denominator is alive there: $\sqrt[3]{(-1)^2+2(-1)}=\sqrt[3]{-1}=-1\ne0$. Good.

**Step 5.** Denominator zero: $x(x+2)=0 \Rightarrow x=0$ and $x=-2$. Both lie in $[-2,3]$.

**Step 6.** Endpoints $x=-2$ and $x=3$. ($x=-2$ was already on the list; it stays once.)

**Step 7.** The candidate table.

| $x$ | why it is here | $f(x)$ exactly | $f(x)$ decimal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $-2$ | left endpoint **and** a cusp | $0$ | $0.0000000000$ |
| $-1$ | $f'=0$ | $\lvert -1\rvert^{2/3}=1$ | $1.0000000000$ |
| $0$ | interior **cusp**, $f'$ undefined | $0$ | $0.0000000000$ |
| $3$ | right endpoint | $15^{2/3}=\sqrt[3]{225}$ | $6.0822019956$ |

**Step 8.**
Absolute maximum $15^{2/3}\approx 6.0822019956$ at $x=3$.
Absolute minimum $0$ at $x=-2$ **and** $x=0$.
The value $1$ at $x=-1$ is a **local** maximum only.

### Route B: the shape, using the symmetry

$x^2+2x=(x+1)^2-1$, so $f(x)=\left|(x+1)^2-1\right|^{2/3}$ depends on $x$ only through $(x+1)^2$.
**$f$ is symmetric about the vertical line $x=-1$**, and the reflection $x\mapsto -2-x$ leaves it
unchanged.

That one observation gives the whole shape for free:

- $f$ falls from $f(-2)=0$? No: $f(-2)=0$ is a minimum, $f(-1)=1$ is a local max, $f(0)=0$ is a minimum again.
- The arch on $[-2,0]$ is a perfect mirror image about $x=-1$, peaking at $1$.
- Outside $[-2,0]$, $(x+1)^2-1$ grows without bound, so $f$ grows without bound in both directions.
- On $[-2,3]$ the right tail runs from $x=0$ out to $x=3$ and reaches $15^{2/3}$; the left tail is not in the interval at all.

So the maximum has to be at $x=3$ (nothing else gets that far from the centre) and the minimum has to
be $0$ at the two mirror-image points $x=-2$ and $x=0$.
No derivative required.

This is also the honest reason the two zeros tie: **they are each other's reflection.**
$-2$ and $0$ are $2\cdot(-1)-0$ and $2\cdot(-1)-(-2)$.

### Route C: monotonicity from a sign chart

$f'(x)=\dfrac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x(x+2)}}$.
Both factors change sign, so track them.

| interval | $x+1$ | $\sqrt[3]{x(x+2)}$ | $f'$ | $f$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| $(-2,-1)$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | increasing |
| $(-1,0)$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | decreasing |
| $(0,3)$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | increasing |

$f$ rises from $0$ to $1$, falls back to $0$, then rises to $15^{2/3}$.
Minimum $0$ twice, maximum at the right end.
Note the sign chart needs the cusp at $x=0$ as a **break point** whether or not you call it a
critical point, so a sign chart drawn correctly cannot miss it.

### Route comparison

| Route | Work | Risk | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A. closed-interval table** | one derivative, four evaluations | forgetting the denominator's zeros | **use this one under pressure** |
| B. symmetry about $x=-1$ | no derivative at all | only works because this base is a perfect square minus a constant | brilliant as a **check**, fragile as a method |
| C. sign chart | one derivative, three sign tests | more bookkeeping, and it gives local information you were not asked for | use when the question says "increasing/decreasing" |

**Use Route A.**
Then spend ten seconds on Route B as a check, because the symmetry instantly confirms that the two
minimisers tie and that $x=-1$ is only a local peak.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

This is what earns the method marks if a human ever reads it, and it takes about ninety seconds.

> $f(x)=(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ is continuous on the closed bounded interval $[-2,3]$, so by the Extreme
> Value Theorem $f$ attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on $[-2,3]$.
>
> $f'(x)=\dfrac{2}{3}(x^2+2x)^{-1/3}(2x+2)=\dfrac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}}$.
>
> $f'(x)=0$ when $x=-1$.
> $f'(x)$ **does not exist** when $x^2+2x=0$, that is at $x=0$ and $x=-2$, and $f$ is defined at both.
> All three are critical points, and both endpoints are $x=-2$ and $x=3$.
>
> $f(-2)=0$, $f(-1)=1$, $f(0)=0$, $f(3)=15^{2/3}$.
>
> Therefore the absolute maximum value is $15^{2/3}=\sqrt[3]{225}\approx 6.08$, attained at $x=3$,
> and the absolute minimum value is $0$, attained at $x=-2$ and at $x=0$.
>
> (At $x=0$, $f'\to-\infty$ from the left and $f'\to+\infty$ from the right, so the graph has a
> **cusp** there.)

The parenthetical last line is not needed for the value, but it is the sentence that shows you knew
why $x=0$ was on the list.

**Inference, not fact:** with 6 points on the item and no published scheme, a plausible split is
about 3 for part (a) and 3 for part (b), with part (a)'s marks sitting on the derivative, the
complete candidate list, and the two reported values.

### Family 2: an odd numerator gives a vertical tangent, not a cusp

**Trigger.** $g(x)^{m/n}$ with $m$ **odd** and $n$ odd, $m<n$.
**Tool.** One-sided limits with the **same** sign.
**Worked shape.** The base's sign survives, so $f$ passes through the point instead of bouncing.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at | species at the break |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $x^{1/3}$ | $[-8,27]$ | $-2.0000000000$ | $-8$ | $3.0000000000$ | $27$ | vertical tangent |
| $(x+1)^{1/3}$ | $[-9,7]$ | $-2.0000000000$ | $-9$ | $2.0000000000$ | $7$ | vertical tangent |
| $x^{5/3}-5x^{2/3}$ | $[-1,8]$ | $-6.0000000000$ | $-1$ | $12.0000000000$ | $8$ | vertical tangent at $0$, $f'=0$ at $2$ |

Row 1 is the cleanest contrast in the whole catalog: $x^{1/3}$ is **strictly increasing**, so despite
having an infinite slope at the origin its extremes sit at the endpoints and nowhere else.
An infinite slope is not an extremum.
Row 3 carries **both** species at once and is the best single drill in this family.

### Family 3: an absolute value gives a corner

**Trigger.** $\lvert g(x)\rvert$ with $g$ changing sign inside the interval.
**Tool.** One-sided limits, **finite** and disagreeing.
**Worked shape.** Every zero of $g$ where $g'\ne0$ is a corner and therefore a critical point.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $\lvert x\rvert$ | $[-3,5]$ | $0$ | $0$ | $5.0000000000$ | $5$ |
| $\lvert x^2-4\rvert$ | $[-3,3]$ | $0$ | $-2,\;2$ | $5.0000000000$ | $-3,\;3$ |
| $4-\lvert x-1\rvert$ | $[-2,6]$ | $-1.0000000000$ | $6$ | $4.0000000000$ | $1$ |

Row 2 is the corner analogue of Q15.5: two corners tie for the minimum and two endpoints tie for the
maximum, all four forced by evenness.
Row 3 puts the corner at the **maximum**, which is the arrangement most students never rehearse.
On all three rows the centred difference quotient at the corner is exactly $0$ (rows 1 and 2) or the
average of the two slopes (asymmetric corners), which is why section 2.6 says to test one-sided.

### Family 4: the cusp is the MAXIMUM

**Trigger.** A minus sign in front of a $2/3$ power, or a constant minus it.
**Tool.** Identical procedure; only the reading of the table flips.
**Worked shape.** Reflecting $f$ in a horizontal line swaps every maximiser with every minimiser.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $4-\lvert x\rvert^{2/3}$ | $[-8,1]$ | $0$ | $-8$ | $4.0000000000$ | $0$ |
| $6-(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-2,3]$ | $-0.0822019956$ | $3$ | $6.0000000000$ | $-2,\;0$ |
| $-\lvert x-2\rvert^{2/3}$ | $[0,5]$ | $-2.0800838231$ | $5$ | $0$ | $2$ |

Row 2 is Q15.5 turned upside down and it is the single best variation to rehearse, because every
number in it comes from a number you already know: $6-0=6$ at the two cusps and $6-15^{2/3}=-0.0822$
at $x=3$.
A student who memorised "the cusp is the minimum" gets row 2 exactly backwards.

### Family 5: $f'$ undefined from a factor, not from the whole function

**Trigger.** A product like $x^{2/3}(x-5)$, where only one factor misbehaves.
**Tool.** Differentiate the product, then find where the combined $f'$ has a vanishing denominator.
**Worked shape.** $\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^{2/3}(x-5)\right]=\frac{5x-10}{3x^{1/3}}$, so $x=2$ is a zero
and $x=0$ is a non-existence point, and they are **different points**.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $x^{2/3}(x-5)$ | $[-1,8]$ | $-6.0000000000$ | $-1$ | $12.0000000000$ | $8$ |
| $x\lvert x-1\rvert^{1/3}$ | $[-1,4]$ | $-1.2599210499$ | $-1$ | $5.7689982812$ | $4$ |
| $(x^2)^{1/3}(x+3)$ | $[-4,2]$ | $-2.5198420998$ | $-4$ | $7.9370052598$ | $2$ |

This family is the one most likely to appear as a **different** exam question, because it hides the
fractional power inside a product where the quotient rule is not obviously involved.
On row 1 the cusp at $x=0$ is a local **maximum** of a function whose absolute maximum is at the far
endpoint, so the cusp is on the list and yet is neither extreme.
Being a critical point does not make you an answer.

### Family 6: the cusp lies OUTSIDE the interval

**Trigger.** The interval avoids the base's zeros entirely.
**Tool.** The ordinary two-endpoint comparison. Nothing else.
**Worked shape.** With no critical point inside, $f$ is monotone and the extremes are the two ends.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[1,4]$ | $2.0800838231$ | $1$ | $8.3203352922$ | $4$ |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-5,-3]$ | $2.0800838231$ | $-3$ | $6.0822019956$ | $-5$ |

Row 2 is the useful one: the same function on a left-hand interval is **decreasing**, so the larger
value sits at the **left** end.
"Maximum at the right endpoint" is not a rule, it is what happened once.
And a cusp outside $[a,b]$ must **not** be listed as a candidate, which is the mirror error to
forgetting one inside.

### Family 7: the cusp is AT an endpoint

**Trigger.** An endpoint coincides with a zero of the base, as $x=-2$ does here.
**Tool.** Nothing extra. Endpoints are candidates regardless.
**Worked shape.** Only one branch of the cusp is in the domain, so only one one-sided limit exists.

| Instance | Interval | min | at | max | at |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-2,-0.5]$ | $0$ | $-2$ | $1.0000000000$ | $-1$ |
| $\lvert x\rvert^{2/3}$ | $[0,8]$ | $0$ | $0$ | $4.0000000000$ | $8$ |

**An endpoint cusp costs you nothing**, which is exactly why Q15.5's does not punish anybody.
See section 9.
Row 1 also shows the local max at $x=-1$ becoming the **absolute** max once the right tail is cut off.

### Family 8: the discriminator, where omitting the cusp finally SHOWS

**Trigger.** The interval contains the interior cusp but not the endpoint that shadows it.
**Tool.** The same table. The difference is entirely in the interval.
**Worked shape.** Move the left endpoint from $-2$ to $-1.5$ and the second zero disappears.

| Instance | Interval | true min | at | min if you drop the cusp | wrong by |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-2,3]$ | $0$ | $-2,\;0$ | $0$ | **nothing (it hides)** |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-1.5,3]$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0.8254818122$ | the whole minimum |
| $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ | $[-1.2,3]$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0.9731523193$ | the whole minimum |

This is the family to rehearse, because it proves the hiding on the exam belongs to the **interval**
and not to the method.
One shifted endpoint, and the same mistake costs the mark.

### Family 9: the same function, the interval moves the answer

**Trigger.** "on $[a,b]$" changes and nothing else does.
**Tool.** The same four-line table, re-run.
**Worked shape.** Only candidates inside the new interval survive; the endpoints are the new ends.

### Family 10: the integral bound this question feeds

**Trigger.** "Use the properties of the definite integral to bound $\int_a^b f$."
**Tool.** $m(b-a)\le \int_a^b f \le M(b-a)$, with $m$ and $M$ the answers to part (a).
**Worked shape.** Multiply both extreme values by the interval's **length**.

| Interval | $m$ | $M$ | length | bound | true integral |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| $[-2,3]$ | $0$ | $6.0822019956$ | $5$ | $[0,\;30.4110099779]$ | $10.6736300365$ |
| $[-2,0]$ | $0$ | $1.0000000000$ | $2$ | $[0,\;2.0000000000]$ | $1.4783483196$ |
| $[0,3]$ | $0$ | $6.0822019956$ | $3$ | $[0,\;18.2466059867]$ | $9.1952817169$ |

**Forgetting the $(b-a)$ factor gives $[0,\;6.0822]$, which excludes the true value $10.6736$.**
That is Q17.3's own trap and it is the one place on item 7 where a slip is guaranteed to show.
Rows 2 and 3 also demonstrate additivity: their integrals sum to row 1's to twelve digits.
The integral column needed a substitution to compute: $x = s^3$ turns the cusp's $s^2$ factor into

to about seven decimals and no further.

Note the bound here is **one-sided in practice**, because $m=0$ makes the lower end trivially true.
All the information is in the upper bound, and it is loose: the true integral is only $35.1$ percent
of it.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks.

1. **Solving only $f'=0$ and stopping.**
   You get $x=-1$, a candidate list of $\{-2,-1,3\}$, and the answer $0$ and $15^{2/3}$.
   Which is **right**.
   This is the trap the question exists to punish and on this interval it does not punish it, so
   nothing tells you that you did it wrong.
   Fix the habit, not the answer: write the derivative's domain down every time.

2. **Reading $(-1)^{2/3}$ as $-1$.**
   That reports $f(-1)=-1$ and makes $-1$ the minimum, which contradicts $f\ge 0$.
   This one **does** show here, unlike on Q15.1(a) where the same misread gave $+3$, comfortably
   inside $[-16,\,23.28]$, and hid completely.

3. **Typing `15^2/3`.**
   Möbius returns $75$.
   The correct string is `15^(2/3)` or `225^(1/3)`.

4. **Reporting the location instead of the value.**
   $3$ instead of $6.0822$.
   Half the size, so a magnitude check catches it here, but the habit is what matters: value and
   location are two answers.

5. **Listing only one minimiser.**
   The minimum is attained **twice**.
   If the box asks where, $-2$ and $0$ both belong in it.

6. **Trying to classify $x=0$ with $f''$.**
   $f''(x)=\dfrac{4\left((x+1)^2-3\right)}{9\left(x^2+2x\right)^{4/3}}$ blows up at $x=0$ like
   $x^{-4/3}$, so the second-derivative test is silent exactly where you wanted it.
   It **does** work at $x=-1$: $f''(-1)=-\tfrac43<0$, confirming the local maximum.
   Use it there and nowhere else.

7. **Using a centred difference quotient to check for a critical point.**
   It reads $5.29\times10^{-5}$ at the cusp while the real one-sided slopes are past $158$.
   See section 2.6.

8. **Adding the inflection points as candidates.**
   $f''=0$ at $x=-1\pm\sqrt3$, and $-1+\sqrt3\approx 0.7321$ is inside the interval.
   It is harmless here (its value sits strictly between $0$ and $6.0822$) but it is not a critical
   point and it does not belong in the table.

9. **Dropping an endpoint.**
   Dropping $x=3$ costs the entire maximum: you report $1$ instead of $6.0822$.
   Dropping $x=-2$ costs nothing, because $x=0$ covers it.
   Two endpoints, two opposite outcomes, which is the argument for writing both down as a habit
   rather than as a judgement.

10. **Forgetting the $(b-a)$ factor on part (b).**
    Gives $[0,\,6.0822]$, which excludes the truth. See Family 10.

### The census, measured

Fifteen mutilations of the candidate list were scanned, with nothing excluded as degenerate.
Each was scored on whether the reported **pair of values** changes.

**Result: 10 HIDE, 5 SHOW.**

| Mutilation | reported min | reported max | verdict |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| drop the interior cusp $x=0$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| drop both cusps as a class | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| drop the left endpoint $x=-2$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| drop the right endpoint $x=3$ | $0$ | $1$ | SHOWS |
| drop the $f'=0$ point $x=-1$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| drop both endpoints | $0$ | $1$ | SHOWS |
| keep only $x=-1$ | $1$ | $1$ | SHOWS |
| add the inflection point | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| add a spurious cusp at $x=2$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| solve the base's zeros as $0$ and $+2$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| sign error giving $x=+1$ for $x=-1$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| read the exponent as $3/2$, so no cusp | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| work on $[-2,0]$ | $0$ | $1$ | SHOWS |
| work on $[0,3]$ | $0$ | $6.0822$ | **HIDES** |
| second-derivative test only | $1$ | $1$ | SHOWS |

**The headline: the defining mistake this question exists to punish HIDES, and it hides structurally.**

The minimum is $0$ and it is attained at $x=-2$ **and** $x=0$.
$x=-2$ is the left **endpoint**, so it is on every candidate list ever written, including one built
by somebody who has never heard of a cusp.
Its twin $x=0$ is therefore covered, and omitting the interior cusp reproduces both reported values
exactly.

The omission is invisible in the **values** and visible only in the **points**: the true argmin list
has two entries and the mutilated one has a single entry, $x=-2$.
So the only box on the exam that can catch this error is a box asking *where* the minimum occurs.

The hiding belongs to the **interval**, not to the method.
Shift the left endpoint to $-1.5$ and dropping the cusp reports $0.8254818122$ instead of $0$.
That is Family 8, and it is the reason to fix the habit rather than trust this instance.

Note also that this is the **third** arrangement of a tie in three closed-interval questions:

| Question | how the minimum ties | endpoint omission |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Q15.1(b) | no tie, extremes at different endpoints | **both** omissions fatal |
| Q15.3 | tie at **both endpoints** ($f(-1)=f(1)=\tfrac12$) | **both** omissions harmless |
| Q15.5 | tie at **one endpoint and one interior cusp** | left harmless, right fatal |

Three questions, three different answers to "does omitting an endpoint show".
Write both endpoints down every time.

## 11. Closing the category: one procedure for all five questions

Category 15 is five questions and, before the sharing is accounted for, 41 points.
Two of those five sit inside items that also contain an integral question.

| Q | Paper and item | Points | Shape | What it alone teaches |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **15.1** | F1 item 5 | 11 | closed interval, two parts | *run* the candidate table; a fractional power with an odd numerator |
| **15.2** | F1 item 6 | 6 | optimization, open domain | build the objective; there is no endpoint to fall back on |
| **15.3** | F2 item 3a | 8, **shared with Q17.2** | closed interval | *name* the theorem; what EVT guarantees and what it does not locate |
| **15.4** | F2 item 5 | 10 | optimization, open domain, costs | a constrained model with a $Aw^2+B/w$ objective |
| **15.5** | F2 item 7a | 6, **shared with Q17.3** | closed interval | the **cusp**: a critical point $f'=0$ can never find |

**The sharing is real and it was verified from the paper.**
F2 items 3 and 7 each carry two sub-parts under a single point tag, and in both cases part (b) is an
integral bound on the *same function over the same interval* as part (a).
Exactly two of F2's twelve items are built this way, and between them they are 14 of the paper's 81 points.
So "41 points" over-counts category 15: $8$ and $6$ of those points are shared with category 17.
**Point split within an item is inference** and no scheme exists to confirm it, but an even split
would put Q15.3 and Q15.5 at about 4 and 3 respectively.

**Study categories 15 and 17 together on F2.**
Part (a) hands part (b) its $m$ and $M$, and part (b) is a free check on part (a).

### The one procedure

```
IS THE DOMAIN CLOSED AND BOUNDED?
 |
 +-- YES -> CLOSED-INTERVAL METHOD                        (Q15.1, Q15.3, Q15.5)
 |          1. continuity -> EVT guarantees both extremes exist
 |          2. f'  ->  write its DOMAIN beside it
 |          3. candidates = {f'=0}  U  {f' undefined}  U  {a, b}
 |          4. evaluate f at each; biggest and smallest win
 |          5. report VALUE and LOCATION separately; list every tie
 |
 +-- NO  -> OPTIMIZATION ON AN OPEN DOMAIN                (Q15.2, Q15.4)
            1. name the variable and write the CONSTRAINT
            2. reduce to ONE variable; state the domain (usually (0, inf))
            3. f' = 0 -> the single interior critical point
            4. prove it is a MINIMUM without endpoints:
               f'' > 0 there, or f -> +inf at both ends of the domain
            5. answer the question actually asked (dimensions? cost? perimeter?)
```

The two halves differ in exactly one place: **step 4**.
On a closed interval the endpoints do the work of proving the extreme is global.
On an open domain there are no endpoints, so you have to supply that proof yourself.

### The one-page decision tree for this question

```
"absolute extreme values ... on [a,b]"
        |
        v
  f continuous on [a,b]?  ---- no ----> EVT does not apply; check by hand
        | yes
        v
  differentiate f
        |
        v
  DOES f' HAVE A DENOMINATOR, A FRACTIONAL POWER < 1, AN |x|, OR A SEAM?
        |                                   |
       no                                  yes
        |                                   |
        v                                   v
  candidates = {f'=0} U {a,b}      solve DENOMINATOR = 0 too
        |                          keep the roots inside [a,b]
        |                          candidates = {f'=0} U {f' undef} U {a,b}
        +-------------------+---------------+
                            v
              evaluate f at EVERY candidate
                            v
              biggest = MAX value, smallest = MIN value
                            v
              LIST every location that ties
                            v

              finite + disagree  -> corner
              infinite + same    -> vertical tangent
              infinite + opposite-> CUSP

              preview the Mobius string:  15^(2/3)  NOT  15^2/3
```

Method notes worth keeping:

- The critical-point census used **four independent arms**: a naive strict sign-change scan, a
  step-over scan that skips exact zeros, a golden-section refinement of local minima of $\lvert f'\rvert$,
  and a separate bisection for the zeros of the *base* to find the non-existence points.
- Every critical point here is at a **rational** fraction of the interval ($0$, $1/5$, $2/5$), so the
  naive scan's answer depends on the grid: it returns $0$ on any grid whose subinterval count is
  divisible by $5$ and $2$ on any grid that is not. The step-over scan returns $2$ at every
  resolution tested.
- The sign-change census counts **zeros and cusps together** and cannot tell them apart, which is why
  the fourth arm exists.
- The dense sweep's tolerance is **derived from the function's own variation across one grid step**
  at each candidate, because $f$ is not Lipschitz at a cusp: the sweep's value error there is
  $O(\text{step}^{2/3})$ and a Lipschitz-derived tolerance produces false failures.
- Every decimal in sections 8 and 10 is computed, printed and then digit-locked by the harness.
  None was typed.
- **A digit-lock inherits the precision of the method it locks.** The part-(b) integrals were
  first computed with plain Simpson and locked to ten decimals; Simpson's order on this integrand
  is a measured $5/3$, not $4$, because the integrand has a cusp, so the tenth decimal was wrong
  while two independent resolutions agreed with each other. The substitution $x=s^3$ removes the
  singularity and the corrected values are in harness block `q155c`. Self-consistency is not
  accuracy.
