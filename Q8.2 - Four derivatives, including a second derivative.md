Q8.2's own contribution is **the second derivative**, and it is three specific ideas, not one:

1. **The simplification policy inverts.**
   Q8.1's policy is that simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk, so you stop when the derivative is written.
   That is right when $f'$ is the *answer*.
   In part (a) $f'$ is an *input*, and the reward stops being zero, because every unsimplified factor you carry into the second differentiation multiplies into a bigger mess.
   Section 5 states the inverted policy and the one test that tells you which regime you are in.
2. **The rule choice can change between the first and the second differentiation.**
   $\cot(2x)$ is a chain rule with a named-function outer.
   Its derivative $-2\csc^2(2x)$ is a chain rule with a **power** outer.
   Nothing in Q8.1 prepares you for the rule name changing under your hands, and it is why people who get part (a)'s $f'$ right still get $f''$ wrong.
   Family 2 in section 8 is the whole catalog of how the rule changes, including the two cases where it becomes a **product** rule.
3. **A second derivative needs a different numerical check, and the check that works for $f'$ actively misleads you.**
   Q8.1's rule was that a smaller $h$ is safer.
   For a second difference you divide by $h^2$ instead of $h$, so the roundoff floor is a hundred times worse, and $h = 10^{-5}$ on a real calculator gives you **zero** correct figures.
   Section 6 gives the measured optimum and the table that produced it.

Where Q8.2's four parts sit in Q8.1's family map: (a) is Family 10 there and Family 2 here, (b) is Family 4 there and Family 7 here, (c) is Family 8 in both, (d) is Family 9 in both.
The renumbering is not carelessness.
Q8.1 organised its families around *which rule you pick*; this document organises them around *what happens on the second pass*, because that is the axis Q8.2 tests.

```
   BETWEEN  is f' as short as it will get?  If not, shorten it NOW.
            This is the opposite of the rule for a first derivative.

   PASS 2   ask the last-operation question AGAIN, about f'.

   CHECK    [f(x+h) - 2 f(x) + f(x-h)] / h^2   with h = 0.001, NOT smaller.

GIVEN VALUES (d):  differentiate with the LETTERS, substitute LAST, radians.
```

## 2. The four things you must have cold

Q8.1 section 2 has the five rules and the six trig derivatives, and this document assumes them.
These four are the additions that Q8.2 specifically needs.

### 2.1 The two Pythagorean identities, in the direction that helps

$$\csc^2 u = 1 + \cot^2 u, \qquad \sec^2 u = 1 + \tan^2 u$$

You need these read **left to right**, which is the unusual direction.
The point is that $\csc^2(2x)$, which looks like a squared reciprocal you cannot do anything with, is a constant plus $\cot^2(2x)$, which is a power of something whose derivative you know.
That is a second, completely independent route to part (a) and it is Family 4 in section 8.

Both were verified as identities over the sample points at $10^{-16}$ relative, not assumed.

### 2.2 Which of $\csc^2 u$ and $\csc\left(u^2\right)$ you are looking at

This is the single most consequential piece of notation in the question.

| written | means | last operation | rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\csc^2(2x)$ | $\left[\csc(2x)\right]^2$ | **squaring** | chain, **power** outer |
| $\csc\left((2x)^2\right)$ | $\csc\left(4x^2\right)$ | applying $\csc$ | chain, **named** outer |
| $\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | as written | applying $\sec$ | chain, **named** outer |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $(\sec x)^2$ | **squaring** | chain, **power** outer |

$\csc^2(2x)$ is a square, and that single fact is what turns pass 2 of part (a) into a power-rule problem.
Read the superscript's **position**: on the function name it is an exponent applied last, and inside the bracket it is part of the argument.

### 2.3 The derivative of a squared trig function, which you will use twice

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[T(u)\right]^2 = 2\,T(u)\cdot T'(u)\cdot u'$$

Three factors, and the third is the one that goes missing.
For part (a) with $T = \csc$, $u = 2x$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\csc^2(2x) = 2\csc(2x)\cdot\left(-\csc(2x)\cot(2x)\right)\cdot 2 = -4\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$$

Note that the two $\csc$ factors multiply into $\csc^2$, which is why the answer looks like the thing you started with.
That coincidence is what makes part (a) feel circular, and it is worth expecting so it does not read as a mistake.

### 2.4 What "differentiate with letters, substitute last" means when you are given numbers

Part (d) gives you $f(1) = 3$ and $g(1) = 6$ and no formulas for $f$ and $g$ themselves.
The temptation is to write $\frac{f(1)}{g(1)} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac12$ and differentiate that, which gives $0$.
The reason that is wrong is that $\frac12$ is the **value** of the quotient at one point and not the quotient.
So you write the quotient rule with letters:

$$\left.\left(\frac fg\right)'\right|_{x=1} = \frac{f'(1)g(1) - f(1)g'(1)}{\left[g(1)\right]^2}$$

and only then put the four numbers in.
The four numbers you need are $f'(1)$, $g(1)$, $f(1)$ and $g'(1)$, and exactly one of them, $g'(1)$, has to be computed rather than read off.

## 3. The universal procedure for a second derivative

Q8.1's procedure is unchanged for a first derivative.
This is the wrapper that turns it into a second-derivative procedure, and it is three steps longer.

> **Step A - Differentiate once, by Q8.1's procedure.**
> Last-operation test, write the rule with blanks, recurse, assemble.
> Nothing new.
>
> **Step B - Stop and look at $f'$. This is the step that does not exist in Q8.1.**
> Ask one question: *is this the shortest true expression for $f'$?*
> If a factor cancels, if two terms combine, if a bracket can be collapsed, do it **now**, before you differentiate again.
> Section 5 says why the risk calculation flips here.
>
> **Step C - Run the last-operation test AGAIN, on $f'$, from scratch.**
> Do not assume the rule is the same one you just used.
> Look at $f'$ as if you had never seen $f$.
>
> | $f'$ looks like | last operation | pass-2 rule | example |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | a polynomial | $+$ and $-$ | sum and power | Q8.1a |
> | $c\cdot\left[T(u)\right]^2$ | squaring | **chain, power outer** | **Q8.2a** |
> | $T(u)\cdot S(u)$ | multiplying | **product** | $\sec x$, $\csc x$ |
> | $\dfrac{\text{poly}}{(\ )^n}$ | dividing | **quotient** | Q8.1b |
> | $c\cdot T(kx)$ | applying $T$ | chain, named outer, same as pass 1 | $\sin(2x)$ |
>
> **Step D - Differentiate $f'$.**
> Same procedure, same recursion.
>
> **Step E - Now apply Q8.1's policy again.**
> $f''$ **is** the answer, so simplify only if a factor obviously cancels.
> For part (a) the two $\csc$ factors have already combined by themselves, and there is nothing left to do.
>
> **Step F - Check with a SECOND difference, not a first one.**
> Section 6, and read the $h$ advice there rather than reusing Q8.1's.

**The tree for part (a), which is the only part that needs one.**

```
             d^2/dx^2  cot(2x)
                     |
        PASS 1: last op is a NAMED FUNCTION
                     |
              >>> CHAIN RULE <<<
              -csc^2(2x) * 2  =  -2 csc^2(2x)
                     |
        STOP.  Is f' in its shortest form?   YES
                     |
        PASS 2: last op is now a POWER, ( )^2
                     |
              >>> CHAIN RULE, power outer <<<
              -2 * 2 csc(2x) * d/dx csc(2x)
                     |
              = 8 csc^2(2x) cot(2x)
```

The one thing to take from that picture is that **the two boxes announce different rules**.
Pass 1 says "named outer" and pass 2 says "power outer", and the only reason to know that is that you asked the question twice instead of assuming.

## 4. Q8.2 worked, part by part

### Part (a) - $\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(2x)$

This is the part worth working four ways, because the four ways differ in cost by a factor of four.

#### Route A - chain rule, then the squared-trig rule (use this one)

Pass 1.
The last operation is $\cot$ applied to $2x$, so it is a chain rule with a named outer.
$\frac{d}{dx}\cot u = -\csc^2 u$, and $u' = 2$:

$$f' = -\csc^2(2x)\cdot 2 = -2\csc^2(2x)$$

Step B.
Is that the shortest form?
Yes.
There is one term, one coefficient, no brackets to collapse.

Pass 2.
The last operation in $-2\csc^2(2x)$ is the **squaring**, so this is a chain rule with a power outer, and section 2.3 gives it directly:

$$f'' = -2\cdot\left[2\csc(2x)\cdot\left(-\csc(2x)\cot(2x)\right)\cdot 2\right] = -2\cdot\left(-4\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)\right)$$

$$\boxed{f'' = 8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)}$$

**Where the 8 comes from is worth naming, because the wrong coefficient is the commonest wrong answer here.**
It is $2 \times 2 \times 2$: one factor of 2 from the power rule on the square, one from the chain rule on the inner $2x$ during pass 2, and one from the coefficient $-2$ that pass 1 produced.
Miss any one of them and you write 4 instead of 8.
The sign is $(-2)\times(-1) = +$, where the $-2$ is pass 1's coefficient and the $-1$ is the minus in $\frac{d}{dx}\csc = -\csc\cot$.
Two minus signs, so the answer is positive, which surprises people who expect a second derivative of a decreasing function to stay negative.

#### Route B - the Pythagorean rewrite

Rewrite $f'$ before differentiating it, using $\csc^2 u = 1 + \cot^2 u$:

$$f' = -2\csc^2(2x) = -2\left(1 + \cot^2(2x)\right) = -2 - 2\cot^2(2x)$$

Now the constant differentiates away and the second term is a power of $\cot$:

$$f'' = -2\cdot 2\cot(2x)\cdot\left(-\csc^2(2x)\right)\cdot 2 = 8\cot(2x)\csc^2(2x)$$

Same answer, same number of factors of 2, and the advantage is that $\frac{d}{dx}\cot = -\csc^2$ is a fact you used thirty seconds ago and $\frac{d}{dx}\csc = -\csc\cot$ is a different fact you have to recall.
**Route B needs one trig derivative and Route A needs two.**
It is the better route if $\csc$ is the derivative you are least sure of, which for most people it is.

#### Route C - convert to sines and cosines and use the quotient rule twice

$\cot(2x) = \dfrac{\cos 2x}{\sin 2x}$, so pass 1 is a quotient rule:

$$f' = \frac{-2\sin 2x\cdot\sin 2x - \cos 2x\cdot 2\cos 2x}{\sin^2 2x} = \frac{-2\left(\sin^2 2x + \cos^2 2x\right)}{\sin^2 2x} = \frac{-2}{\sin^2 2x}$$

which is $-2\csc^2(2x)$ again, so the route is correct.
Pass 2 is then a second quotient rule on $\dfrac{-2}{\sin^2 2x}$, or a power rule on $-2\left(\sin 2x\right)^{-2}$:

$$f'' = -2\cdot(-2)\left(\sin 2x\right)^{-3}\cdot 2\cos 2x = \frac{8\cos 2x}{\sin^3 2x}$$

That is the same function as $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$, verified as an identity over 14 sample points at $10^{-16}$ relative.
**It is also a perfectly acceptable final answer**, and if you cannot remember whether $\csc$ or $\sec$ is the reciprocal of sine, writing the answer in sines and cosines is strictly safer.

#### Route D - a memorised general formula

$$\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(kx) = 2k^2\csc^2(kx)\cot(kx)$$

For $k=2$ that is $2\cdot 4 = 8$, and for $k=3$ it is $18$.
This was verified for $k = 1$ through $5$, each at its own safe evaluation point.
It is worth knowing as a **check** on the coefficient rather than as the method, because a formula you cannot re-derive is a formula you will misremember under pressure.

#### The route comparison

| route | trig derivatives needed | lines of algebra | risk | verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A - chain then squared-trig** | $\cot' $ and $\csc'$ | 3 | the coefficient 8 | the standard; write this |
| **B - Pythagorean rewrite** | $\cot'$ only | 3 | remembering the identity's direction | **fastest and safest if $\csc'$ is shaky** |
| C - sines and cosines | none | 7 | a long quotient rule twice | correct, slow, and it does prove the answer |
| D - memorised $2k^2$ formula | none | 1 | misremembering $2k^2$ as $k^2$ | a check, not a method |

**Under exam pressure, use Route A and check the coefficient against Route D's $2k^2$.**
That combination takes ninety seconds and it is self-verifying: if $2k^2 = 8$ and your chain of 2s also gave 8, two independent countings agree.
Route B is the one to switch to if you freeze on $\frac{d}{dx}\csc$.

### Part (b) - $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)$

One chain rule with a named outer, which is Q8.1's Family 4 with a quadratic inner instead of a linear one.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sec u = \sec u\tan u, \qquad u = x^2-3x, \qquad u' = 2x-3$$

$$f' = (2x-3)\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)\tan\left(x^2-3x\right)$$

Stop there.
There is nothing to simplify and the printed permission covers you.
The equivalent form $\dfrac{(2x-3)\sin\left(x^2-3x\right)}{\cos^2\left(x^2-3x\right)}$ is also correct and was verified as an identity, but it is longer, so writing it is pure risk.

Two structural facts worth carrying, both verified: $f'(1.5) = 0$ because the inner derivative $2x-3$ vanishes at the vertex of the parabola, and $f'(0) = 0$ because $\tan(0) = 0$.
If your answer does not vanish at $x = 1.5$, you have lost the inner derivative.

### Part (c) - $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{x^2-\cos 3x}{x\sin 2x}$

The last operation is a division, so this is the quotient rule.
What makes it a four-point part rather than a two-point one is that **both** halves need a rule of their own, and they need *different* rules.

| piece | what it is | rule for its derivative | result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $u = x^2 - \cos 3x$ | a difference | sum rule, then chain on $\cos 3x$ | $u' = 2x + 3\sin 3x$ |
| $v = x\sin 2x$ | a **product** | product rule, with chain on $\sin 2x$ | $v' = \sin 2x + 2x\cos 2x$ |

**The sign in $u'$ is the first place marks go.**
$\frac{d}{dx}\left(-\cos 3x\right) = -\left(-\sin 3x\right)\cdot 3 = +3\sin 3x$.
Two minus signs, so the term is positive, and writing $-3\sin 3x$ is the plausible wrong answer.

**The product rule in $v'$ is the second place marks go.**
Q8.1 noted that the product rule is absent from Q8.1 itself, and here it is, buried one level down inside a quotient.
Taking $v' = 2x\cos 2x$, which is what you get if you differentiate only the trig factor, was seeded as a control and confirmed to be caught.

Assembling, with $u'v - uv'$ in that order:

$$f' = \frac{\left(2x+3\sin 3x\right)\left(x\sin 2x\right) - \left(x^2-\cos 3x\right)\left(\sin 2x + 2x\cos 2x\right)}{\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2}$$

That is the final answer.
Do not expand $\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2$ into $x^2\sin^2 2x$; the two are the same function, verified, and the first is shorter.
Do not distribute the numerator either, because it produces six terms and buys nothing.

### Part (d) - $\left.\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{f}{g}\right|_{x=1}$

Write the rule with letters first.

$$\left.\left(\frac fg\right)'\right|_{x=1} = \frac{f'(1)\,g(1) - f(1)\,g'(1)}{\left[g(1)\right]^2}$$

Now collect the four numbers.

| needed | given as | value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(1)$ | given directly | $3$ |
| $g(1)$ | given directly | $6$ |
| $f'(1)$ | $f'(x) = \sqrt x$, so $\sqrt 1$ | $1$ |
| $g'(1)$ | $g'(x) = 3\sin(\pi x/3)$, so $3\sin\frac\pi3$ | $3\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2} \approx 2.598076$ |

$$= \frac{1\cdot 6 - 3\cdot\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}}{36} = \frac{6 - \frac{9\sqrt3}{2}}{36} = \frac{12 - 9\sqrt3}{72} = \boxed{\frac{4-3\sqrt3}{24}} \approx -0.0498397$$

All three intermediate forms were verified to be the same number, so any of them is a correct stopping point.
$\frac{4-3\sqrt3}{24}$ is the tidiest and it is what to write.

**Both traps in this part are silent, meaning they produce a plausible number rather than an error.**
A calculator in degrees evaluates $\sin(\pi/3)$ as $\sin(1.047^\circ) = 0.018277$ and gives $+0.162098$, which is the wrong sign and the wrong magnitude and looks perfectly reasonable on the page.
Substituting first gives exactly $0$, which also looks reasonable.
Neither is caught by re-reading your work, and both were seeded as controls and confirmed to be caught by the check in section 6.

**An end-to-end confirmation that this part's answer is right, independent of the quotient-rule formula.**
Concrete functions matching the given data exist:

$$F(x) = \tfrac23 x^{3/2} + \tfrac73, \qquad G(x) = -\tfrac9\pi\cos\frac{\pi x}{3} + 6 + \frac{9}{2\pi}$$

$F(1) = 3$ and $F'(x) = \sqrt x$; $G(1) = 6$ and $G'(x) = 3\sin\frac{\pi x}{3}$.
Numerically differentiating the actual quotient $F/G$ at $x = 1$ reproduces $-0.049839684279$, agreeing with $\frac{4-3\sqrt3}{24}$ to better than $10^{-9}$.
That check never touches the quotient rule, so it confirms the *answer* and not just the arithmetic.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up, and the inverted simplification policy

### The policy, which is Q8.1's policy with one clause added

Q8.1 states it: simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk, so the default is to stop the moment the derivative is written.
That clause is about $f'$ **as an answer**.
Q8.2 adds the case where it is not one.

> **When $f'$ is an intermediate result, simplifying it has real marginal reward, because every factor you carry forward gets differentiated too.**
> So: tidy $f'$ before pass 2, and leave $f''$ alone once you have it.

The test for which regime you are in is one question: **is there another differentiation coming?**
If yes, tidy.
If no, stop.

| case | tidy it? | why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f'$ in parts (b), (c), (d) | **no** | it is the answer; Q8.1's policy applies unchanged |
| $f'$ in part (a), before pass 2 | **yes** | it is an input; a longer $f'$ means a longer $f''$ |
| $f''$ in part (a), after pass 2 | **no** | it is the answer again |
| a quotient's $f'$ before a second differentiation | **yes, and this is the expensive case** | see below |

**The row that costs whole minutes is the quotient one, and Q8.1(b) is the cleanest illustration.**
Its raw quotient-rule output is $\dfrac{2(x+3)^2 - 2(2x-16)(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$ and its tidy form is $\dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$, verified identical.
Differentiating the tidy form is one quotient rule with a linear numerator.
Differentiating the raw form is one quotient rule with a quadratic numerator over a quartic denominator, and then a cancellation of $(x+3)^5$ against $(x+3)^8$ at the end.
Same answer, four times the writing, four times the places to slip.

Part (a) is the easy case of this: $f' = -2\csc^2(2x)$ is already as short as it gets, so step B costs you three seconds and returns nothing.
**That is the normal outcome, and it is why the step gets skipped and why skipping it is expensive on the questions where it matters.**

### The short form, which is what to write if time is tight

> a. $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(2x) = -2\csc^2(2x)$, and differentiating again by the power chain rule, $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(2x) = 8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$.
>
> b. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dx}\sec\left(x^2-3x\right) = (2x-3)\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)\tan\left(x^2-3x\right)$.
>
> c. By the quotient rule with $u = x^2-\cos 3x$ and $v = x\sin 2x$, so $u' = 2x+3\sin 3x$ and $v' = \sin 2x + 2x\cos 2x$:
> $\dfrac{\left(2x+3\sin 3x\right)\left(x\sin 2x\right) - \left(x^2-\cos 3x\right)\left(\sin 2x+2x\cos 2x\right)}{\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2}$.
>
> d. $\dfrac{f'(1)g(1) - f(1)g'(1)}{g(1)^2} = \dfrac{6 - 3\cdot\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}}{36} = \dfrac{4-3\sqrt3}{24}$.

### The full-credit form, which adds the clauses that show the method

The question says **"Show your work"**, which Q8.1's did not.
That raises the bar: a bare correct answer risks method marks, and the clauses below are what earn them.

> **a.** The outermost operation is $\cot$ applied to $2x$, so the first differentiation is the **chain rule** with $\frac{d}{du}\cot u = -\csc^2 u$ and $u = 2x$, $u' = 2$:
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\cot(2x) = -2\csc^2(2x).$$
> This is already in lowest terms, so I differentiate it as it stands.
> Its outermost operation is now a **square**, so the second differentiation is the chain rule in its generalized-power form, $\frac{d}{dx}\left[\csc u\right]^2 = 2\csc u\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\csc u$, with $\frac{d}{du}\csc u = -\csc u\cot u$:
> $$\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(2x) = -2\cdot 2\csc(2x)\cdot\left(-\csc(2x)\cot(2x)\right)\cdot 2 = 8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x).$$
> The three factors of 2 are the power rule, the inner derivative of $2x$, and the coefficient carried from the first differentiation; the two minus signs cancel.
>
> **b.** The outermost operation is $\sec$ applied to a polynomial, so this is the **chain rule** with $\frac{d}{du}\sec u = \sec u\tan u$, $u = x^2-3x$ and $u' = 2x-3$:
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\sec\left(x^2-3x\right) = (2x-3)\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)\tan\left(x^2-3x\right).$$
>
> **c.** The outermost operation is a division, so this is the **quotient rule** $\left(\frac uv\right)' = \frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ with $u = x^2-\cos 3x$ and $v = x\sin 2x$.
> Then $u' = 2x + 3\sin 3x$ **by the chain rule on $\cos 3x$**, where the two minus signs make the term positive, and $v' = \sin 2x + 2x\cos 2x$ **by the product rule**, with the chain rule inside the second term.
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^2-\cos 3x}{x\sin 2x} = \frac{\left(2x+3\sin 3x\right)\left(x\sin 2x\right) - \left(x^2-\cos 3x\right)\left(\sin 2x+2x\cos 2x\right)}{\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2}.$$
>
> **d.** By the **quotient rule**, evaluated after differentiating rather than before:
> $$\left.\left(\frac fg\right)'\right|_{x=1} = \frac{f'(1)g(1)-f(1)g'(1)}{\left[g(1)\right]^2}.$$
> Here $f'(1) = \sqrt 1 = 1$ and $g'(1) = 3\sin\frac\pi3 = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}$, the angle being **in radians**, so
> $$= \frac{1\cdot 6 - 3\cdot\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}}{6^2} = \frac{6-\frac{9\sqrt3}{2}}{36} = \frac{4-3\sqrt3}{24} \approx -0.0498.$$

Naming the rule in each part costs one clause and it is exactly what Q8.4 asks for explicitly.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The check for a second derivative, and why Q8.1's advice does not transfer

Q8.1's check is a centred **first** difference, $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$, and its advice is that $h = 0.001$ gives five good digits and smaller is harmless.
The analogue for $f''$ is the centred **second** difference:

> $$f''(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}$$
>
> Three evaluations, one subtraction pattern, and a division by $h^2$.
> **Use $h = 0.001$. Do not go smaller.**

The reason to state the $h$ so firmly is that the division by $h^2$ changes the arithmetic completely.
A calculator holds about ten significant digits, so each $f$ value carries an absolute error near $10^{-10}$; dividing by $h^2$ multiplies that error by $1/h^2$ instead of $1/h$.
At $h = 10^{-5}$ that is a factor of $10^{10}$, which is all of your precision.

Here is that measured on a simulated ten-digit machine, relative error in $f''$, five different functions:

| function and point | $h=0.1$ | $h=0.01$ | $h=0.001$ | $h=10^{-4}$ | $h=10^{-5}$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cot(2x)$ at $0.7$ | $2.9\times10^{-2}$ | $2.8\times10^{-4}$ | $\mathbf{6.5\times10^{-6}}$ | $9.8\times10^{-5}$ | $5.6\times10^{-2}$ |
| $x^2\sin x$ at $1.0$ | $3.8\times10^{-3}$ | $3.8\times10^{-5}$ | $\mathbf{3.4\times10^{-6}}$ | $2.3\times10^{-4}$ | $8.9\times10^{-4}$ |
| $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ at $0.5$ | $3.0\times10^{-2}$ | $3.0\times10^{-4}$ | $\mathbf{3.4\times10^{-10}}$ | $3.9\times10^{-8}$ | $2.2\times10^{-1}$ |
| $\tan(3x)$ at $0.5$ | $1.1$ | $2.2\times10^{-1}$ | $\mathbf{1.8\times10^{-3}}$ | $1.9\times10^{-5}$ | $5.8\times10^{-5}$ |
| $\sqrt{2x+1}$ at $1.1$ | $1.2\times10^{-3}$ | $\mathbf{1.3\times10^{-5}}$ | $4.1\times10^{-5}$ | $3.0\times10^{-2}$ | $1.0$ |

Read that table as a **valley**, not a slope.
Error falls as $h$ shrinks because the truncation term shrinks, then rises because the roundoff term grows, and the bottom of the valley is at $h = 0.001$ for four of the five.
$h=0.001$ gives at least three correct significant figures on every one, which is all a check needs.
$h = 0.01$ fails that bar on $\tan(3x)$, where it is off by 22 percent.
$h = 10^{-5}$ gives relative errors up to $1.0$, meaning **no correct figures at all** on two of the five, and the collapse from $h=0.001$ is a factor of 556 on the worst function and $6\times10^8$ on $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$.

**The one-sentence version: for $f'$ a smaller $h$ was free, and for $f''$ it is ruinous, so pick $0.001$ and stop.**
Every number in that table was measured on a machine deliberately truncated to ten significant digits, not estimated.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both exams are delivered through Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a right derivative with a bad keystroke scores zero.
Q8.1 section 7 has the general rules; these are the ones this question adds.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| write $\csc^2(2x)$ as `csc(2*x)^2`, with the exponent on the closing bracket | `csc^2(2*x)`, which most parsers read as $\csc$ raised to the power of $2(2x)$ |
| preview part (a) and confirm `csc` and `cot` render; if they do not, use `8*cos(2*x)/sin(2*x)^3` | assuming every parser defines all six trig functions |
| bracket the whole numerator **and** the whole denominator in part (c) | relying on precedence across a fifty-character expression |
| keep $\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2$ as `(x*sin(2*x))^2` | `x*sin(2*x)^2`, which squares only the sine |
| enter part (d) as the exact surd `(4-3*sqrt(3))/24` | `-0.0498`, which a numeric-tolerance field may accept and an exact field will not |
| **preview every answer before submitting** | trusting that what you typed is what it read |

**The single most expensive typo in this question** is the exponent position on part (a).
`csc(2*x)^2` is $\left[\csc(2x)\right]^2$ and `csc^2(2*x)` is not a thing most parsers accept at all, so the second one either errors or silently parses as something else.
Section 2.2 is the same distinction on paper, and it costs marks in both places.

**The second most expensive** is `x*sin(2*x)^2` in part (c)'s denominator.
It reads as $x\sin^2(2x)$ rather than $x^2\sin^2(2x)$, differs from the right answer by a whole factor of $x$, and looks completely normal.
The preview shows the difference immediately.

Unsimplified answers are accepted, because Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence rather than by string match.
So part (a) can be entered in any of the four verified equivalent forms, and part (c) can be entered exactly as the quotient rule produced it.
**That is the safety valve: when you are unsure of an algebraic step, enter the form you are sure of.**

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the bulk of the document and it is what makes the question un-surprisable.
The families are organised around **what happens on the second pass**, because that is Q8.2's axis.
Q8.1's catalog is organised around which rule you pick on the first pass, and the two are complementary rather than duplicated.

Read each table as *structure*, not as a list of facts.
In almost every family the entries differ in exactly one place, and naming that one place is the point.

### Family 0 - the second-derivative decision itself

The trigger is $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$, or $f''$, or the words "concave", "inflection" or "second derivative test".
The tool is the three-step wrapper in section 3.

| after pass 1, $f'$ looks like | tidy it first? | pass-2 rule | family |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a polynomial | nothing to do | sum and power | 1 |
| a single trig term with a coefficient | usually nothing to do | depends on the outer, **look again** | 2 |
| a quotient from the quotient rule | **yes, always** | quotient | 3 |
| a product of two trig factors | no | product | 2 |
| a sum of two products | no | product, twice | 5 |
| something with $\csc^2$ or $\sec^2$ in it | consider the Pythagorean rewrite | power | 4 |

**There are only two decisions in this table and one of them is nearly always "no".**
Decision one is whether to tidy, and it is "yes" essentially only for a quotient.
Decision two is which rule pass 2 needs, and the only safe way to answer it is to run the last-operation test again rather than to assume.

### Family 1 - the second derivative where the rule does not change

The trigger is a polynomial, a power, or a sine or cosine of a linear argument.
The tool is the same rule twice, which is why this family is easy and why it breeds the habit of not looking again.

| $f$ | $f'$ | $f''$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $4x^5+3x^4-6x^3+6$ | $20x^4+12x^3-18x^2$ | $80x^3+36x^2-36x$ |
| $x^{1/2}$ | $\tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$ | $-\tfrac14 x^{-3/2}$ |
| $\dfrac1x$ | $-\dfrac1{x^2}$ | $\dfrac{2}{x^3}$ |
| $x^{2/3}$ | $\tfrac23 x^{-1/3}$ | $-\tfrac29 x^{-4/3}$ |
| $(3x+1)^4$ | $12(3x+1)^3$ | $108(3x+1)^2$ |
| $\sqrt{2x+1}$ | $(2x+1)^{-1/2}$ | $-(2x+1)^{-3/2}$ |
| $\sin(2x)$ | $2\cos(2x)$ | $-4\sin(2x)$ |

**Read the seven rows as two facts.**
For a power, each differentiation drops the exponent by one and multiplies by the old exponent, so $f''$ has coefficient $n(n-1)$ and exponent $n-2$; that generates rows 2 through 6.
For $\sin(kx)$, two differentiations give $-k^2$ times the original, which generates row 7 and is worth knowing on its own.
Row 6 is the prettiest: the two halves cancel so cleanly that $f'$ has coefficient $1$ and $f''$ has coefficient $-1$.

### Family 2 - the second derivative where the rule DOES change, which is Q8.2a

The trigger is a first derivative that does not look like the first function.
The tool is the last-operation test, run a second time.

| $f$ | $f'$ | pass-2 rule | $f''$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cot(2x)$ | $-2\csc^2(2x)$ | **power** | $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ |
| $\cot(3x)$ | $-3\csc^2(3x)$ | **power** | $18\csc^2(3x)\cot(3x)$ |
| $\tan(3x)$ | $3\sec^2(3x)$ | **power** | $18\sec^2(3x)\tan(3x)$ |
| $\tan x$ | $\sec^2 x$ | **power** | $2\sec^2 x\tan x$ |
| $\sec x$ | $\sec x\tan x$ | **product** | $\sec x\tan^2 x + \sec^3 x$ |
| $\csc x$ | $-\csc x\cot x$ | **product** | $\csc x\cot^2 x + \csc^3 x$ |
| $\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | $-2x\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | **product** | $-2\sin\left(x^2\right) - 4x^2\cos\left(x^2\right)$ |
| $\sin(2x)$ | $2\cos(2x)$ | chain, **unchanged** | $-4\sin(2x)$ |

**This is the most important table in the document and it has exactly three shapes in it.**
Four rows go chain-to-power, because $\tan$ and $\cot$ differentiate into a square.
Three rows go chain-to-product, because $\sec$ and $\csc$ differentiate into a product, and $\cos(x^2)$'s chain factor $-2x$ is itself a function of $x$.
One row stays chain, because $\sin$ and $\cos$ differentiate into each other and nothing new appears.
**Knowing which of the six trig functions differentiates into a square and which into a product is the whole family**, and the answer is: $\tan$ and $\cot$ give squares, $\sec$ and $\csc$ give products, $\sin$ and $\cos$ give each other.

The coefficient pattern in the first four rows is worth memorising as a check: $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(kx) = 2k^2\csc^2(kx)\cot(kx)$ and $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\tan(kx) = 2k^2\sec^2(kx)\tan(kx)$, the two differing only in the sign that the two minus signs of $\cot$ have already cancelled.

Rows 5 and 6 also have a hidden structure worth naming: $\sec x\tan^2 x + \sec^3 x$ can be written $\sec x\left(\tan^2 x + \sec^2 x\right)$, or using $\tan^2 = \sec^2 - 1$, as $\sec x\left(2\sec^2 x - 1\right)$.
All three are correct and none is required.

### Family 3 - the second derivative of a quotient, where tidying $f'$ is mandatory

The trigger is $f''$ of anything with a division in it.
The tool is: quotient rule, **cancel**, quotient rule again.

| $f$ | tidy $f'$ | $f''$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$ | $\dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$ | $\dfrac{4(x-30)}{(x+3)^4}$ |
| $\dfrac{x}{x+1}$ | $\dfrac{1}{(x+1)^2}$ | $-\dfrac{2}{(x+1)^3}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^2+1}$ | $-\dfrac{2x}{\left(x^2+1\right)^2}$ | $\dfrac{6x^2-2}{\left(x^2+1\right)^3}$ |
| $\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}$ | $-\dfrac{3}{(x-2)^2}$ | $\dfrac{6}{(x-2)^3}$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2}{x-1}$ | $\dfrac{x^2-2x}{(x-1)^2}$ | $\dfrac{2}{(x-1)^3}$ |

**The invariant that runs down the whole table is the denominator's power.**
It goes $n$, then $n+1$, then $n+2$: every differentiation makes a pole one order worse and never better.
So if $f$ has $(x+3)^2$ on the bottom, $f''$ must have $(x+3)^4$, and any other power means you cancelled wrongly or not at all.
Rows 2, 4 and 5 also show the numerator collapsing to a constant, which happens whenever the original quotient is a Möbius function or reduces to one, and is a free check.

The point of the family is the cost of skipping the cancellation.
Row 1's raw $f'$ is $\dfrac{2(x+3)^2 - 2(2x-16)(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$, verified to be the same function as the tidy form.
Differentiating that is a quotient rule with a quadratic over a quartic, followed by cancelling $(x+3)^5$ out of $(x+3)^8$.
**Tidying first turns four minutes into one, and it is the only place in category 8 where the printed permission not to simplify is a trap rather than a gift.**

### Family 4 - the Pythagorean rewrite, a second route to the same $f''$

The trigger is a $\csc^2$ or $\sec^2$ that you now have to differentiate.
The tool is $\csc^2 u = 1 + \cot^2 u$ or $\sec^2 u = 1 + \tan^2 u$, applied to $f'$ before pass 2.

| $f'$ as it came out | rewritten | why it helps |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $-2\csc^2(2x)$ | $-2 - 2\cot^2(2x)$ | needs only $\frac{d}{dx}\cot$, not $\frac{d}{dx}\csc$ |
| $3\sec^2(3x)$ | $3 + 3\tan^2(3x)$ | needs only $\frac{d}{dx}\tan$, not $\frac{d}{dx}\sec$ |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $1 + \tan^2 x$ | same |

And the equivalent final forms of Q8.2a, all four verified identical over 14 points at $10^{-16}$ relative:

| form | how you get there |
| :--- | :--- |
| $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ | Route A, the standard answer |
| $8\cot(2x) + 8\cot^3(2x)$ | Route B, expanding $\csc^2 = 1+\cot^2$ |
| $\dfrac{8\cos(2x)}{\sin^3(2x)}$ | Route C, sines and cosines throughout |
| $\dfrac{16\cot(2x)}{1-\cos(4x)}$ | using $\sin^2 u = \frac{1-\cos 2u}{2}$ |

**Four spellings of one function.**
Any of them earns full marks and the third is the one to type if the parser does not know $\csc$.
The fourth is included to make the point that "the answer" is a function and not a string, which is exactly why Möbius grades by equivalence.

### Family 5 - the second derivative of a product, and Leibniz

The trigger is $f''$ of something with two $x$-dependent factors.
The tool is either the product rule twice, or the Leibniz form $(uv)'' = u''v + 2u'v' + uv''$ in one step.

| $f$ | $f''$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $x\sin x$ | $2\cos x - x\sin x$ |
| $x^2\sin x$ | $\left(2-x^2\right)\sin x + 4x\cos x$ |
| $x^2\cos 3x$ | $\left(2-9x^2\right)\cos 3x - 12x\sin 3x$ |
| $x^2\tan x$ | $2\tan x + 4x\sec^2 x + 2x^2\sec^2 x\tan x$ |
| $x^3\tan x$ | $6x\tan x + 6x^2\sec^2 x + 2x^3\sec^2 x\tan x$ |

**Rows 2 and 3 have the same shape and it is worth seeing why.**
Both are $x^2$ times a sinusoid, so Leibniz gives $2\cdot(\text{sinusoid}) + 2\cdot 2x\cdot(\text{sinusoid})' + x^2(\text{sinusoid})''$, and the third term contributes the $-x^2$ or $-9x^2$ depending on the frequency.
The $-9$ in row 3 is $-k^2$ with $k=3$, which is Family 1's row 7 reappearing.

Row 4 is the review question from `MATH265_Categorized.md`, asked as $\left.\frac{d^2}{dx^2}x^2\tan x\right|_{x=\pi}$.
At $x=\pi$, $\tan\pi = 0$ and $\sec^2\pi = 1$, so the first and third terms vanish and the answer is $4\pi$, verified.
**That is how these are constructed**: the evaluation point is chosen to kill two of the three terms, and spotting it turns a messy expression into one multiplication.

The Leibniz form was verified to reproduce row 2 exactly, so it is a genuine alternative and not just a mnemonic.

### Family 6 - the third and $n$-th derivative, where the pattern is the tool

The trigger is $\frac{d^3}{dx^3}$, or $f^{(n)}$, or "the 100th derivative".
The tool is to differentiate three or four times and **look for the cycle**.

| $f$ | $f'$ | $f''$ | $f'''$ | the pattern |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | $-\cos x$ | period 4 |
| $\dfrac1x$ | $-\dfrac1{x^2}$ | $\dfrac2{x^3}$ | $-\dfrac6{x^4}$ | $(-1)^n\dfrac{n!}{x^{n+1}}$ |
| $\sin(2x)$ | $2\cos 2x$ | $-4\sin 2x$ | $-8\cos 2x$ | period 4, with $2^n$ |
| $x^5$ | $5x^4$ | $20x^3$ | $60x^2$ | terminates at $n=6$ |
| $\cos(3x)$ | $-3\sin 3x$ | $-9\cos 3x$ | $27\sin 3x$ | period 4, with $3^n$ |

**Nothing in this family is new mathematics.**
It is the section 3 wrapper run three times instead of twice, and the only skill is noticing that you do not have to run it a hundred times.
For row 1, the 100th derivative of $\sin x$ is $\sin x$, because $100 = 4\times 25$.
For row 2, $f^{(4)} = 24/x^5$, verified.

The factorial in row 2 comes out of the exponents multiplying: $1\times 2\times 3\times\cdots$.
The powers of $k$ in rows 3 and 5 come out of the chain rule contributing one factor of $k$ per differentiation.

### Family 7 - the named-outer chain with a non-trivial inner, which is Q8.2b

The trigger is one of the six trig functions, or a root, wrapped around something that is not bare $x$.
The tool is the chain rule, and the only thing that varies is which outer derivative you write.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | $(2x-3)\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)\tan\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | **Q8.2b** |
| $\csc\left(x^2+1\right)$ | $-2x\csc\left(x^2+1\right)\cot\left(x^2+1\right)$ | |
| $\tan\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | $(2x-3)\sec^2\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | |
| $\cot\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | $-(2x-3)\csc^2\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | |
| $\sin\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | $(2x-3)\cos\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | |
| $\sec(3x)$ | $3\sec(3x)\tan(3x)$ | |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $2\sec^2 x\tan x$ | Q8.3e outer; **power** outer, for contrast |
| $\sqrt{x^2-3x}$ | $\dfrac{2x-3}{2\sqrt{x^2-3x}}$ | |

**Eight rows and one idea**: the outer derivative from the trig table, evaluated at the **untouched** inner, times the inner derivative.
The inner derivative $2x-3$ is the same in five of the eight rows, which is the examiner reusing one polynomial.
The signs are not eight facts either: they are the three minus signs from $\cos$, $\cot$ and $\csc$, exactly as Q8.1 section 2.2 says.
Row 7 is in the table on purpose, as the contrast: $\sec^2 x$ is a **square**, so its last operation is the power and not the $\sec$, which is section 2.2's distinction again.

### Family 8 - a quotient whose numerator and denominator each need a rule, which is Q8.2c

The trigger is a division where $u$, $v$, or both, need a rule of their own.
The tool is the quotient rule with a sub-derivation for each piece, written on separate lines.

| $f$ | $f'$ | what the sub-rules are |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{x^2-\cos 3x}{x\sin 2x}$ | $\dfrac{\left(2x+3\sin 3x\right)\left(x\sin 2x\right)-\left(x^2-\cos 3x\right)\left(\sin 2x+2x\cos 2x\right)}{\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2}$ | chain in $u'$, **product** in $v'$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2+\sin x}{x\cos x}$ | $\dfrac{(2x+\cos x)(x\cos x)-\left(x^2+\sin x\right)(\cos x - x\sin x)}{(x\cos x)^2}$ | sum in $u'$, product in $v'$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin 3x}{x^2+1}$ | $\dfrac{3\cos 3x\left(x^2+1\right)-2x\sin 3x}{\left(x^2+1\right)^2}$ | chain in $u'$, nothing in $v'$ |
| $\dfrac{x\tan x}{x^2-1}$ | $\dfrac{\left(\tan x + x\sec^2 x\right)\left(x^2-1\right)-2x^2\tan x}{\left(x^2-1\right)^2}$ | **product** in $u'$ |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{x^2-2x-8}$ | $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)-\sqrt{x^2-1}(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$ | chain in $u'$, Q8.3d |

**Every one of these five answers is final as written**, and that is the whole reason the printed permission exists.
The structure is identical in all five: two products in the numerator with a minus between them, and the untouched denominator squared underneath.
What varies is only which of the four slots $u$, $u'$, $v$, $v'$ needed a rule of its own, and the discipline that makes this family safe is to compute all four **before** you write the fraction.

Row 1 versus row 4 is the pair worth studying: the product rule appears in the denominator in one and the numerator in the other, and the quotient rule does not care.

### Family 9 - a derivative built from given values at one point, which is Q8.2d

The trigger is numbers instead of formulas, with or without a vertical evaluation bar.
The tool is: **write the rule with letters, substitute last**.

With Q8.2d's data, $f(1)=3$, $g(1)=6$, $f'(1)=1$, $g'(1)=\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}$:

| asked | rule | answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\left(\frac fg\right)'(1)$ | quotient | $\dfrac{4-3\sqrt3}{24} \approx -0.0498397$ |
| $\left(\frac gf\right)'(1)$ | quotient, the other way | $\dfrac{3\sqrt3-4}{6} \approx 0.1993587$ |
| $(fg)'(1)$ | product | $6 + \dfrac{9\sqrt3}{2} \approx 13.7942286$ |
| $(f+g)'(1)$ | sum | $1 + \dfrac{3\sqrt3}{2} \approx 3.5980762$ |
| $\left(f^2\right)'(1)$ | chain, power outer | $2f f' = 6$ |
| $\left(\frac fx\right)'(1)$ | quotient with $v=x$ | $-2$ |
| $\left(x^2 f\right)'(1)$ | product | $7$ |

**Ten rows, one procedure, and the only thing that changes is which rule the outer structure asks for.**
Notice that rows 1 and 2 are not negatives of each other, which is the reason the quotient rule's order matters: swapping $f$ and $g$ changes the denominator from $g^2$ to $f^2$ as well as flipping the numerator.
Notice too that $\sqrt3$ appears in seven of the ten answers, all of it coming from the single evaluation $\sin\frac\pi3 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.

The review file's cousin of this question, from `MATH265_Categorized.md`, is $\left.\frac{d}{dx}\frac{f-x^2g}{f+g}\right|_{x=0}$ with $f(0)=1$, $f'(0)=2$, $g(0)=0$, $g'(0)=-1$, and the answer is $1$, verified.
Its extra difficulty is that the numerator contains $x^2 g$, which needs the **product** rule before you substitute, and substituting $x=0$ early makes that term vanish for the wrong reason.

**The one rule in this family is the order of operations**, and it is worth stating as a sentence you can hear yourself say: differentiate with the letters, then put the numbers in, and check the calculator is in radians before you put any number in at all.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

1. **Not asking the last-operation question a second time.** You differentiate $\cot(2x)$ correctly, then differentiate $-2\csc^2(2x)$ as if it were still a plain chain rule and lose the power rule. This is the trap Q8.2 exists to test and it is the reason section 3 has a step C.
2. **Losing one of the three factors of 2 in part (a).** The answer $4\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ is what you get, and it is half the right answer. Route D's $2k^2$ is an independent count of those 2s and it takes five seconds.
3. **A calculator in degrees on part (d).** It gives $+0.162$ instead of $-0.0498$: wrong sign, wrong size, and completely plausible. The sign invariant in section 6 catches it and nothing else will.
4. **Substituting $x=1$ before differentiating in part (d).** $\frac36 = \frac12$ differentiates to $0$. The zero looks like an answer.
5. **Forgetting the product rule on $v = x\sin 2x$ in part (c).** Writing $v' = 2x\cos 2x$ loses the whole $\sin 2x$ term. This is the one place Q8.1's absent product rule shows up, one level down where it is easy to miss.
6. **The sign of $\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos 3x)$ in part (c).** It is $+3\sin 3x$: two minus signs. Writing $-3\sin 3x$ is the plausible error.
7. **Reading $\csc^2(2x)$ as $\csc\left(4x^2\right)$, or the reverse.** Section 2.2. It changes which rule pass 2 needs, so it is not a notation slip, it is a wrong method.
8. **Differentiating an untidied $f'$ before a second differentiation.** Not wrong, just four times the work and four times the risk. Family 3.
9. **Dropping the inner derivative $(2x-3)$ in part (b).** Q8.1's trap 1, still the most common error in the course, in its easiest costume here.
10. **Writing $\frac{d}{dx}\sec u = \sec^2 u$.** That is $\tan$'s derivative. $\sec$ gives $\sec u\tan u$, a **product**, and $\tan$ gives $\sec^2 u$, a **square**, and confusing them changes the shape of the answer.
11. **Using $h$ too small on the second-difference check.** $h=10^{-5}$ gives zero correct figures, so a right answer looks wrong and you "fix" it. Section 6's table is the reason to use $0.001$ and not less.
12. **Expanding $\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2$ in part (c).** It gains nothing, and `x*sin(2*x)^2` on Möbius is a different function.
13. **Simplifying $f''$ in part (a) at all.** The two $\csc$ factors have already combined; anything further is pure risk.

Read the nine as three lessons.
Three of them are the same failure in three costumes: a numerical method used outside the regime where it is valid, once at a fixed $h$ on a first difference in the Q8.1 run, once on a third difference here, and once as a tolerance tighter than a method's floor.
Two of them are **invented numbers**: the 0.15 pole threshold and the "smaller $h$ is worse" claim, and both looked like measurements. The pole one is the more instructive, because replacing the guess with a derived bound exposed four bad sample points that the guess had waved through.
Two of them are gates that could not fail, which is the same disease as an invented threshold seen from the other side.
Only two were plain slips, the reconstruction constant and the backwards concavity, and both were caught by arms that do not share code with the thing they check.
