# Q15.2 - Rectangle of least perimeter

**The question (Final Exam 1, item 6, 6 points).**

### 1.2 The one idea

Q15.1's method is a **comparison**: list the candidates, evaluate them all, pick the largest and the smallest.
That method is licensed by the Extreme Value Theorem, which needs a **closed and bounded** interval, and the theorem is what makes the comparison a proof rather than a guess.

Here the feasible set is $w > 0$.
It is open at the left end, because a width of $0$ has no matching length at all.
It is unbounded at the right end, because no rule caps how long a thin rectangle may be.
So the Extreme Value Theorem does not apply, there are no endpoints to put in the table, and the comparison cannot be run.

**The answer is therefore free and the argument is the whole question.**
Almost any wrong route lands on the square: three of the ten plausible mutilations tested in this run report the correct dimensions.
What separates a full-mark answer from a lucky one is the sentence that says why $\sqrt{220}$ is the global minimum, and that sentence has to come from convexity, a sign change, or a limit at the ends.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

### 2.1 The two formulas, and which is which

| quantity | formula | unit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| area of a $w$ by $l$ rectangle | $A = wl$ | cm$^2$ |
| perimeter of a $w$ by $l$ rectangle | $P = 2w + 2l$ | cm |

Neither is printed on the exam.
The one coincidence worth knowing so it does not confuse you: a $4$ by $4$ square has area $16$ and perimeter $16$, and it is the only rectangle where the two numbers agree.
A $5$ by $5$ square has area $25$ and perimeter $20$.

### 2.2 Constraint against objective

| the constraint | the objective |
| :--- | :--- |
| a **given** number in the stem | the thing the stem says to make small or large |
| an **equation**, so it can be solved for one variable | a **function**, so it can be differentiated |
| here: $wl = 220$ | here: $P = 2w + 2l$ |
| never differentiated | always differentiated |

Getting these the wrong way round is the one modelling error that produces nonsense rather than a wrong number.
Differentiating the constraint instead of the objective gives $\frac{d}{dw}(220) = 0$, which says nothing at all.

### 2.3 Why the domain is open, in one sentence each

| end | why it is not attainable |
| :--- | :--- |
| $w = 0$ | a rectangle of zero width has no length that gives it area $220$ |
| $w \to \infty$ | nothing in the stem caps the width, and every large $w$ has a matching thin $l$ |

Both ends therefore have to be handled by a **limit statement**, not by an evaluation.
$P(10^{-9}) > 4 \times 10^{11}$ and $P(10^{9}) > 2 \times 10^{9}$, both measured, and the growth is monotone over eight decades in each direction.

### 2.4 The three ways to justify a minimum on an open domain

| argument | what you write | when to reach for it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **convexity** | $P'' = 880/w^3 > 0$ for all $w > 0$, so the local minimum is global | first choice here, it is one line and it settles everything |
| **sign change** | $P' < 0$ before $\sqrt{220}$ and $P' > 0$ after, so $P$ falls then rises | always available, and the only one that survives $P'' = 0$ |
| **the ends** | $P \to \infty$ at both ends, so the minimum cannot escape to an end | the honest one, and the only one that explains why a minimum exists |

The second-derivative test **is** available on this question, which is a change from Q15.1 where it was unusable on both parts.
On Q15.1 part (a) it blows up at the very point it is wanted, because $f''$ carries an $x^{-2/3}$ term at $x = 0$.
On part (b) it is silent, because $f''(\pi/2) = 0$.
Here $P''(\sqrt{220}) = 4/\sqrt{220} \approx 0.269680$, comfortably positive.

**Convexity is strictly stronger than the one-point test**, and that is worth saying explicitly.
$P''(c) > 0$ at the critical point proves a **local** minimum.
$P'' > 0$ on the **whole** domain proves the local minimum is the **global** one, with no comparison and no endpoints, which is exactly the gap the open domain opens up.

### 2.5 The positive root is a domain decision, not a convention

$P'(w) = 0$ gives $w^2 = 220$, which has **two** real roots.
The census in this run found both: $+\sqrt{220}$ and $-\sqrt{220}$.
The negative one is a genuine critical point of the algebraic function $2w + 440/w$, and $P''(-\sqrt{220}) < 0$, so it is a local **maximum**.

So "take the positive root" is not tidiness.
It is the feasible domain doing its job, and the discarded root is the wrong kind of extreme as well as the wrong sign.
Write the reason down: *a width must be positive, so $w = \sqrt{220}$.*

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Name the variables** and draw the thing. Two unknowns, $w$ and $l$.
> 2. **Write the constraint** from the given number. $wl = 220$.
> 3. **Write the objective** from what the stem asks to optimise. $P = 2w + 2l$.
> 4. **Check the units are homogeneous.** Every term of $P$ must be a length.
> 5. **Eliminate** using the constraint. $l = 220/w$, so $P(w) = 2w + 440/w$.
> 6. **State the domain.** $w > 0$, open at both ends.
> 7. **Differentiate and solve.** $P'(w) = 2 - 440/w^2 = 0$, so $w = \sqrt{220}$.
> 8. **Discard infeasible roots**, saying why. $-\sqrt{220}$ is not a width.
> 9. **JUSTIFY**, because there are no endpoints. $P'' > 0$ everywhere on $(0,\infty)$.
> 10. **Recover the other variable.** $l = 220/\sqrt{220} = \sqrt{220}$.
> 11. **Check the constraint on your own answer.** $\sqrt{220}\cdot\sqrt{220} = 220$.
> 12. **Answer the question asked.** Dimensions first, then the perimeter.

### 3.1 Which step the marks are on

There is no marking scheme anywhere in the four course files, so what follows is inference and is flagged as such.
Zero occurrences of "rubric", "marking scheme" or "part marks" across all four files, re-verified this run for the eleventh consecutive time.

That said, steps 2, 3, 5, 7 and 12 are the ones a marker can see went missing, and step **9** is the one a student is most likely to skip while still writing the right number.
On a 6-point item with four things to produce, the safest reading is that the model, the derivative, the justification and the two reported answers each carry weight.

### 3.2 Why step 6 comes before step 7 and not after

If the domain is written down before the differentiation, step 8 is automatic and step 9 is obviously necessary.
If it is written down after, the temptation is to invent an interval to compare on, and inventing one gets the right answer here by luck.
See trap 4.

## 4. Q15.2 worked four ways

### 4.1 Route A, eliminate and differentiate

Let $w$ be the width and $l$ the length, both in cm.

The constraint is $wl = 220$, so $l = \dfrac{220}{w}$.

The objective is
$$P = 2w + 2l = 2w + 2\cdot\frac{220}{w} = 2w + \frac{440}{w}, \qquad w > 0.$$

Differentiate:
$$P'(w) = 2 - \frac{440}{w^2}.$$

Set it to zero:
$$2 = \frac{440}{w^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w^2 = 220 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad w = \sqrt{220}$$
taking the positive root because $w$ is a width.

Justify:
$$P''(w) = \frac{880}{w^3} > 0 \text{ for every } w > 0,$$
so $P$ is strictly convex on the whole feasible domain and the critical point is the **absolute** minimum.

Recover the length: $l = \dfrac{220}{\sqrt{220}} = \sqrt{220}$, so the rectangle is a square.

Report:
$$w = l = \sqrt{220} = 2\sqrt{55} \approx 14.8324 \text{ cm}, \qquad P = 4\sqrt{220} = 8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.3296 \text{ cm}.$$

### 4.2 Route B, the sign chart instead of the second derivative

Identical up to $w = \sqrt{220}$, then:

| interval | test point | $P'$ there | $P$ is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(0,\sqrt{220})$ | $w = 14$ | $2 - 440/196 = -0.2449$ | decreasing |
| $(\sqrt{220},\infty)$ | $w = 20$ | $2 - 440/400 = +0.9$ | increasing |

One sign change, minus to plus, so $w = \sqrt{220}$ is a minimum.
The sign is constant on each side, checked at 2000 sample points per side in this run, so there is nowhere else for a smaller value to hide.

This route is longer to write but it never fails.
Reach for it whenever $P''$ at the critical point is zero or undefined, which is precisely what happened on both parts of Q15.1.

### 4.3 Route C, AM-GM, with no calculus at all

For positive $a$ and $b$, $a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$ with equality exactly when $a = b$.
Apply it to the two terms of the objective:
$$P(w) = 2w + \frac{440}{w} \ \ge\ 2\sqrt{2w \cdot \frac{440}{w}} = 2\sqrt{880} = 8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.3296,$$
with equality exactly when $2w = 440/w$, that is $w^2 = 220$.

This is a complete proof of a **global** minimum on the open domain in two lines, and it needs no derivative, no domain discussion and no endpoint.
The bound was checked at 5000 widths in this run and holds at every one.

Two warnings.
The exponents have to be $+1$ and $-1$ for the two-term version to work, so this route does **not** transfer to Q15.4 as written: the naive two-term AM-GM on $5.4w^2 + 432/w$ gives $2\sqrt{5.4 \cdot 432} \approx 96.598137$, which is far below the true minimum $189.476349$ and is therefore not a valid bound at all.
The three-term version does work there: split the middle term and $5.4w^2 + \frac{216}{w} + \frac{216}{w} \ge 3\sqrt[3]{5.4\cdot 216^2} \approx 189.476349$, which is exact.

### 4.4 Route D, the symmetry route

Let $s = w + l$ be the semi-perimeter.
Then $w$ and $l$ are the two roots of
$$t^2 - st + 220 = 0,$$
which are real numbers exactly when the discriminant $s^2 - 880 \ge 0$, that is $s \ge \sqrt{880}$.

So the smallest achievable semi-perimeter is $\sqrt{880}$, the perimeter is $2\sqrt{880} = 8\sqrt{55}$, and at that value the discriminant is exactly zero, so the two roots coincide and the rectangle **is** a square.

This route explains **why** the answer is a square rather than merely computing it.
A worked check: at $s = 32$ the discriminant is $1024 - 880 = 144$, the roots are $10$ and $22$, and that rectangle's perimeter is $64$, above the minimum as it must be.

### 4.5 Route comparison

| route | what it needs | length | proves it is GLOBAL? | fails when |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, eliminate + $P''$ | one derivative, one second derivative | 6 lines | yes, if you say $P''>0$ **everywhere** | $P''(c) = 0$ or undefined |
| B, eliminate + sign chart | one derivative, two test points | 8 lines | yes, with a sentence about the ends | never, but it is the slowest |
| C, AM-GM | the inequality, memorised | 2 lines | yes, outright | exponents are not $\pm 1$ |
| D, symmetry | the discriminant | 4 lines | yes, outright | the objective is not symmetric |

**Use route A under pressure.**
It is the one the course notes set up, it is the shortest complete argument, and the second-derivative test genuinely works on this function.
Write the words "for all $w > 0$" after $P'' > 0$, because that phrase is what upgrades local to global and it costs three words.

Keep route C in your pocket as a **check**, not as the answer.
It gives $8\sqrt{55}$ in one line, so it confirms the perimeter before you commit.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

### 5.1 The short form, as it should appear on the paper

> Let the rectangle be $w$ by $l$ cm.
> Constraint: $wl = 220$, so $l = 220/w$, with $w > 0$.
> Objective: $P = 2w + 2l = 2w + 440/w$.
> $P'(w) = 2 - 440/w^2 = 0 \Rightarrow w^2 = 220 \Rightarrow w = \sqrt{220}$ (positive, since $w$ is a width).
> $P''(w) = 880/w^3 > 0$ for all $w > 0$, so $P$ is convex and this is the absolute minimum.
> Then $l = 220/\sqrt{220} = \sqrt{220}$, so the rectangle is a square.
> **Dimensions $\sqrt{220} = 2\sqrt{55} \approx 14.8324$ cm by $2\sqrt{55}$ cm; perimeter $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.3296$ cm.**

Seven lines.

### 5.2 The sentences that are doing work

| sentence | what it buys |
| :--- | :--- |
| "Let the rectangle be $w$ by $l$ cm" | names the variables, so everything after it has a referent |
| "with $w > 0$" | states the domain, which is what makes step 9 obviously necessary |
| "positive, since $w$ is a width" | discards the second root for a reason instead of silently |
| "**for all** $w > 0$" | upgrades local to global; without it the argument is incomplete |
| "so the rectangle is a square" | answers the "dimensions" half in words as well as numbers |
| the two answers written separately | protects against the value-for-point confusion |

### 5.3 What to write if you are asked for the largest perimeter instead

> $P(w) = 2w + 440/w$ on $w > 0$.
> $P \to \infty$ as $w \to 0^+$ and as $w \to \infty$, so $P$ is unbounded above and **there is no largest perimeter**.

That is a complete answer, and it is a written answer rather than a blank.
Q15.1's lesson that an absence is a deliverable carries over unchanged.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### 6.1 Four checks that actually catch things

| check | on this answer | catches |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **the constraint**: multiply your dimensions back | $\sqrt{220}\cdot\sqrt{220} = 220$ | a rounded or mis-solved dimension |
| **the two terms**: are $2w$ and $440/w$ equal? | $2\sqrt{220} = 440/\sqrt{220}$, both $29.664794$ | an arithmetic slip in solving $P'=0$ |
| **AM-GM**: is $P \ge 2\sqrt{880}$, with equality? | $8\sqrt{55} = 59.3296$, equality | a wrong perimeter |
| **the shape**: is it a square? | yes | nothing, and that is the point (see trap 1) |

The second check is the cheapest and it is specific to a $aw + b/w$ objective.
At the minimum of such a function the rising term and the falling term are **exactly equal**, each contributing half the perimeter.
That is one multiplication and it needs no derivative.

Do **not** try to check the dimensions using the perimeter.
The objective is flat to second order at the minimum, so a width of $15$ instead of $14.8324$ is $1.13$ percent wrong and gives a perimeter of $59.333333$ against the true $59.329588$, which is $0.0063$ percent wrong and agrees to four significant figures.
And $15$ by $220/15$ satisfies the constraint exactly, so the constraint check does not catch it either.
Only the derivative does.

### 6.2 Magnitude sanity

$220$ is between $196 = 14^2$ and $225 = 15^2$, so the side is between $14$ and $15$ and nearer to $15$.
The perimeter is therefore a shade under $60$.
Any answer far from $59.3$ is wrong, and any answer of exactly $60$ has been rounded somewhere it should not have been.

### 6.3 Numbers worth memorising

| quantity | exact | decimal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the side | $\sqrt{220} = 2\sqrt{55}$ | $14.8323969742$ |
| the perimeter | $4\sqrt{220} = 8\sqrt{55} = \sqrt{3520} = 2\sqrt{880}$ | $59.3295878968$ |
| the semi-perimeter | $2\sqrt{220} = \sqrt{880}$ | $29.664794$ |
| $P''$ at the minimum | $4/\sqrt{220}$ | $0.269680$ |
| the diagonal of that square | $\sqrt{440}$ | $20.976177$ |

Note $\sqrt{440} = 20.976177$ appears three separate times in this question's neighbourhood: as the square's diagonal, as the long side of the three-sided-fence variation, and as the answer to "solve $w^2 = 440$ by mistake".
That is a coincidence and it is a trap, because seeing $20.976177$ come out is not evidence you solved the right problem.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| give **both** dimensions and the perimeter, as three answers | give one number and hope |
| write `2*sqrt(55)` or `sqrt(220)`, with the explicit `*` | write `2sqrt(55)` |
| write `8*sqrt(55)` for the perimeter | write `4sqrt(220)` without the `*` |
| say the shape in words too, "a square of side ..." | leave the marker to infer it |
| keep the radical exact | round to `14.83` and lose the constraint |
| write `sqrt(220)` not `220^1/2` | write `220^1/2`, which reads as $220/2 = 110$ |
| bracket any fractional exponent as `(1/2)` | write `^1/2` |

**The single most expensive typo** is the unbracketed fractional exponent, exactly as on Q15.1.
`220^(1/2)` is $14.8324$ and `220^1/2` is $110$, a factor of about seven, and Möbius marks the second one wrong without comment.

**The second most expensive** is answering `59.3296` where the dimensions are wanted.
That is exactly four times the true side, because the answer is a square, so the error is a clean factor of four rather than something that looks like a rounding difference.
Answering `14.8324` for the perimeter is $75$ percent low.

**Always preview.**
The exam is answer-only and machine-marked, so a right method with a bad keystroke scores zero.

## 8. The variation catalog

Ten families, grouped by **the tool the variation forces**.
Read each table as structure rather than as a list of facts.
Families 0 and 1 contain exactly one magnitude between them, $\sqrt{A}$; families 2 and 3 contain exactly one, $\sqrt{bA/a}$; and the whole catalog is three formulas with different letters in them.

### Family 0 - fixed area, minimise perimeter

**Trigger:** the area is given and the perimeter is to be made small.
**Tool:** $P(w) = 2w + 2A/w$, minimiser $\sqrt{A}$, minimum $4\sqrt{A}$.

| area $A$ | side | minimum perimeter |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $1$ | $1$ | $4$ |
| $24$ | $2\sqrt{6} \approx 4.898979$ | $8\sqrt{6} \approx 19.595918$ |
| $100$ | $10$ | $40$ |
| $144$ | $12$ | $48$ |
| $220$ | $2\sqrt{55} \approx 14.832397$ | $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ |
| $500$ | $10\sqrt{5} \approx 22.360680$ | $40\sqrt{5} \approx 89.442719$ |
| $1000$ | $10\sqrt{10} \approx 31.622777$ | $40\sqrt{10} \approx 126.491106$ |

**Every row is a square and every minimum is exactly four times the side.**
Three of the seven areas give an integer side, and the exam's $220$ is not one of them, so a radical answer is the normal case.

### Family 1 - fixed perimeter, maximise area (the dual)

**Trigger:** the perimeter is given and the area is to be made large.
**Tool:** $A(w) = w(P/2 - w)$, a downward parabola; maximiser $P/4$, maximum $P^2/16$.

| perimeter $P$ | side | maximum area |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $40$ | $10$ | $100$ |
| $220$ | $55$ | $3025$ |
| $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ | $2\sqrt{55}$ | exactly $220$ |

The third row is this exam question run backwards, and it closes the circle.

Two things make this family easier than family 0 and both are worth noticing.
The objective is a **parabola**, so $A'' = -2$ is a constant and concavity settles the whole domain in one stroke, whereas family 0's $P'' = 880/w^3$ varies with $w$.
And the two deliverables differ in **units**, $55$ cm against $3025$ cm$^2$, so the value-for-point confusion is much harder to make.

The domain is $(0, P/2)$, still open, and now there is a maximum and **no minimum**: the area falls towards $0$ at both ends without ever reaching it.

### Family 2 - only some of the sides are fenced

**Trigger:** a wall, a river, an existing fence, or an internal divider.
**Tool:** minimise $aw + bl$ subject to $wl = A$. Then $w = \sqrt{bA/a}$, $l = \sqrt{aA/b}$, minimum $2\sqrt{abA}$, and $l/w = a/b$.

| situation | $a$ | $b$ | $w$ | $l$ | minimum |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| three sides, wall behind ($2w + l$) | $2$ | $1$ | $\sqrt{110} \approx 10.488088$ | $\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177$ | $2\sqrt{440} \approx 41.952354$ |
| three sides, wall on a long side ($w + 2l$) | $1$ | $2$ | $\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177$ | $\sqrt{110} \approx 10.488088$ | $2\sqrt{440} \approx 41.952354$ |
| **all four sides ($2w + 2l$), the exam** | $2$ | $2$ | $2\sqrt{55}$ | $2\sqrt{55}$ | $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ |
| four sides plus one divider ($2w + 3l$) | $2$ | $3$ | $\sqrt{330} \approx 18.165902$ | $\sqrt{440/3} \approx 12.110601$ | $2\sqrt{1320} \approx 72.663608$ |
| four sides plus two dividers ($2w + 4l$) | $2$ | $4$ | $\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177$ | $\sqrt{110} \approx 10.488088$ | $2\sqrt{1760} \approx 83.904708$ |

**The exam's row is the only one in the family that gives a square**, because $l/w = a/b$ equals $1$ only when $a = b$.
Every other row is a rectangle whose side ratio is exactly the ratio of the two weights.

The first row is the most useful entry in the whole catalog, because it is **also the wrong answer** a student produces by forgetting to double one side of the exam question.
Dropping one of the four sides turns Q15.2 into the wall problem, which has a perfectly respectable answer of its own, so the slip does not look like a slip.

### Family 3 - the sides cost different amounts

**Trigger:** a price per unit length that differs between the pairs of sides.
**Tool:** minimise $2c_1 w + 2c_2 l$ subject to $wl = A$. Then $w = \sqrt{c_2 A / c_1}$ and the minimum cost is $4\sqrt{c_1 c_2 A}$.

| $c_1$ | $c_2$ | $w$ | $l$ | minimum cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $1$ | $1$ | $2\sqrt{55} \approx 14.832397$ | $2\sqrt{55}$ | $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ |
| $1$ | $3$ | $\sqrt{660} \approx 25.690465$ | $\sqrt{220/3} \approx 8.563488$ | $4\sqrt{660} \approx 102.761861$ |
| $1.2$ | $1.5$ | $\sqrt{275} \approx 16.583124$ | $\sqrt{176} \approx 13.266499$ | $4\sqrt{396} \approx 79.598995$ |
| $3$ | $1$ | $\sqrt{220/3} \approx 8.563488$ | $\sqrt{660} \approx 25.690465$ | $4\sqrt{660} \approx 102.761861$ |

**The expensive pair of sides comes out short.**
At a $3:1$ cost ratio the side ratio is exactly $3$, and using the square instead would make the width $73.21$ percent too large.
Family 0 is just the $c_1 = c_2$ row of this family, which is the honest way to see why the square appears at all.

### Family 4 - an extra restriction closes the domain

**Trigger:** a printed cap or floor on a dimension, or an integer requirement.
**Tool:** the domain becomes closed at one or both ends, so Q15.1's comparison comes back.

| restriction | domain | where the minimum is | value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $w \le 10$ | $(0,10]$ | the **cap**, because $P$ is still decreasing there | $P(10) = 64$, with $l = 22$ |
| $w \ge 20$ | $[20,\infty)$ | the **floor**, because $P$ is already increasing | $P(20) = 62$, with $l = 11$ |
| $10 \le w \le 20$ | $[10,20]$ | the interior critical point, which is inside | $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ |
| both sides whole numbers | the divisors of $220$ | $w = 11$, $l = 20$ | $P = 62$ |

The third row is the seeded control for the whole family, and its lesson is that **the method was never wrong, the domain was open**.
Close the domain and the comparison works again, and on $[10,20]$ a maximum exists too, $64$ at the left endpoint.

The fourth row is worth a second look.
The integer answer is $11$ by $20$, **not** the $10$ by $22$ that looks like the obvious near-square pair, because $11 + 20 = 31$ beats $10 + 22 = 32$.
Both are feasible, so this is a wrong answer that is reachable rather than absurd.

### Family 5 - the same algebra with no geometry

**Trigger:** "two positive numbers whose product is ...", "a number plus its reciprocal".
**Tool:** identical; the geometry was never doing any work.

| problem | answer | minimum |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| product $220$, least sum | both $2\sqrt{55}$ | $2\sqrt{220} \approx 29.664794$ |
| product $220$, least sum, **whole numbers** | $11$ and $20$ | $31$ |
| $w + 1/w$ for $w > 0$ | $w = 1$ | $2$ |

The first row is exactly the **semi-perimeter slip** from the trap list, and it is a legitimate exam question in its own right.
That is precisely why the slip is invisible in the dimensions: forgetting the factor $2$ turns this question into a different real question with the **same** answer for the sides.

The third row is the family stripped to its bones, and the answer being $2$ rather than $0$ is worth having in your head.

### Family 6 - the objective is a power sum, not a linear sum

**Trigger:** a three-dimensional object, so the objective has a squared term.
**Tool:** minimise $aw^p + bw^{-q}$. At the minimum the reciprocal term over the power term is $p:q$, **not** $q:p$.

| problem | objective | answer | minimum |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the exam | $2w + 440/w$ | $w = 2\sqrt{55}$ | $8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.329588$ |
| open-top box, square base, volume $220$ | $x^2 + 880/x$ | $x = \sqrt[3]{440} \approx 7.605905$, $h = x/2 \approx 3.802952$ | $3\cdot 440^{2/3} \approx 173.549369$ |
| closed can, volume $220$ | $2\pi r^2 + 440/r$ | $r \approx 3.271505$, $h = 2r \approx 6.543010$ | $6\pi r^2 \approx 201.742004$ |
| **Q15.4's cost** | $5.4w^2 + 432/w$ | $w = \sqrt[3]{40} \approx 3.419952$ | $16.2\cdot 40^{2/3} \approx 189.476349$ |

Two shapes in this family are the three-dimensional analogues of the square, and both are worth memorising.
The open box comes out **exactly twice as wide as it is tall**.
The closed can comes out with its **height equal to its diameter**, $h = 2r$.

The "two terms are equal" check from section 6.1 **fails** here, because $p = 2$.
On the last row the two terms measure $63.2$ and $126.3$, a ratio of exactly $2$, which is the exponent ratio.

### Family 7 - the extreme does not exist

**Trigger:** the question asks for the optimum in the direction the objective runs away in.
**Tool:** a limit statement, and the words "there is none".

| problem | domain | what exists | what does not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| area $220$, **largest** perimeter | $(0,\infty)$ | nothing | no maximum; $P$ is unbounded above |
| perimeter $40$, **smallest** area | $(0,20)$ | nothing | no minimum; the infimum $0$ is not attained |
| area $220$, smallest perimeter | $(0,\infty)$ | the minimum $8\sqrt{55}$ | no maximum |
| perimeter $40$, largest area | $(0,20)$ | the maximum $100$ | no minimum |

Read the four rows together.
On an open domain **either** outcome is possible for either direction, and only the behaviour at the ends tells you which.
That is the whole reason step 6 of the procedure exists.

### Family 8 - which justification to write

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks.

1. **Skipping the justification, because the number came out clean.**
   The domain is open, so there is nothing to compare against and the derivative test or the sign chart is the only evidence there is.
   This is the one trap that is specific to this question and it is the most expensive.

2. **The square hides three different wrong objectives.**
   Minimising the semi-perimeter $w + l$, the diagonal $\sqrt{w^2+l^2}$, or $w^2 + l^2$ all give **exactly** $w = l = \sqrt{220}$, because all three are symmetric in $w$ and $l$ and are therefore stationary where the two are equal.
   Three of ten mutilations tested in this run report the correct dimensions.
   So "I got a square, so I must be right" is worth nothing, and the shape is not a check.

3. **Forgetting to double one side.**
   Minimising $2w + l$ gives $\sqrt{110} \approx 10.488088$ by $\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177$ with a total of $41.952354$.
   That is the correct answer to the three-sides-against-a-wall problem, so it looks like a real answer rather than an error.
   Catch it by checking that the objective has **four** side lengths in it before differentiating.

4. **Inventing a closed interval so the Q15.1 method can be used.**
   Choosing $[1,100]$ and comparing $P(1) = 442$, $P(100) = 204.4$ and $P(\sqrt{220})$ returns the **correct** minimum, so the wrong method is rewarded.
   Choosing $[16,100]$ returns $59.5$ instead, which is only $0.2872$ percent above the truth and looks like a rounding difference.
   And the invented interval reports a spurious maximum of $442$ where the real problem has none.
   A wrong rule that gives the right answer on the exam question is the hardest kind of error to notice, and it is the third consecutive category in this queue where one turned up.

5. **Reporting the perimeter where the dimensions were asked for, or the reverse.**
   $59.3296$ is exactly four times $14.8324$, so the error is a factor of four.
   Both numbers are lengths in cm, so neither looks wrong on its own.

6. **Rounding the dimensions and breaking the constraint.**
   A $14.83$ by $14.83$ square has area $219.9289$, not $220$, and its perimeter $59.32$ is **below** the true minimum, which is impossible for a feasible rectangle.
   If you must round, round one side and recompute the other: $14.83$ by $220/14.83 = 14.8348$ has area exactly $220$ and perimeter $59.3296$.

7. **Using the perimeter to check the dimensions.**
   The objective is flat to second order at the minimum, so a width of $15$ is $1.13$ percent wrong and its perimeter is $0.0063$ percent wrong.
   The constraint does not catch it either, since $15 \cdot (220/15) = 220$ exactly.
   Only the derivative catches this one, which is worth knowing before you start looking for a cheap self-check.

8. **A sign slip in the derivative.**
   $P' = 2 + 440/w^2$ is positive at every width, so it reports no critical point at all and the problem appears to have no answer.
   The tell is the absence of a solution rather than a wrong one.

9. **Solving $w^2 = 440$ instead of $w^2 = 220$.**
   That gives $\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177$, a $41.42$ percent overshoot, with a perimeter of $62.928531$, which is $6.07$ percent high.
   Unlike the flat-width slips this one **is** visible in the reported value, so the AM-GM check catches it.

10. **Differentiating the constraint instead of the objective.**
    $\frac{d}{dw}(220) = 0$ says nothing, and $\frac{d}{dw}(wl) = 0$ produces a relation between $w$ and $l$ that is not the answer to anything.
    Write down which equation is which before differentiating anything.

11. **Reading $220$ as a length rather than an area.**
    It is printed as $220\text{ cm}^2$ and the exponent is the whole of the information.
    The units gate catches this one for free: $2w + 440$ adds a length to an area and can be discarded before any arithmetic.

12. **Simplifying $\sqrt{220}$ to $2\sqrt{110}$.**
    $220 = 4 \cdot 55$ and $55 = 5 \cdot 11$ is squarefree, so $2\sqrt{55}$ is the fully simplified form.
    $2\sqrt{110} \approx 20.976177$ is $41$ percent too big, and note it is the same $20.976177$ that turns up in traps 3 and 9.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
  a word problem asking for a smallest or largest something
                        |
        name the variables, draw it, label it
                        |
    which equation is GIVEN?  ->  that is the CONSTRAINT
    which quantity is ASKED?  ->  that is the OBJECTIVE
                        |
        check units: every term of the objective must match
                        |
      solve the constraint for one variable, substitute in
                        |
              STATE THE DOMAIN  <-- the fork
                        |
        +---------------+---------------+
        |                               |
   CLOSED [a,b]                    OPEN (0, inf)
        |                               |
  Q15.1's method:                 no endpoints to test
  critical points                       |
  + BOTH endpoints                 differentiate, solve
  compare the values               keep only feasible roots
        |                               |
        |                          JUSTIFY, one of:
        |                            P'' > 0 for ALL w   <- best
        |                            sign change - to +
        |                            P -> inf at both ends
        |                            AM-GM (only if exponents +-1)
        |                               |
        +---------------+---------------+
                        |
        recover the other variable from the constraint
                        |
      CHECK: multiply the dimensions back to the given area
      CHECK: are the two terms of aw + b/w equal?
                        |
      REPORT the DIMENSIONS, then the objective VALUE, separately
                        |
                     PREVIEW
```

The fork in the middle is the only decision in the tree, and it is decided by whether an interval was printed in the stem.
If one was, you are on Q15.1, Q15.3 or Q15.5.
If not, you are on Q15.2 or Q15.4, and the justification is the mark.
