# Q1.3 - Which equations define a function of x

**The question (MT2 Q1, 4 points).**
Determine which of the following equations are well defined functions with an independent variable $x$.
Explain.

- a. $\dfrac{2y}{x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert} = 1$
- b. $y^2 + x^2 = 10$

**The answers.**

$$\textbf{a.}\quad \textbf{Yes, a function.}\qquad
y = \frac{x^{2} - 3\lvert x \rvert}{2},
\qquad \text{domain } \mathbb{R}\setminus\{-3,\,0,\,3\}$$

$$\textbf{b.}\quad \textbf{No, not a function.}\qquad
y = \pm\sqrt{10 - x^{2}},
\qquad \text{e.g. } x=1 \text{ gives both } y=3 \text{ and } y=-3$$

scores is the sentence, not a keystroke.
If a box does ask for the solved form, type `(x^2-3*abs(x))/2`, and if it asks for the domain, type
the three exclusions `x <> 0`, `x <> 3`, `x <> -3` or the interval union in Section 7.

## 0. The 30-second version

If you only remember one thing, remember that this question is a **counting** question.

```
Fix an x.  Ask: how many real y satisfy the equation?

   0 y-values   ->  fine.  x is simply NOT IN THE DOMAIN.
   1 y-value    ->  fine.  this is what a function does.
   2 or more    ->  FATAL.  not a function of x.

a) 2y/(x^2-3|x|) = 1     solve for y   ->   y = (x^2-3|x|)/2
   y appears to power 1, coefficient 2/(x^2-3|x|)   ->  ONE y wherever it is defined
   denominator |x|(|x|-3) = 0 at x = 0, 3, -3       ->  those x give ZERO y
   VERDICT: function, domain R minus {0, 3, -3}

b) y^2 + x^2 = 10        solve for y   ->   y = +- sqrt(10 - x^2)
   x = 1  ->  y^2 = 9  ->  y = 3 AND y = -3          ->  TWO y
   VERDICT: not a function.  One witness is the whole proof.
```

Four lines, about thirty seconds, no calculator.

The one habit that carries the whole question: **do not confuse "no output" with "two outputs".**
Only the second one breaks a function.
Part (a) is on the paper precisely to see whether you know the difference.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is 4 of the 94 points on midterm 2, and it is the cheapest question on the paper in minutes per
mark.
It looks like a definition recital, but the examiner is checking five reflexes, and every one of them
reappears later in the same paper and again in the final.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| Solve an implicit equation for $y$ | Implicit differentiation, MT2 Q8, $x^3y+xy^2=4xy+7$, which only makes sense because $y$ is *assumed* to be a function of $x$ locally |
| A vanishing denominator removes an $x$ | Q1.2 composite domains, and the vertical asymptote of $\frac{x^2-3x-4}{x^2-16}$ in MT2 Q6 |
| $\lvert x \rvert$ splits into two cases at a corner | Q2.2, graphing $-3\lvert x-2\rvert$, and "not differentiable at $x=-2$" in MT2 Q10 |
| The $\pm$ that appears when you undo an even power | Every related-rates problem where you take a square root, and MT2 Q9's $\sqrt{9.2}$ |
| Vertical line test as a picture of the definition | MT2 Q10, sketch a single function meeting four conditions, where "single function" is the constraint |

The deeper point is the one the whole of calculus rests on.
Differentiation, limits and continuity are all statements about a *function*, so before any of them
apply you need to know that "$y$" is a single well determined number once $x$ is fixed.
Implicit differentiation in MT2 Q8 is the sharpest example: it differentiates $y$ as though it were a
function of $x$, and that step is only legitimate on a piece of the curve where the answer to this
question is yes.
The circle in part (b) is the standard illustration.
You cannot call $y$ a function of $x$ on the whole circle, but you can on the top half, and that is why
implicit differentiation of a circle still produces a meaningful $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

### 2.1 The definition, stated so it can be tested

> $y$ is a **function of $x$** if, for every $x$ in the domain, the equation determines **exactly one**
> value of $y$.

The phrase "well defined" in the question wording is not an extra condition.
It is the same condition, said with emphasis: the rule must *define* one output, with no ambiguity
about which one.

Two things this definition does **not** require, and both are tested here.

| It does NOT require | Why part (a) is still a function |
| :--- | :--- |
| That every real $x$ works | $x=0,\pm3$ produce no $y$ at all, so they are excluded from the domain and the question of uniqueness never arises for them |
| That the formula be one single algebraic expression | $y=(x^2-3\lvert x\rvert)/2$ is really two formulas glued at $x=0$, and a piecewise rule is a perfectly good function |

### 2.2 The count test, which is the entire question

For a fixed $x$, count the real solutions $y$.

| Count of $y$ at this $x$ | What it means | Effect on the verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $0$ | The equation has no solution here | $x$ is not in the domain. **Verdict unaffected.** |
| $1$ | Exactly one output | $x$ is in the domain. **This is what you want.** |
| $2$ or more, finite | Ambiguous output | **Not a function.** One such $x$ is enough. |
| Infinitely many | $y$ is unconstrained here | **Not a function.** |

Read that table as a single asymmetry: **zero is harmless, two is fatal.**
Students lose this mark in both directions, and the two mistakes are mirror images.

- Saying (a) is *not* a function because it blows up at $x=0$. That confuses the domain with
  well-definedness.
- Saying (b) *is* a function because you can write $y=\sqrt{10-x^2}$. That silently throws away the
  negative root, which the equation does not authorise you to do.

### 2.3 The degree of $y$ is a cue, not a proof

Look at the highest power of $y$ in the equation.
This is the fastest read available, and it is right most of the time, but it is a *cue* and you must
still confirm it.

| Highest power of $y$ | Usual verdict | The exception that makes it a cue and not a proof |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $1$ (linear in $y$) | Function | Where the coefficient of $y$ is zero, you get either no $y$ or every $y$ |
| Even ($2$, $4$, $\dots$) | Not a function | $y^2+x^2=0$ has the single solution $y=0$, so it is a function on the one-point domain $\{0\}$ |
| Odd ($3$, $5$, $\dots$) | Function | $y^3-3y=x$ is odd degree and **not** a function, because $y^3-3y$ is not monotone |

The honest version of the rule is about **monotonicity in $y$**, not degree.
If the left side is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing as a function of $y$, it crosses any
given level exactly once, so there is exactly one $y$.
Odd powers happen to be monotone; $y^3-3y$ is not, which is why the cue fails there.
This is the content of Family 3 versus Family 5 in Section 8.

### 2.4 The three ways uniqueness actually dies

Every "not a function" instance on this course is one of three shapes.
Learn the shape and you stop needing to think.

| Shape | The mechanism | Canonical instance |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Even power of $y$ | Undoing $y^{2n}$ introduces $\pm$ | $y^2+x^2=10$ (part b) |
| $\lvert y \rvert$ | $\lvert y \rvert = c$ with $c>0$ has the two solutions $\pm c$ | $\lvert y \rvert = x$ |
| Periodic function of $y$ | $\sin y = c$ repeats forever | $\sin y = x$ |

There is a fourth, rarer shape worth one line: the **coefficient of $y$ collapses**.
In $xy=0$, the value $x=0$ leaves $0\cdot y=0$, which every $y$ satisfies.
That is infinitely many outputs, so it is not a function, and it is the exact opposite failure from
part (a), where the collapsing coefficient left *no* $y$.
Family 10 in Section 8 is built around this pair.

### 2.5 Where the $\lvert x \rvert$ in part (a) fits

It does nothing to the verdict.
An absolute value, an even power or any other decoration **on the $x$ side** cannot threaten
uniqueness, because uniqueness is a question about $y$ only.
Its only job is to shape the domain, and here it does that in a way worth noticing:

$$x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert = \lvert x \rvert^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert = \lvert x \rvert\left(\lvert x \rvert - 3\right)$$

using $x^2=\lvert x\rvert^2$.
That factorisation is the fastest route to the three excluded values, and it is worth doing rather than
splitting into cases: the product is zero exactly when $\lvert x\rvert=0$ or $\lvert x\rvert=3$, which
is $x\in\{0,3,-3\}$.
The $\lvert x \rvert$ is a decoy, and recognising a decoy quickly is worth as much as any technique.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Isolate $y$ as far as the algebra allows.** Multiply out denominators, but write down what you
>    multiplied by, because that is where the domain lives.
> 2. **Count the $y$-values for a general $x$.** Highest power of $y$ is the cue; monotonicity in $y$
>    is the proof.
> 3. **If the count is ever 2 or more, stop.** Produce one specific $x$ with two specific $y$-values
>    and you are done. That single witness is a complete proof.
> 4. **If the count is always 1, say so, then find where it is 0.** Set every denominator to zero, every
>    even radicand to negative, every log argument to non-positive. Those $x$ leave the domain.
> 5. **State the verdict, the reason, and the domain.** The word *Explain* is in the question.

Classify before you compute:

| What you see | Where it goes | First move |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y$ alone on one side already | Family 0 | Verdict is yes. Go straight to the domain. |
| $y$ times something, or $y$ over something | Family 1 | Divide, then set the coefficient to zero |
| $y^2$, $y^4$, or a hidden quadratic in $y$ | Family 2 | Find one $x$ with two $y$. Done. |
| $y^3$, $y^5$, alone | Family 3 | Verdict is yes, unique real odd root |
| $y$ in two terms of different degree | Family 4 or 5 | Differentiate the $y$-side with respect to $y$ and check the sign |
| $\lvert y \rvert$ | Family 6 | Verdict is no, unless the right side is forced to $0$ |
| $\sqrt{y}$ or $\sqrt[4]{y}$ | Family 7 | Verdict is yes. The domain needs the other side $\geq 0$. |
| $e^y$, $\ln y$, $\sin y$, $\tan y$ | Family 8 | Exp and log are yes. Trig is no. |
| $\lvert x \rvert$, $x^2$ on the $x$ side only | Family 9 | Ignore for the verdict, use for the domain |
| No $y$ at all, or a sum of squares $=0$ | Family 10 | Count solutions at the special $x$ by hand |
| A graph or a table | Family 11 | Vertical line test, or look for a repeated $x$ |
| A piecewise rule | Family 12 | Check the seam values agree |
| "Is $x$ a function of $y$?" | Family 13 | Swap the letters and rerun the procedure |

## 4. Q1.3 worked four ways

### Route A - solve for $y$, then hunt the domain (use this one)

**Part (a).**

Start from

$$\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert} = 1.$$

The equation only says anything at all when the denominator is nonzero, so record that condition first,
before touching the algebra:

$$x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert \neq 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lvert x \rvert\left(\lvert x \rvert - 3\right) \neq 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x \neq 0,\ x \neq 3,\ x \neq -3.$$

Now multiply both sides by that nonzero denominator and divide by $2$:

$$2y = x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
y = \frac{x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert}{2}.$$

The right side is a single number for each admissible $x$.
So there is exactly one $y$, and the answer is **yes, a well defined function of $x$**, with

$$\text{domain } = (-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty).$$

**Why the domain remark is not optional, and why it does not change the verdict.**

Look at what happens at $x=0$ in the original equation:

$$\frac{2y}{0} = 1,$$

which no $y$ satisfies, because the left side is not even defined.
So $x=0$ contributes *zero* outputs, not two, and by the count test in Section 2.2 the verdict stands.
The same happens at $x=3$ and $x=-3$.

Here is the subtlety that makes this a 4-point question rather than a 1-point one.
The *cleared* equation

$$2y = x^2 - 3\lvert x \rvert$$

is **not the same relation** as the original.
At $x=0$ the cleared version says $2y=0$, which has the perfectly good unique solution $y=0$, whereas
the original says nothing at all.
Multiplying through by a denominator added three points to the graph that were not there before.
Both relations are functions, but they have different domains, and the one the question asked about is
the one with the three holes.
Writing the domain down is how you show the marker you noticed.

**Sanity checks on the formula.**
$y$ is an even function, since both $x^2$ and $\lvert x\rvert$ are even, so $y(-x)=y(x)$.
Spot values: $y(1)=\frac{1-3}{2}=-1$, $y(2)=\frac{4-6}{2}=-1$, $y(4)=\frac{16-12}{2}=2$ and
$y(5)=\frac{25-15}{2}=5$.
Substituting $x=4,y=2$ into the original gives $\frac{2\cdot 2}{16-12}=\frac{4}{4}=1$, so the solved
form really does satisfy the equation it came from.

If you want the piecewise form:

$$y = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^2-3x}{2}, & x>0,\ x\neq 3,\\[6pt]
\dfrac{x^2+3x}{2}, & x<0,\ x\neq -3.
\end{cases}$$

You are not asked for it, but it is what the graph looks like: two upward parabola pieces, mirror images
across the $y$-axis, each with a hole punched at its own zero.

**Part (b).**

$$y^2 + x^2 = 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y^2 = 10 - x^2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = \pm\sqrt{10-x^2}.$$

The $\pm$ is the answer.
For any $x$ with $x^2<10$, both roots are real and they are different, so one input produces two
outputs and $y$ is **not a function of $x$**.

Do not stop at the $\pm$.
Name a witness, because that is what "Explain" is asking for:

$$x=1: \quad y^2 = 10-1 = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=3 \ \text{ and } \ y=-3,$$

and both $(1,3)$ and $(1,-3)$ satisfy $y^2+x^2=10$.
One input, two outputs, so not a function.
$x=3$ works just as nicely and gives $y=\pm1$.

Two remarks that show you understand the boundary, worth including if you have room.
At $x=\pm\sqrt{10}$ there is exactly one $y$, namely $0$, and for $\lvert x\rvert>\sqrt{10}$ there is no
real $y$ at all.
Neither rescues the relation, because a single bad $x$ is already fatal.

### Route B - the degree-in-$y$ reflex (the eight-second read)

Do not solve anything.
Just look at the power of $y$.

| | Power of $y$ | Read | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| (a) | $1$ | Linear in $y$, so at most one $y$ | Function |
| (b) | $2$ | Even power, so $\pm$ | Not a function |

This is the read to use when you are short of time, and it gets the verdict right on both parts.
Its weakness is that it gets you the verdict and nothing else: no domain for (a), no witness for (b),
so on a question that says *Explain* it is a starting point rather than an answer.
Use it to decide the verdict in eight seconds, then spend the remaining minute writing Route A's
justification.

### Route C - the witness and uniqueness arguments (this is what earns the marks)

Strip the question to what a proof actually requires, and it is asymmetric.

**To prove "not a function", exhibit one $x$ and two $y$.**
That is a complete proof and it is short.
For (b): $(1,3)$ and $(1,-3)$ both satisfy the equation.
Nothing else is needed, and in particular you do not need to discuss the domain, the boundary points,
or the shape of the curve.

**To prove "is a function", you must argue for *every* $x$.**
A witness cannot help you, because no finite list of good $x$ rules out a bad one.
For (a) the argument is the algebra itself: once the denominator is known nonzero, the equation is
equivalent to $y=(x^2-3\lvert x\rvert)/2$, and the right side is a single number.
The phrase to use is **"the equation is linear in $y$ with nonzero coefficient, so it has exactly one
solution"**.

This asymmetry is the single most useful thing in this section, because it tells you how much to write.
A "no" answer is one line and a pair of points.
A "yes" answer is a chain of equivalences plus the domain.
Budget your two minutes accordingly.

### Route D - geometric recognition

Recognise the curve and read the answer off the picture.

For (b), $y^2+x^2=10$ is a **circle** centred at the origin of radius $\sqrt{10}\approx3.162$.
The vertical line $x=1$ cuts it twice, so it fails the vertical line test.
This is the fastest route on part (b) if you recognise the conic instantly, and it is also the most
persuasive thing to say in one sentence: *"the graph is a circle, and a vertical line through its
interior meets it twice."*

For (a), the recognition is less standard but still available.
$y=\frac{\lvert x\rvert^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$ is the parabola $\frac{t^2-3t}{2}$ in the variable
$t=\lvert x\rvert\geq0$, reflected to be even in $x$, with holes at its roots.
Any such graph passes the vertical line test by construction, because it was built as "one $y$ per
$x$".

The limitation of Route D is that it only works on curves you recognise.
$y^3-3y=x$ has no name you know, and the vertical line test on a curve you cannot sketch is not a test
at all.

### Which route, and why

| Route | Time on this question | Gets the verdict | Gets the domain | Gets the explanation | Fails when |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A. Solve for $y$ | 90 s | Yes | Yes | Yes | $y$ cannot be isolated, e.g. $y^3+y=x$ |
| B. Degree in $y$ | 8 s | Yes | No | No | Non-monotone odd degree, e.g. $y^3-3y=x$ |
| C. Witness / uniqueness | 40 s | Yes | Only if you add it | Yes, this *is* the explanation | Never, but you need Route B first to know which side to argue |
| D. Geometric | 15 s | Yes, if you know the curve | No | One good sentence | Unfamiliar curves |

**Use Route B to pick the side within seconds, then write it up as Route A for (a) and Route C for
(b).**
That is the combination that gets all four marks: (a) needs the equivalence chain and the domain, (b)
needs one pair of points.
Route D is a free extra sentence on (b), and a sentence that names the curve reads as understanding
rather than manipulation.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

This is what you actually put on the page.
It is short on purpose.
Four points, two minutes.

> **a.** Yes, this is a well defined function of $x$.
>
> The equation requires $x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\neq0$.
> Since $x^2=\lvert x\rvert^2$, we have $x^2-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert(\lvert x\rvert-3)$, which is
> zero exactly when $x=0$, $x=3$ or $x=-3$.
> For every other $x$ we may multiply by the denominator to get $2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert$, so
>
> $$y=\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}.$$
>
> The equation is linear in $y$ with nonzero coefficient, so each admissible $x$ determines exactly one
> $y$.
> Hence $y$ is a function of $x$ with domain
> $(-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)$.
> The three excluded values give no $y$ at all, which removes them from the domain but does not affect
> well-definedness.
>
> **b.** No, this is not a well defined function of $x$.
>
> Solving gives $y^2=10-x^2$, so $y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^2}$, and the two signs give different values whenever
> $x^2<10$.
> For example $x=1$ gives $y^2=9$, so both $y=3$ and $y=-3$ satisfy the equation.
> The single input $x=1$ produces two different outputs, so $y$ is not a function of $x$.
> Geometrically the graph is the circle of radius $\sqrt{10}$ centred at the origin, and the vertical
> line $x=1$ meets it twice, so it fails the vertical line test.

Three sentences in that write-up are the ones carrying the marks, and it is worth knowing which.

| Sentence | Why it scores |
| :--- | :--- |
| "linear in $y$ with nonzero coefficient, so exactly one $y$" | This is the *reason* for (a), not the computation. Without it you have shown a formula, not argued uniqueness. |
| "both $y=3$ and $y=-3$ satisfy the equation" | This is the *whole proof* for (b). A named witness beats any amount of talk about $\pm$. |
| "the excluded values give no $y$, which does not affect well-definedness" | This is the sentence that shows you know zero and two are different failures. It is the difference between a complete and a partial answer on (a). |

## 6. Verify before you commit

Cheap checks, in the order they catch mistakes.

**1. Substitute the witness back.**
For (b), put $(1,3)$ and $(1,-3)$ into $y^2+x^2$ and confirm both give $10$.
$9+1=10$ and $9+1=10$.
If a witness does not check out, the whole "not a function" answer collapses, and this takes five
seconds.

**2. Substitute the solved formula back.**
For (a), put $x=4$ and $y=2$ into $\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}$: $\frac{4}{16-12}=1$.
This catches a dropped factor of $2$, which is the most common algebra slip here.
Test a negative $x$ too, since the $\lvert x\rvert$ is exactly where sign errors live: $x=-4$ gives
$\frac{2\cdot 2}{16-12}=1$.

**3. Count the excluded values.**
$\lvert x\rvert(\lvert x\rvert-3)=0$ should give **three** values, not two.
$\lvert x\rvert=3$ means $x=\pm3$, and forgetting the negative one is the standard error.
Three exclusions, and they are symmetric about zero because the expression is even.

**4. Check the parity.**
Anything built only from $x^2$ and $\lvert x\rvert$ is even, so the domain and the graph must be
symmetric about the $y$-axis.
If your excluded set is not symmetric, you made an error.

**5. Magnitude check on the circle.**
$\sqrt{10}$ is between $3$ and $3.5$, so a witness at $x=1$ or $x=3$ is safely inside and will have two
real roots.
A witness at $x=4$ would have none, which would prove nothing.
Pick your witness well inside the curve.

Numbers worth memorising for this question:

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Excluded $x$ in part (a) | $0,\ 3,\ -3$ |
| $y$ at $x=1,2,4,5$ in part (a) | $-1,\ -1,\ 2,\ 5$ |
| Circle radius in part (b) | $\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623$ |
| Cleanest witnesses for (b) | $x=1\Rightarrow y=\pm3$, and $x=3\Rightarrow y=\pm1$ |

## 7. Answer-entry rules

**What kind of Möbius item this is.**
The paper says *Explain*, and 4 points split across two parts, so on a machine-marked version this
almost certainly appears as a verdict widget plus a free-text or file-upload explanation, or as a
"select all that are functions" checkbox item.
That means the expensive failure mode here is **not** a parser problem.
It is answering the verdict and leaving the explanation blank.

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The verdict | Answer both parts explicitly | Answer only the interesting one |
| "Select all that apply" | Tick (a), leave (b) unticked | Tick neither because you are unsure |
| A free-text explanation | Name the witness for (b) and the uniqueness reason for (a) | Write "vertical line test" with nothing else |
| If a box asks for the solved form | `(x^2-3*abs(x))/2` | `x^2-3*abs(x)/2`, which divides only the last term |
| If a box asks for a $y$-value | `-1` at $x=1$ | `1`, a sign slip from $\frac{1-3}{2}$ |

**The single most expensive typo on this question** is `x^2-3*abs(x)/2`, which parses as
$x^2-\frac{3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$ rather than $\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$.
Bracket the whole numerator before dividing.

Absolute value in Möbius is `abs(x)`.
The vertical-bar notation `|x|` is not a reliable input in either grading engine, so do not use it in an
answer box even though it is standard on paper.

**The domain, if it is asked for.**

**This part is not fully verified and you should settle it on the sample exam, not in the exam.**
My platform notes cover expression entry thoroughly but do not document Möbius interval-notation syntax.
Treat the following as a strategy rather than a guaranteed string.

Three exclusions means the interval answer has **four** pieces:

```
(-infinity,-3)U(-3,0)U(0,3)U(3,infinity)
```

If the box takes an inequality instead, the three conditions are `x <> 0`, `x <> 3`, `x <> -3`, where
some engines want `!=` instead of `<>`.
Write exact values, never decimals.

**Always click the preview icon.**
An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics.
Preview is the only proof that the parser agrees with you; reading your own answer back is not.
On a four-piece union this matters more than usual, because a mis-parsed union still looks tidy in the
input box.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
The families below are organised by **the tool the variation forces**, not by how the question looks.
Two equations that look nothing alike but force the same tool are the same question.

**Read each table as structure, not as a list of facts.**
Across all fourteen families there are only **three** reasons a relation fails to be a function, and
you already have them from Section 2.4: an even power of $y$, an $\lvert y\rvert$, or a periodic
function of $y$.
Everything else on the "not a function" side of these tables is one of those three wearing a costume,
plus the one structural failure where the coefficient of $y$ collapses to zero.
On the "is a function" side there is only **one** reason: the equation pins $y$ down uniquely, either by
being linear in $y$ or by being monotone in $y$.

Every claim below was checked by numerically counting the real $y$ that solve the equation at the stated
$x$, so the "how many $y$" column is measured rather than asserted.

### Family 0 - already solved for $y$: the verdict is free, only the domain is work

**Trigger.** The equation reads $y = \text{something in } x$, with no $y$ anywhere else.

**Tool.** None for the verdict. It is automatically a function, because the right side is one number.
All the work is the domain, and it is exactly the three restriction triggers from Q1.2: a denominator
cannot be zero, an even radicand cannot be negative, a log argument must be positive.

**Worked shape.** $y=\frac{1}{x^2-4}$. Verdict: function. Denominator zero at $x=\pm2$. Domain
$\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm2\}$.

| Equation | Verdict | How many $y$ per admissible $x$ | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y=\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$ | Function | 1 | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $y=\sqrt{x-1}$ | Function | 1 | $[1,\infty)$ |
| $y=\frac{1}{x^2-4}$ | Function | 1 | $x\neq\pm2$ |
| $y=\ln x$ | Function | 1 | $(0,\infty)$ |
| $y=\frac{x}{x^2+1}$ | Function | 1 | $\mathbb{R}$ |

Note the contrast between rows 3 and 5: $x^2-4$ vanishes, $x^2+1$ never does.
A denominator only costs you a domain if it actually reaches zero.

### Family 1 - linear in $y$: $A(x)\,y = B(x)$

**Trigger.** $y$ appears to the first power only, possibly multiplied or divided by stuff in $x$.
**This is part (a).**

**Tool.** Divide by the coefficient, then ask where the coefficient is zero.
That last step is the whole family, and it splits into two cases you must distinguish:

$$A(x)=0 \ \text{ and } \ B(x)\neq0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \textbf{no } y \ \text{(x leaves the domain, verdict survives)}$$
$$A(x)=0 \ \text{ and } \ B(x)=0 \ \Longrightarrow\ \textbf{every } y \ \text{(verdict dies)}$$

**Worked shape.** $\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}=1$ is $A(x)y=B(x)$ with
$A=\frac{2}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}$ and $B=1$.
Where the denominator dies, $A$ is undefined and $B=1\neq0$, so there is no $y$.
Function, with holes.

| Equation | Solved form | Verdict | Excluded $x$, and why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}=1$ | $y=\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$ | Function | $0,\pm3$: no $y$ |
| $xy=1$ | $y=\frac1x$ | Function | $0$: no $y$ |
| $(x-2)y=x+1$ | $y=\frac{x+1}{x-2}$ | Function | $2$: no $y$ |
| $\frac{3y}{x^2-4}=1$ | $y=\frac{x^2-4}{3}$ | Function | $\pm2$: no $y$ |
| $y\sin x=1$ | $y=\csc x$ | Function | $x=k\pi$: no $y$ |
| $x^2y+y=x$ | $y=\frac{x}{x^2+1}$ | Function | none, $x^2+1$ never zero |
| $\frac{5y}{x^2-2x}=1$ | $y=\frac{x^2-2x}{5}$ | Function | $0,2$: no $y$ |
| $\lvert x\rvert y=4$ | $y=\frac{4}{\lvert x\rvert}$ | Function | $0$: no $y$ |
| $\frac{2y}{x^3-4x}=1$ | $y=\frac{x^3-4x}{2}$ | Function | $0,\pm2$: no $y$ |
| $\frac{y}{x^2+1}=3$ | $y=3x^2+3$ | Function | none |
| $\frac{2y}{x^2-5\lvert x\rvert}=1$ | $y=\frac{x^2-5\lvert x\rvert}{2}$ | Function | $0,\pm5$: no $y$ |
| $\frac{3y}{\lvert x\rvert-2}=1$ | $y=\frac{\lvert x\rvert-2}{3}$ | Function | $\pm2$: no $y$ |

**Every row is a function.** That is the structural fact about this family, and it is why part (a) is
the *easy* half of Q1.3 once you see the shape.
The variation between rows is entirely in the third column, and the reworded exam question will change
the $3$ to a $5$ or the $x^2$ to an $x^3$ and change nothing else.
Row 11 is the most likely reword of the actual exam question: same structure, exclusions at $0,\pm5$.

### Family 2 - an even power of $y$: the $\pm$ kills it

**Trigger.** $y^2$, $y^4$, or any quadratic in $y$, including a disguised one like $y^2+y$.
**This is part (b).**

**Tool.** Find one $x$ that makes the right side a positive number, then take both roots.
Give the two $y$-values explicitly.
Choose an $x$ that makes the arithmetic clean, which usually means making the right side a perfect
square.

**Worked shape.** $y^2+x^2=10$ at $x=1$ gives $y^2=9$, so $y=\pm3$. Not a function.

| Equation | Witness $x$ | The two $y$ | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^2+x^2=10$ | $1$ | $\pm3$ | Not a function |
| $y^2+x^2=10$ | $3$ | $\pm1$ | Not a function |
| $y^2=x$ | $4$ | $\pm2$ | Not a function |
| $y^2=x^3$ | $4$ | $\pm8$ | Not a function |
| $\frac{x^2}{9}+\frac{y^2}{4}=1$ | $0$ | $\pm2$ | Not a function |
| $y^4=x^2$ | $4$ | $\pm2$ | Not a function |
| $x^2-y^2=9$ | $5$ | $\pm4$ | Not a function |
| $y^2=(x-1)^2$ | $4$ | $\pm3$ | Not a function |
| $y^2+4x^2=16$ | $1$ | $\pm2\sqrt3$ | Not a function |
| $3y^2=x$ | $3$ | $\pm1$ | Not a function |
| $y^2+y=x$ | $6$ | $2$ and $-3$ | Not a function |
| $y^4=x+1$ | $15$ | $\pm2$ | Not a function |
| $xy^2=1$ | $4$ | $\pm\frac12$ | Not a function |

**Every row is not a function**, and twelve of the thirteen have witnesses of the symmetric form
$\pm c$.
Row 11 is the one to study, because the $\pm$ is hidden: $y^2+y=x$ is a quadratic in $y$, so the
quadratic formula gives $y=\frac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4x}}{2}$, and the two roots are not negatives of each
other.
At $x=6$ they are $2$ and $-3$.
If you only look for the pattern "$y^2=\text{something}$" you will miss this row.
The reliable trigger is **"the highest power of $y$ is even"**, not "$y^2$ sits alone".

Row 8 is worth one line too: $y^2=(x-1)^2$ gives $y=\pm(x-1)$, two straight lines crossing at $x=1$.
At the crossing point there is only one $y$, and everywhere else there are two, so it is still not a
function.
One good $x$ does not save a relation, just as one bad $x$ condemns it.

### Family 3 - an odd power of $y$: still a function

**Trigger.** $y^3$, $y^5$, $y^7$ appearing alone.

**Tool.** Every real number has exactly one real odd root.
$y^3=c$ has the single real solution $y=\sqrt[3]{c}$, for negative $c$ as well as positive.
There is no $\pm$, and there is no domain restriction either, because odd roots accept negatives.

**Worked shape.** $y^3=x$ gives $y=x^{1/3}$, one $y$ for every real $x$. Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$.

| Equation | Solved form | Verdict | Spot check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^3=x$ | $y=x^{1/3}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=-8\Rightarrow y=-2$ |
| $y^3+x=1$ | $y=(1-x)^{1/3}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=-7\Rightarrow y=2$ |
| $y^3=x^2$ | $y=x^{2/3}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=8\Rightarrow y=4$ |
| $y^5=x-2$ | $y=(x-2)^{1/5}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=34\Rightarrow y=2$ |
| $y^3=\lvert x\rvert$ | $y=\lvert x\rvert^{1/3}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=-27\Rightarrow y=3$ |
| $2y^3=x$ | $y=(x/2)^{1/3}$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=16\Rightarrow y=2$ |

**Every row is a function with domain all of $\mathbb{R}$.**
This family exists to be contrasted with Family 2, and the pair is the reason the degree-in-$y$ cue in
Section 2.3 works: even means $\pm$, odd means unique.
The examiner tests the contrast by putting a $y^2$ question and a $y^3$ question side by side.

### Family 4 - monotone in $y$ with no closed form: a function you cannot solve for

**Trigger.** $y$ appears in two or more terms of different kinds, and no algebra isolates it.

**Tool.** Treat the $y$-side as a function of $y$ and differentiate **with respect to $y$**.
If the derivative is always positive, the $y$-side is strictly increasing, so it hits each level exactly
once, so there is exactly one $y$ for each $x$.
This proves the verdict without producing any formula at all.

**Worked shape.** $y^3+y=x$.
Then $\frac{d}{dy}(y^3+y)=3y^2+1\geq1>0$, so $y^3+y$ is strictly increasing from $-\infty$ to
$+\infty$.
Each $x$ is hit exactly once.
Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$, and there is no elementary formula for $y$.

| Equation | Derivative in $y$ | Verdict | Spot check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^3+y=x$ | $3y^2+1>0$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=2\Rightarrow y=1$; $x=10\Rightarrow y=2$; $x=30\Rightarrow y=3$ |
| $y+e^y=x$ | $1+e^y>0$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=1\Rightarrow y=0$ |
| $y^5+3y=x$ | $5y^4+3>0$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=4\Rightarrow y=1$ |
| $2y+\sin y=x$ | $2+\cos y\geq1>0$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=0\Rightarrow y=0$ |
| $y^3+2y-5=x$ | $3y^2+2>0$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | $x=-2\Rightarrow y=1$ |

**Every row is a function**, and none of them can be solved for $y$ by hand.
That is the entire point of the family: **"I cannot solve for $y$" is not evidence that it is not a
function.**
Row 4 is the prettiest, because $\sin y$ looks like it should cause the periodicity problem from
Family 8, and the $2y$ term defeats it: $2+\cos y$ can drop to $1$ but never to $0$, so monotonicity
survives.

This is also the family that connects to the rest of the course.
The reason implicit differentiation works at all is that the curve is locally of this type, and
$\frac{dy}{dx}$ exists even where no formula for $y$ does.

### Family 5 - odd degree but non-monotone: the cue fails

**Trigger.** An odd power of $y$ **plus** a lower $y$ term with the opposite sign, like $y^3-3y$.

**Tool.** Differentiate in $y$ and find that the derivative changes sign.
Then the $y$-side has a local maximum and a local minimum, and every level strictly between those two
values is hit three times.
Pick such a level and you have three $y$-values.

**Worked shape.** $y^3-3y=x$.
$\frac{d}{dy}(y^3-3y)=3y^2-3=0$ at $y=\pm1$, giving a local max of $2$ at $y=-1$ and a local min of
$-2$ at $y=1$.
So for any $x$ with $-2<x<2$ there are three real $y$.
At $x=0$: $y^3-3y=0$ gives $y(y^2-3)=0$, so $y=0,\sqrt3,-\sqrt3$.
Three outputs, not a function.

| Equation | Witness $x$ | The $y$-values | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^3-3y=x$ | $0$ | $0,\ \pm\sqrt3$ | Not a function |
| $y^3-3y=x$ | $2$ | $2$, and $-1$ twice | Not a function |
| $y^3-y=x$ | $0$ | $0,\ \pm1$ | Not a function |
| $y^4-y^2=x$ | $0$ | $0,\ \pm1$ | Not a function |
| $y^3-12y=x$ | $0$ | $0,\ \pm2\sqrt3$ | Not a function |

**Every row is not a function, and every row has $x=0$ as its witness.**
That is not a coincidence: these left sides all factor with $y$ as a factor when the constant term is
absent, so $x=0$ is always the cheapest witness to check.
Reach for $x=0$ first in this family.

The second row is the interesting one and worth understanding rather than memorising.
At $x=2$ the equation is $y^3-3y-2=0$, which factors as $(y-2)(y+1)^2=0$.
So $y=2$ and $y=-1$, where $-1$ is a repeated root.
There are only **two distinct** outputs there, not three, because $x=2$ is exactly the local maximum
value where two of the three roots have merged.
Two is still fatal, so the verdict is unchanged, but this row shows that the count varies with $x$ and
that the boundary values of $x$ are where it changes.

Families 3 and 5 together are why Section 2.3 insists the degree is a cue and not a proof.
$y^3=x$ and $y^3-3y=x$ have the same degree in $y$ and opposite verdicts.

### Family 6 - $\lvert y\rvert$ in the equation

**Trigger.** An absolute value wrapped around $y$, or around an expression containing $y$.

**Tool.** $\lvert u\rvert=c$ has two solutions when $c>0$, one when $c=0$, none when $c<0$.
So find an $x$ making the other side strictly positive and read off the two.

**Worked shape.** $\lvert y\rvert=x$ at $x=5$ gives $y=\pm5$. Not a function.

| Equation | Witness $x$ | The $y$ | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lvert y\rvert=x$ | $5$ | $\pm5$ | Not a function |
| $\lvert y\rvert+x=3$ | $2$ | $\pm1$ | Not a function |
| $\lvert y\rvert=x^2+1$ | $0$ | $\pm1$ | Not a function |
| $\lvert y\rvert=\lvert x\rvert$ | $7$ | $\pm7$ | Not a function |
| $\lvert y-1\rvert=x$ | $4$ | $5$ and $-3$ | Not a function |
| $y\lvert y\rvert=x$ | any | one $y$ | **Function**, domain $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\lvert y\rvert=0$ | any | $y=0$ only | **Function**, domain $\mathbb{R}$ |

The last two rows are the ones that make this a family rather than a rule.
$y\lvert y\rvert$ equals $y^2$ for $y\geq0$ and $-y^2$ for $y<0$, so it is strictly increasing across
all of $\mathbb{R}$, and Family 4's monotonicity argument applies: $x=9$ gives $y=3$ and $x=-9$ gives
$y=-3$, uniquely.
And $\lvert y\rvert=0$ forces $y=0$, the one-solution case of the tool.
So $\lvert y\rvert$ is a strong signal, not a certainty.

Row 5 also earns a look, because the two solutions are not $\pm c$.
$\lvert y-1\rvert=4$ means $y-1=\pm4$, so $y=5$ or $y=-3$, symmetric about $y=1$ rather than about
$0$.
Solve for the bracket, not for $y$.

### Family 7 - an even root of $y$: unique, but watch the other side

**Trigger.** $\sqrt{y}$, $\sqrt[4]{y}$, or $y^{1/2}$ somewhere in the equation.

**Tool.** Squaring is safe *in this direction*.
$\sqrt{y}=E$ forces $y=E^2$, one value, no $\pm$, because $\sqrt{\ }$ denotes the non-negative root.
The catch is the domain: $\sqrt{y}$ is never negative, so any $x$ making $E<0$ gives no solution at all.

**Worked shape.** $\sqrt y=x-1$ gives $y=(x-1)^2$, but only for $x\geq1$.
For $x<1$ the right side is negative and no $y$ works.
Function, domain $[1,\infty)$.

| Equation | Solved form | Verdict | Domain, and why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt y=x$ | $y=x^2$ | Function | $[0,\infty)$: need $x\geq0$ |
| $\sqrt y=x-1$ | $y=(x-1)^2$ | Function | $[1,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt y=x^2+1$ | $y=(x^2+1)^2$ | Function | $\mathbb{R}$: right side always positive |
| $\sqrt[4]y=x$ | $y=x^4$ | Function | $[0,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt y+\sqrt x=3$ | $y=(3-\sqrt x)^2$ | Function | $[0,9]$: need $x\geq0$ and $\sqrt x\leq3$ |
| $\sqrt{y+1}=x$ | $y=x^2-1$ | Function | $[0,\infty)$ |

**Every row is a function**, which is the surprise of this family: a square root *of $y$* is safe, while
a square *of $y$* is fatal.
The asymmetry is worth stating in one sentence, because it is the single most confusable pair on this
topic.

$$y^2=E \ \Longrightarrow\ y=\pm\sqrt E \quad \textbf{two } y \qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\sqrt y = E \ \Longrightarrow\ y=E^2 \quad \textbf{one } y$$

Row 5 is the one to practise, because the domain is a genuine two-sided interval.
$\sqrt y=3-\sqrt x$ needs $x\geq0$ for the $\sqrt x$ and $3-\sqrt x\geq0$ for the left side, so
$0\leq x\leq9$.
Spot values: $x=1\Rightarrow y=4$, $x=4\Rightarrow y=1$, $x=9\Rightarrow y=0$.

### Family 8 - transcendental in $y$: exp and log are safe, trig is not

**Trigger.** $e^y$, $\ln y$, $2^y$, $\sin y$, $\tan y$, $\cos y$.

**Tool.** Ask whether the function of $y$ is one-to-one.
$e^y$ and $\ln y$ are strictly increasing, so they are.
$\sin$, $\cos$ and $\tan$ are periodic, so they are emphatically not, and the failure is infinite
rather than merely double.

**Worked shape.** $\sin y=x$ at $x=\frac12$ has $y=\frac\pi6$, $y=\frac{5\pi}6$, and then every one of
those plus $2k\pi$. Infinitely many outputs, not a function.

| Equation | Verdict | Detail |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $e^y=x$ | Function | $y=\ln x$, domain $(0,\infty)$; $e^y>0$ always, so $x\leq0$ gives no $y$ |
| $\ln y=x$ | Function | $y=e^x$, domain $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $2^y=x$ | Function | $y=\log_2 x$, domain $(0,\infty)$; $x=64\Rightarrow y=6$ |
| $e^y=x^2$ | Function | $y=2\ln\lvert x\rvert$, domain $x\neq0$ |
| $\sin y=x$ | Not a function | $x=\frac12\Rightarrow y=\frac\pi6,\frac{5\pi}6,\frac\pi6+2\pi,\dots$ |
| $\sin y=x$ | Not a function | $x=0\Rightarrow y=0,\pi,-\pi,2\pi,\dots$ |
| $\tan y=x$ | Not a function | $x=0\Rightarrow y=0,\pi,-\pi,\dots$ |
| $\cos y=x$ | Not a function | $x=0\Rightarrow y=\pm\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2,\dots$ |
| $y+e^y=x$ | Function | Family 4, monotone, no closed form |

The split is clean: **exponential and logarithmic in $y$ are functions, trigonometric in $y$ are not.**
Row 4 is the one that rewards care.
$e^y=x^2$ has the solution $y=\ln(x^2)=2\ln\lvert x\rvert$, and the absolute value is compulsory,
because $x^2$ is positive for negative $x$ too.
Writing $2\ln x$ would wrongly shrink the domain to $x>0$.
The domain is everything except $x=0$, where $x^2=0$ and $e^y=0$ has no solution.

For the trig rows, a one-line justification is enough and better than a list:
*"$\sin y = \sin(\pi-y)$, so any solution $y$ comes with a second solution $\pi-y$."*

### Family 9 - decorations on the $x$ side: irrelevant to the verdict

**Trigger.** $\lvert x\rvert$, $x^2$, $\sqrt x$, $\sin x$ appearing in the equation, but only ever
attached to $x$.
**Part (a)'s $\lvert x\rvert$ lives here.**

**Tool.** Ignore them entirely when deciding the verdict, because the verdict is about counting $y$.
Use them only to build the domain.

**Worked shape.** $y(\lvert x\rvert-2)=1$ is Family 1 with $A(x)=\lvert x\rvert-2$.
Function.
$A=0$ at $\lvert x\rvert=2$, so exclude $x=\pm2$, and note that an $\lvert x\rvert$ or an $x^2$ in the
coefficient produces exclusions **in symmetric pairs**.

| Equation | Verdict | Domain | Symmetry note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y=\lvert x\rvert$ | Function | $\mathbb{R}$ | even |
| $\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}=1$ | Function | $x\neq0,\pm3$ | even, exclusions paired |
| $y(\lvert x\rvert-2)=1$ | Function | $x\neq\pm2$ | even, exclusions paired |
| $y=\frac{\lvert x\rvert}{x}$ | Function | $x\neq0$ | odd, the sign function |
| $\frac{2y}{\lvert x\rvert-4}=1$ | Function | $x\neq\pm4$ | even, exclusions paired |
| $y=\sqrt{\lvert x\rvert}$ | Function | $\mathbb{R}$ | even |
| $y\lvert x\rvert=x$ | Function | $x\neq0$ | at $x=0$ **every** $y$ works |

**Every row is a function**, confirming that $x$-side decorations never threaten the verdict.
The payoff is a checking rule you can apply in three seconds: **if the equation is even in $x$, the
excluded set must be symmetric about $0$.**
Part (a) excludes $0,3,-3$, which is symmetric.
An answer of "$x\neq0,3$" is visibly wrong by this test alone, and that is the most common error on the
real question.

Row 7 is the exception that proves it is a rule about the *verdict* and not about the domain.
$y\lvert x\rvert=x$ at $x=0$ reads $0=0$, satisfied by every $y$, so strictly this one is not a function
either.
Its coefficient collapsed with a zero right side, which is Family 10.

### Family 10 - degenerate: no $y$ anywhere, every $y$, or a one-point domain

**Trigger.** A sum of squares equal to zero, an equation with no $y$ in it at all, or a coefficient of
$y$ that collapses at the same $x$ where the right side vanishes.

**Tool.** Stop pattern matching and count solutions by hand at the suspicious $x$.
This family is where every general rule breaks, so the count test in Section 2.2 is the only reliable
instrument.

| Equation | Verdict | What happens |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^2+y^2=0$ | **Function**, domain $\{0\}$ | Only $(0,0)$ works. One $x$, one $y$, so the definition is satisfied on a one-point domain. |
| $x^2+y^2=-1$ | Not a function | No real solutions at all, an empty relation, so there is nothing to be a function of. |
| $y=3$ | Function, domain $\mathbb{R}$ | A constant is a function. |
| $x=3$ | Not a function | At $x=3$ every $y$ works, and elsewhere none does. The vertical line. |
| $xy=0$ | Not a function | At $x=0$ every $y$ works. |
| $x^2y=x$ | Not a function | Cancelling gives $y=\frac1x$, but at $x=0$ the equation reads $0=0$ and every $y$ works. |
| $(x-1)^2+(y-2)^2=0$ | **Function**, domain $\{1\}$ | Only $(1,2)$ works. |

Two of these deserve real attention.

**$x^2+y^2=0$ is a function.** This is the counterexample to the "even power of $y$ means not a
function" rule, and it works because the right side is forced to zero, which is the one case where
$y^2=c$ has a single solution.
The domain is the single point $x=0$.
A one-point domain is unusual but entirely legal.

**$x^2y=x$ is the mirror of part (a), and the most instructive row in the whole catalog.**
Both come from a coefficient of $y$ that collapses.
In part (a) the collapse left an impossible equation, so those $x$ had **no** $y$ and dropped out of the
domain, verdict intact.
Here the collapse leaves $0=0$, so $x=0$ has **every** $y$, and the verdict dies.
The difference is entirely whether the right side vanishes at the same time as the coefficient.
If a variation of Q1.3 ever looks like part (a) but with the numerator also vanishing, this is the row
that catches it.

### Family 11 - a graph or a table instead of an equation

**Trigger.** The question shows a curve, or a table of $x$ and $y$ values.

**Tool.** For a graph, the vertical line test: if any vertical line meets the curve more than once, it
is not a function.
For a table, look for a repeated $x$.

| Given | Verdict | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Circle, ellipse | Not a function | A vertical line through the interior meets it twice |
| Sideways parabola $x=y^2$ | Not a function | Same |
| Hyperbola $x^2-y^2=1$ | Not a function | Same |
| $\lvert x\rvert$-shaped corner graph | Function | Every vertical line meets it once; a corner is not a problem |
| Graph with a hole at $x=2$ | Function | A hole means $x=2$ is not in the domain, which is allowed |
| Graph with a filled and an open dot stacked at $x=2$ | Function | Only the filled dot counts as a value |
| Graph with **two filled dots** at $x=2$ | Not a function | Two outputs at one input |
| Table with $x=2$ twice, giving $y=5$ and $y=7$ | Not a function | Two outputs |
| Table with $x=2$ twice, both giving $y=5$ | Function | Repeated input, same output, no ambiguity |

The last two rows are the whole point of the table version.
A repeated $x$ is only a problem if the $y$-values **disagree**.
The same trap appears in Family 12 at a piecewise seam.

### Family 12 - a piecewise rule: check the seam

**Trigger.** A function defined by cases, especially when the intervals share an endpoint via $\leq$ on
one side and $\geq$ on the other.

**Tool.** Find every $x$ claimed by two branches and evaluate both.
Agreement means well defined; disagreement means not.
This is the one family where "well defined" is doing work that "is a function" does not obviously do,
and it is exactly why the exam uses that phrase.

| Piecewise rule | At the seam | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x+1$ for $x\leq2$; $3x$ for $x\geq2$ | $3$ versus $6$ | **Not well defined** |
| $x+1$ for $x\leq2$; $2x-1$ for $x\geq2$ | $3$ versus $3$ | Well defined |
| $x$ for $x\leq3$; $5$ for $x\geq3$ | $3$ versus $5$ | **Not well defined** |
| $x^2$ for $x\leq1$; $2x-1$ for $x\geq1$ | $1$ versus $1$ | Well defined |
| $2x$ for $x\leq1$; $x^2+2$ for $x\geq1$ | $2$ versus $3$ | **Not well defined** |
| $\sqrt x$ for $x\geq0$; $-x$ for $x\leq0$ | $0$ versus $0$ | Well defined |
| $x^2$ for $x<1$; $2x+7$ for $x\geq1$ | no shared $x$ | Well defined, though discontinuous |

The last row separates two ideas that are easy to merge.
Strict inequality on one side means no $x$ is claimed twice, so the rule is well defined **even though
the graph jumps**.
Continuity is a different question from well-definedness, and MT2 tests both, in different items.
A well defined function can jump.
A badly defined rule is not a function at all, jump or no jump.

### Family 13 - the reverse question: is $x$ a function of $y$?

**Trigger.** The question names $y$ as the independent variable, or asks whether the relation is
one-to-one, or asks for an inverse.

**Tool.** Swap the roles of the letters and rerun the whole procedure.
For a graph, this becomes the **horizontal** line test.

| Relation | $y$ a function of $x$? | $x$ a function of $y$? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y=x^3$ | Yes | Yes, $x=y^{1/3}$ |
| $y=x^2$ | Yes | No: $y=9$ gives $x=\pm3$ |
| $y^2+x^2=10$ | No | No, by the same symmetry |
| $\frac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}=1$ | **Yes** | **No**: $y$ is even in $x$, so $y=2$ comes from both $x=4$ and $x=-4$ |
| $y=e^x$ | Yes | Yes, $x=\ln y$, for $y>0$ |
| $y=\sin x$ | Yes | No, periodic |

Row 4 is the reverse-question version of the actual exam problem and a genuinely good check on whether
you understood part (a).
The relation is a function in one direction and not in the other, and the asymmetry comes from the same
$\lvert x\rvert$ that Family 9 told you to ignore.
Ignoring it was correct for the forward question and would be wrong here.

Rows 1 and 2 are the pair to remember: **odd powers are invertible, even powers are not.**
That is the same fact as Families 2 and 3, seen from the other side, and it is the fact that decides
whether a function has an inverse later in the course.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

1. **Forgetting the domain on part (a).**
   The verdict "function" is correct without it, but the question says *Explain*, and the three excluded
   values are the only non-trivial content in part (a).
   This is the most likely place to drop a mark while still writing the right verdict.

2. **Excluding only $x=0$ and $x=3$, forgetting $x=-3$.**
   $\lvert x\rvert=3$ has two solutions.
   The parity check in Family 9 catches this in three seconds: an even expression must give a symmetric
   excluded set.

3. **Deciding (a) is not a function because it is undefined somewhere.**
   Zero outputs removes an $x$ from the domain.
   Two outputs kills the function.
   These are different, and the whole design of part (a) is to see whether you know it.

4. **Deciding (b) is a function by taking only the positive root.**
   Writing $y=\sqrt{10-x^2}$ is choosing an answer the equation did not authorise.
   The equation contains $y^2$, and $y^2=9$ has two solutions.

5. **Answering (b) with "$\pm$, therefore not a function" and no witness.**
   It is the right reason, but *Explain* wants the specific pair.
   $x=1$ with $y=3$ and $y=-3$ takes four extra words and converts a partial mark into a full one.

6. **Multiplying through by the denominator and forgetting what you multiplied by.**
   $2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert$ is a *different relation* from the original: it is defined at $0,\pm3$ and
   the original is not.
   Write the exclusion down before you clear the fraction, not after.

7. **Trusting the degree-in-$y$ cue as a proof.**
   Correct on both parts of the real question, but $y^3-3y=x$ is odd-degree and not a function, and
   $x^2+y^2=0$ is even-degree and is one.
   Use the cue to pick a side, then justify with uniqueness or a witness.

8. **Bracket error entering $\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$.**
   `x^2-3*abs(x)/2` divides only the last term.
   The whole numerator needs brackets.

9. **Confusing "cannot solve for $y$" with "not a function".**
   $y^3+y=x$ has no elementary solution and is a perfectly good function.
   Monotonicity in $y$ is the argument.

10. **Confusing well-definedness with continuity on a piecewise variation.**
    A rule with a jump can be well defined.
    A rule whose branches disagree at a shared endpoint cannot.

The method is worth describing, because for a conceptual question it is not obvious that there is

Rather than checking arithmetic, the harness independently **counts how many real $y$ satisfy each
equation at a given $x$**, by sweeping $y$ over $[-200,200]$ on a grid of 400001 points, bisecting every
sign change to 80 iterations, and filtering the results by residual so that a pole is not mistaken for
a root.

a function, and at least $2$ for every row claimed not to be, with the specific witness $y$-values also
substituted back and required to give a residual under $10^{-11}$.
Three separate guards were needed and each was added after a false failure:

- a **plateau collector**, because a flat root such as $y^5=0$ keeps $\lvert F\rvert$ under the
  zero threshold across many grid points and was counted 13 times;
- a **residual filter**, because a pole makes $F$ change sign without crossing zero;
- an **analytic branch** for $e^y=c$ with $c\leq0$, because $e^y$ underflows to $0.0$ below
  $y\approx-745$, so a genuine "no solution" looked like a plateau of 25166 roots.
