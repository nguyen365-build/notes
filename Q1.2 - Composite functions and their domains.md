# Q1.2 - Composite functions and their domains

**The question (MT1 Q2, 6 points).**
Let $f(x) = 2x^2 - 5$ and $g(x) = \dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}.$
Find the composite functions and their corresponding domains.

- a. $f\left(g(x)\right)$
- b. $g\left(f(x)\right)$

**The answers.**

$$\textbf{a.}\quad f(g(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2} - 5,
\qquad \text{domain } \left(-\infty,\tfrac92\right)\cup\left(\tfrac92,\infty\right)$$

$$\textbf{b.}\quad g(f(x)) = \frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19},
\qquad \text{domain } \left(-\infty,-\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)\cup\left(-\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)\cup\left(\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2},\infty\right)$$

**What to type into Möbius:** `2*((x+5)/(2*x-9))^2-5` and `2*x^2/(4*x^2-19)`

**Browser version of this tutorial:** https://claude.ai/code/artifact/c3c717c2-b86a-4cc9-8806-b8ae1b41e5f5

Source of the question: `MATH265.md` Q1.2, and `MATH265/MATH265.md` "Midterm Exam 1" item 2, which
prints **(6 points)**.
Unlike item 3 on the same paper, this question carries **no "no credit will be given" clause**, so no
particular method is mandated.

## 0. The 30-second version

If you only remember one thing, remember this chain.

```
f(x) = 2x^2 - 5            domain of f: ALL reals   (no denominator, no root)
g(x) = (x+5)/(2x-9)        domain of g: x != 9/2    (denominator dies at 9/2)

a) f(g(x))  substitute g into f   2*[(x+5)/(2x-9)]^2 - 5
   domain   only g can fail       x != 9/2

b) g(f(x))  substitute f into g   (2x^2-5+5) / (2(2x^2-5)-9) = 2x^2/(4x^2-19)
   domain   g must survive f(x)   4x^2-19 != 0  ->  x != +- sqrt(19)/2
```

Four lines, about thirty seconds, no calculator.

The one habit that carries the whole question: **write the domain down before you simplify.**
Simplification is the step that destroys the evidence.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is 6 of 64 points on the midterm, and it is the largest of the four function questions.
It looks like bookkeeping, but the examiner is checking four reflexes, and every one of them reappears
later in the same paper and again in the final.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| Substituting an entire expression in place of $x$ | the chain rule, which is literally the derivative of $f(g(x))$ |
| Recognising $f\circ g$ structure inside a formula | $u$-substitution in the integral question, implicit differentiation |
| Solving "denominator $=0$" | vertical asymptotes (Q4.1), where a limit fails to exist (Q3.x) |
| Knowing a restriction survives simplification | holes versus poles, removable versus infinite discontinuity |

The chain rule is the reason this question is on the paper at all.
You cannot differentiate $f(g(x))$ until you can *see* that a formula is $f(g(x))$, and that is exactly
the skill being graded here.
Treat Q1.2 as the rehearsal for the derivative questions, not as a precalculus leftover.

The other half of the question is the word **domain**, and that is where the marks actually move.
Almost everyone gets the algebra.
The domain is what separates a 6 from a 4.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2.1 The three restriction triggers

There are only three things in a first-year formula that can refuse an input.
Scan any formula for these three and nothing else.

| Trigger | What you write | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A denominator | set it $\neq 0$ | division by zero is undefined |
| An **even** root ($\sqrt{\ }$, $\sqrt[4]{\ }$) | set the radicand $\geq 0$ | no real even root of a negative |
| A logarithm | set the argument $> 0$ | $\ln$ of zero or a negative is undefined |

Two things that are **not** triggers, and cost people time every year:

- **Odd** roots accept everything. $\sqrt[3]{x-7}$ has domain $\mathbb{R}$.
- Polynomials accept everything. $2x^2-5$ has domain $\mathbb{R}$, and that is what makes part (a) short.

Note the strictness difference: a square root allows the boundary ($\geq$), a logarithm does not ($>$).
That single character is worth a mark on its own, and Family 8 below makes the contrast explicit.

### 2.2 The composition, and the order convention

$$(f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$$

Read it right to left: $g$ runs **first**, $f$ runs **second**.
The notation $f\circ g$ is pronounced "$f$ after $g$", which is the mnemonic that keeps the order straight.

Mechanically, $f(g(x))$ means: take the formula for $f$, and replace **every** $x$ in it by the whole
expression $g(x)$, brackets and all.

Order matters, and this question is built to prove it.
For the given $f$ and $g$ the two composites are not even the same shape: one is a quadratic over a
squared linear, the other is a quadratic over a quadratic.
They were checked at seven sample points and disagree at all seven.

### 2.3 The domain rule, in one line

$$\text{Domain of } f(g(x)) \;=\; \{\,x \;:\; x\in D_g \ \textbf{ and } \ g(x)\in D_f \,\}$$

Two conditions, both must hold, and they are asked in that order because the second one does not even
make sense until the first is true.

The part people get wrong is the second condition.
$f$'s restriction does **not** apply to $x$.
It applies to $g(x)$, the thing $f$ is actually being handed.

In part (b), $g$ refuses the input $9/2$.
$g$ is being handed $f(x)$.
So the thing you must exclude is not "$x = 9/2$" but "$f(x) = 9/2$", and solving that is a different
equation with different answers.

### 2.4 The domains of the two given functions

Get these down before you compose anything.
This is 10 seconds of work that makes the rest automatic.

| Function | Formula | Triggers | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f$ | $2x^2-5$ | none, it is a polynomial | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $g$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | one denominator | $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}$ |

$f$ having domain $\mathbb{R}$ is the single most useful fact in the whole question.
It means that in part (a) the second condition is automatically satisfied and there is nothing to do.
It also means that in part (b) the first condition is automatically satisfied.
Each part is therefore only half a domain problem, which is why 6 points is fair for both.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1.** Write $D_f$ and $D_g$ from the raw formulas, before composing. Scan for the three triggers.
>
> **Step 2.** Write the two conditions for the composite you were asked for, in words, before doing algebra.
>
> **Step 3.** Solve condition 1 (the inner function's own restriction) for $x$.
>
> **Step 4.** Solve condition 2 by setting the **inner formula** equal to the outer's forbidden value, or by putting the inner formula into the outer's trigger and solving.
>
> **Step 5.** Now, and only now, substitute and simplify the algebra.
>
> **Step 6.** State the domain as the set from steps 3 and 4. Do not re-derive it from the simplified formula.

Step 6 is the whole discipline.
Steps 1 to 4 cost you fifteen seconds and they are insurance against the one error that is worth the
most marks.

**Classify before you compute.** One glance at the two functions tells you how much work each part is.

| Inner function | Outer function | Work required |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| polynomial | polynomial | none, domain is $\mathbb{R}$ |
| restricted | polynomial | inner's restriction only (this is part **a**) |
| polynomial | restricted | solve outer's trigger applied to the inner (this is part **b**) |
| restricted | restricted | **both**, and the inner one may vanish when you simplify |

Part (a) and part (b) are deliberately one row apart.
The examiner is checking that you know which half of the rule is doing the work in each.

## 4. Q1.2 worked four ways

### Route A - domain first, then algebra (use this one)

**Part a, $f(g(x))$.**

Step 1. $D_f=\mathbb{R}$, $D_g=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}$.

Step 2. Need $x\in D_g$, and need $g(x)\in D_f$.

Step 3. $x\in D_g$ gives $2x-9\neq 0$, so $x\neq \dfrac92$.

Step 4. $g(x)\in D_f$ is automatic, because $D_f=\mathbb{R}$ accepts every output $g$ can produce.

Step 5. Substitute $g$ into $f$, replacing $x$ by the whole fraction:

$$f(g(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5 = \frac{2(x+5)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}}-5$$

Step 6. Domain $= \left(-\infty,\frac92\right)\cup\left(\frac92,\infty\right)$, from step 3.

**Part b, $g(f(x))$.**

Step 1. Same two domains.

Step 2. Need $x\in D_f$, and need $f(x)\in D_g$.

Step 3. $x\in D_f$ is automatic, since $D_f=\mathbb{R}$.

Step 4. $f(x)\in D_g$ means $f(x)\neq \dfrac92$. Solve the equality and exclude the solutions:

$$2x^2-5=\frac92 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x^2=\frac{19}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2=\frac{19}{4}
\;\Longrightarrow\; x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$$

Step 5. Substitute $f$ into $g$, replacing $x$ by $2x^2-5$:

$$g(f(x)) = \frac{(2x^{2}-5)+5}{2(2x^{2}-5)-9} = \frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-10-9} = \frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19}$$

Step 6. Domain $=\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right\}$, from step 4.

Notice the numerator collapse in part (b): $(2x^2-5)+5 = 2x^2$ exactly.
That is not a coincidence, it is the examiner rewarding you for substituting carefully rather than
expanding blindly.

### Route B - simplify first, read the domain off the result

Substitute, combine, then set the visible denominator to zero.

For part (a), combining over a common denominator gives a single fraction:

$$f(g(x)) = \frac{2(x+5)^{2}-5(2x-9)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}} = \frac{-18x^{2}+200x-355}{(2x-9)^{2}}$$

The denominator $(2x-9)^2$ is visible, so you read off $x\neq 9/2$ and you are right.

For part (b) the denominator $4x^2-19$ is visible, so you read off $x=\pm\sqrt{19}/2$ and you are right.

**This route gets the right answer on this question and it is a bad habit anyway.**
It is right here only because nothing cancelled.
Apply the same route to $g(g(x))$, built from this question's own $g$, and it silently loses an excluded
value: see Family 3.
A method that is correct by luck is not a method.

Also worth knowing: combining part (a) into a single fraction is **not required**.
The question says "find the composite functions", not "simplify".
Combining costs about forty seconds and introduces a sign error opportunity in $-5(2x-9)^2$, and it buys
nothing. Both written forms were verified to agree at 80 sample points.

### Route C - the formal set-builder write-up

$$D_{f\circ g}=\{x\in D_g : g(x)\in D_f\}
=\left\{x\neq \tfrac92 : \tfrac{x+5}{2x-9}\in\mathbb{R}\right\}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\tfrac92\}$$

$$D_{g\circ f}=\{x\in D_f : f(x)\in D_g\}
=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R} : 2x^2-5\neq \tfrac92\right\}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\}$$

This is the definition written out, and it is what earns method marks on a hand-graded paper.
It is slower to write than Route A but it is the same thing, so use Route A to think and Route C to
write if the paper asks you to justify.

### Route D - the cross-check: two ways to the same exclusion

For part (b) there are two independent ways to find the excluded values, and they must agree.

- **Route D1, conceptual.** $g$ dies at input $9/2$, so exclude $x$ where $f(x)=9/2$. That gives $x^2=19/4$.
- **Route D2, mechanical.** Set the simplified denominator to zero: $4x^2-19=0$, so $x^2=19/4$.

Both give $x=\pm\sqrt{19}/2$.
Two genuinely different derivations landing on the same number is a check.
Re-reading your own algebra is not.

Both were verified: $4\left(\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)^2-19 = 0$ and $f\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)=\frac92$.

### Which route, and why

| Route | Speed | Safe when the inner restriction cancels? | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, domain first | fast | **yes** | **use this one under pressure** |
| B, simplify first | fastest | no, silently wrong | never as a habit |
| C, set-builder | slow | yes | write-up only, if method is graded |
| D, cross-check | 10 seconds | n/a | always, on the part with the harder exclusion |

**Use Route A, then spend ten seconds on Route D for part (b).**
Route A costs nothing extra, because you need $D_f$ and $D_g$ anyway to do the substitution, and it is
immune to the one failure mode that actually loses marks.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

If the paper you sit asks for justification, these are the sentences that earn the method marks.
Six lines, and every clause is doing work.

> **(a)** $f$ is a polynomial, so $D_f=\mathbb{R}$; $g$ is rational with $D_g=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}$.
> Substituting, $f(g(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2-5$.
> Since $D_f=\mathbb{R}$, every output of $g$ is acceptable to $f$, so the only restriction is $g$'s own:
> $2x-9\neq0$. Hence the domain is $\left(-\infty,\frac92\right)\cup\left(\frac92,\infty\right)$.
>
> **(b)** Substituting, $g(f(x)) = \frac{(2x^2-5)+5}{2(2x^2-5)-9} = \frac{2x^2}{4x^2-19}$.
> Since $D_f=\mathbb{R}$, $x$ is unrestricted by $f$; but $g$ requires its input $f(x)\neq 9/2$, and
> $2x^2-5=\frac92$ gives $x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$.
> Hence the domain is $\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right\}$.

The two phrases that are actually being marked are "since $D_f=\mathbb{R}$" and "$g$ requires its input
$f(x)\neq 9/2$".
The first shows you knew which condition was vacuous and why.
The second shows you applied the restriction to $g$'s input rather than to $x$.

**Format warning.**
The sample paper prints "(6 points)" and neighbouring questions say "Explain the procedure you are using",
which reads like a hand-marked written exam.
The live delivery for this course is Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, where no work is shown.
Prepare for both: get the two formulas and the two exclusion sets fast, which is all Möbius wants, and be
able to write the six lines above in case the paper you sit does ask for method.

## 6. Verify before you commit

This takes about fifteen seconds for both parts and it catches nearly everything.

**Check 1, spot-evaluate at one easy point.**
Pick an $x$ that makes the arithmetic trivial and run it through both the raw composition and your
simplified answer.
They must match.

For part (b) at $x=0$: raw is $g(f(0))=g(-5)=\frac{0}{-19}=0$, and the formula gives
$\frac{0}{-19}=0$. Match.

For part (a) at $x=0$: raw is $f(g(0))=f\!\left(-\frac59\right)=2\cdot\frac{25}{81}-5=-\frac{355}{81}$,
and the formula gives the same. Match. That is $\approx -4.383$.

**Check 2, the excluded value must actually break something.**
Substitute your excluded $x$ into the **original** composition and confirm you get a division by zero.
For part (b), $f(\sqrt{19}/2)=2\cdot\frac{19}{4}-5=\frac{19}{2}-5=\frac92$, and $g$ is undefined at
$9/2$. Confirmed.
If your excluded value does not break the original, it is not an excluded value.

**Check 3, the $\pm$ check.**
Any exclusion that came from an $x^2$ equation has **two** solutions.
Part (b)'s exclusion came from $x^2=19/4$, so it is $\pm\sqrt{19}/2$, not just the positive one.
Dropping the negative root is the most common half-mark loss on this question.

**Check 4, count your restrictions against the classify table.**
Part (a) is "restricted inner, polynomial outer", so it should produce exactly one restriction. It does.
Part (b) is "polynomial inner, restricted outer", so it should produce as many restrictions as the
outer's trigger equation has roots. The trigger was quadratic, so two. It does.
If you produced zero restrictions on either part, you skipped a step.

**The four numbers worth memorising outright.**

$$\frac92 = 4.5,\qquad \frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\approx 2.1794,\qquad
\frac{91}{16}=5.6875,\qquad -\frac{355}{81}\approx -4.3827$$

The first two are the answers.
The third is where $g(g(x))$ dies, which is the most likely follow-up.
The fourth is $(f\circ g)(0)$, the cheapest spot-check available.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

The exam is machine-marked by Möbius, which hides two different grading engines behind one answer box,
and nothing in the interface tells you which one you are in.
Grading is by mathematical equivalence, so a correct answer is never rejected for its *form*, only for
being *parsed* differently than you intended.

### The function expressions

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Multiplication | `2*x` | `2x` |
| Powers | `x^2` | `x2`, `x**2` |
| Whole denominators | `2*x^2/(4*x^2-19)` | `2*x^2/4*x^2-19` |
| Whole numerators | `(2*x^2-5+5)/(...)` | `2*x^2-5+5/(...)` |
| Bracket before squaring | `((x+5)/(2*x-9))^2` | `(x+5)/(2*x-9)^2` |
| Natural log | `ln(x)` | `log(x)`, which is base 10 in one engine |

**The single most expensive typo on this question** is `(x+5)/(2*x-9)^2`, which squares only the
denominator and gives $\frac{x+5}{(2x-9)^2}$ instead of $\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2$.
Bracket the entire fraction before you raise it to a power.

The second most expensive is `2*x^2/4*x^2-19`, which parses as $\frac{2x^2}{4}\cdot x^2-19$.
Bracket the whole denominator.

These are the two safe strings:

- a: `2*((x+5)/(2*x-9))^2-5`
- b: `2*x^2/(4*x^2-19)`

Equivalent forms that also grade correct for (a), if you prefer them:
`2*(x+5)^2/(2*x-9)^2-5` and `(-18*x^2+200*x-355)/(2*x-9)^2`.
All three were verified to agree numerically at 80 sample points.

### The domain

**This part is not fully verified and you should settle it on the sample exam, not in the exam.**
My platform notes cover expression entry thoroughly but do not document Möbius interval-notation syntax,
so treat the following as a strategy rather than as a guaranteed string.

Möbius questions ask for a domain in one of three shapes, and which one you get is up to the question
author:

1. **A dropdown or multiple choice.** Nothing to type, just read carefully for $\leq$ versus $<$.
2. **An inequality box.** Type the restriction, e.g. `x <> 9/2` or `x != 9/2` depending on what the box accepts.
3. **An interval box.** Interval notation with a union.

For case 3, write exact values, never decimals: `sqrt(19)/2`, not `2.179`.
Infinity is normally `infinity` or `-infinity` spelled out.

**Always click the preview icon.**
An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics.
Preview is the only proof that the parser agrees with you; reading your own answer back is not.
On a domain answer this matters more than usual, because a mis-parsed union looks fine in the input box.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
The families below are organised by **the tool the variation forces**, not by how the question looks.
Two questions that look different but force the same tool are the same question.

Everything below has been numerically verified: each simplified formula was checked against a literal
composition at dozens of sample points, each claimed excluded value was confirmed to break the true
composition, and each exclusion set was swept for unclaimed roots.

Read the whole catalog as one idea with twelve surfaces.
There are only **three** restriction triggers in existence, and only **two** places they can sit, inner
or outer.
Every family below is a choice of trigger and a choice of position.

### Family 0 - polynomial inside polynomial: nothing restricts

**Trigger:** neither function has a denominator, an even root, or a log.
**Tool:** none. Substitute and state $\mathbb{R}$.

| Composite | Simplified | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(3x+1)$ with $f=2x^2-5$ | $18x^2+12x-3$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $(3x+1)\circ f$ | $6x^2-14$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $f(f(x))$ | $8x^4-40x^2+45$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |

The only way to lose a mark here is to invent a restriction.
A quartic has no denominator, so there is nothing to exclude.

### Family 1 - restricted inner, polynomial outer: only the inner restricts

**This is part (a)'s family.**
**Trigger:** the outer function is a polynomial, so $D_{\text{outer}}=\mathbb{R}$.
**Tool:** the domain of the composite equals the domain of the inner function. Full stop.

**Worked shape.** $D_{f\circ g}=D_g$, because condition 2 is vacuous.

| $f$ (outer) | $g$ (inner) | $f(g(x))$ | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $2x^2-5$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2-5$ | $x\neq\frac92$ |
| $x^2+1$ | $\dfrac{1}{x-3}$ | $\dfrac{1}{(x-3)^2}+1$ | $x\neq3$ |
| $3x-2$ | $\dfrac{2x+1}{x+4}$ | $\dfrac{4x-5}{x+4}$ | $x\neq-4$ |

The third row is worth staring at.
The composite simplified all the way down to a *different* rational function, and the excluded value
$x=-4$ happens to still be visible in it.
That is luck, not law. Family 3 is where the luck runs out.

### Family 2 - polynomial inner, restricted outer: solve the trigger on the inner

**This is part (b)'s family.**
**Trigger:** the inner function is a polynomial, the outer has a denominator or a root.
**Tool:** take the outer's trigger, substitute the **inner formula** into it, and solve.

**Worked shape.** For $g$ rational with bad input $c$: exclude the solutions of $f(x)=c$.

| $f$ (inner) | $g$ (outer) | $g(f(x))$ | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $2x^2-5$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $\dfrac{2x^2}{4x^2-19}$ | $x\neq\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$ |
| $x^2-4$ | $\dfrac1x$ | $\dfrac{1}{x^2-4}$ | $x\neq\pm2$ |
| $x^2+1$ | $\dfrac1x$ | $\dfrac{1}{x^2+1}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $3x-7$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $\dfrac{3x-2}{6x-23}$ | $x\neq\frac{23}{6}$ |

Read the table as structure.
The number of excluded values is the number of **real** roots of the trigger equation, which is why row
2 gives two, row 4 gives one, and row 3 gives none.

Row 3 is the trap in this family: $x^2+1=0$ has no real solution, so the domain is all of $\mathbb{R}$
even though the outer function has a denominator.
A denominator in the outer function does not guarantee an exclusion.
It guarantees an *equation*, and that equation may have no real roots.

Row 1's exclusion is irrational, which is the giveaway that the examiner wants exact form.
Write $\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$, never $\pm2.179$.

### Family 3 - both restricted, and the inner restriction VANISHES on simplification

**This is the highest-value family in the catalog, and it is the one Route B fails.**
**Trigger:** both functions have a restriction, and the algebra cancels.
**Tool:** record $D_{\text{inner}}$ **before** simplifying, then add the outer's restriction.

**Worked shape.** Simplify $f(g(x))$ and you get a formula whose visible denominator is missing one of
the true exclusions. The missing one is always an exclusion of the inner function.

| $f$ (outer) | $g$ (inner) | Simplified | True domain | Invisible |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $\dfrac{11x-40}{91-16x}$ | $x\neq\frac92,\ x\neq\frac{91}{16}$ | $\frac92$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x-2}$ | $\dfrac1x$ | $\dfrac{x}{1-2x}$ | $x\neq0,\ x\neq\frac12$ | $0$ |
| $\dfrac1x$ | $\dfrac1x$ | $x$ | $x\neq0$ | $0$ |
| $\dfrac{x}{x+1}$ | $\dfrac1x$ | $\dfrac{1}{1+x}$ | $x\neq0,\ x\neq-1$ | $0$ |

Row 1 is built from **this question's own $g$**, composed with itself, which makes it the single most
likely follow-up the examiner can ask.
Work it once:

$$g(g(x))=\frac{\frac{x+5}{2x-9}+5}{2\cdot\frac{x+5}{2x-9}-9}
=\frac{\frac{(x+5)+5(2x-9)}{2x-9}}{\frac{2(x+5)-9(2x-9)}{2x-9}}
=\frac{11x-40}{91-16x}$$

The $(2x-9)$ cancels top and bottom, and with it goes every trace of $x=9/2$.
Substituting $x=9/2$ into $\frac{11x-40}{91-16x}$ gives a perfectly innocent $\frac{9.5}{19}=\frac12$.
That $\frac12$ is a lie.
It is exactly $g$'s horizontal asymptote, the one value $g$ can never output, appearing as a phantom
because the cancellation erased the fact that $g(9/2)$ does not exist.

Row 3 is the purest demonstration in the catalog: $\frac{1}{1/x}$ simplifies to $x$, the identity
function, whose formula suggests domain $\mathbb{R}$.
The true domain is $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$.

**The rule this family teaches, in one sentence:**
cancellation removes a factor from the formula but it does not remove the point from the domain, so the
domain must be recorded before the cancellation happens.

### Family 4 - even root on the inside

**Trigger:** the **inner** function contains $\sqrt{\ }$.
**Tool:** radicand $\geq0$ first, then check whether the outer adds anything.

**Worked shape.** The outer often squares the root away, leaving a formula with no root in it at all.
The domain is still a ray.

| $f$ (outer) | $g$ (inner) | $f(g(x))$ | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^2-1$ | $\sqrt{x-3}$ | $x-4$ | $[3,\infty)$ |
| $2x^2-5$ | $\sqrt{x}$ | $2x-5$ | $[0,\infty)$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x-2}$ | $\sqrt{x+4}$ | $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}-2}$ | $[-4,0)\cup(0,\infty)$ |

Row 1 is the cleanest trap available to an examiner.
The answer is the straight line $x-4$, and a line obviously has domain $\mathbb{R}$, except that this one
does not, because you can only reach it from $x\geq3$.
Squaring is just as domain-destroying as cancelling.

Row 3 stacks two triggers: the root needs $x\geq-4$, and the outer's denominator needs
$\sqrt{x+4}\neq2$, which is $x\neq0$.
Note that the extra exclusion came from solving $\sqrt{x+4}=2$, an equation in the *inner formula*, which
is exactly Family 2's tool applied on top of Family 4's.

### Family 5 - even root on the outside

**Trigger:** the **outer** function is $\sqrt{\ }$.
**Tool:** solve the inequality $g(x)\geq0$. This is the family that forces a sign chart or a factoring.

| $f$ (outer) | $g$ (inner) | $f(g(x))$ | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $x^2-9$ | $\sqrt{x^2-9}$ | $(-\infty,-3]\cup[3,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $2x^2-5$ | $\sqrt{2x^2-5}$ | $\left(-\infty,-\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\right]\cup\left[\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2},\infty\right)$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $9-x^2$ | $\sqrt{9-x^2}$ | $[-3,3]$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $x^2+1$ | $\sqrt{x^2+1}$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |

The shape of the answer flips with the sign of the leading coefficient, and that is the only thing to
get right.
An upward parabola is non-negative **outside** its roots, so you get two rays.
A downward parabola is non-negative **between** its roots, so you get one closed interval.
Row 4 has no real roots and stays positive everywhere, so nothing is excluded.

Row 2 uses this question's own $f$ as the inner function, and the boundary is
$\frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}\approx1.5811$.
Note the brackets are square: $\sqrt{0}=0$ is perfectly legal, so the boundary points are **included**.

### Family 6 - even root outside a rational inner: the sign chart

**Trigger:** $\sqrt{\ }$ on the outside, a fraction on the inside.
**Tool:** solve a quotient inequality. Both the numerator's zero and the denominator's zero are critical
points, but they get **different bracket types**.

| $f$ (outer) | $g$ (inner) | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $\dfrac{1}{x-2}$ | $(2,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $(-\infty,-5]\cup\left(\frac92,\infty\right)$ |

Row 2 is this question's own $g$ under a square root, and it is the hardest thing the examiner can build
from these two functions without leaving the topic.
Work the sign chart on $\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\geq0$ with critical points at $-5$ and $\frac92$:

| Interval | $x+5$ | $2x-9$ | Quotient | Keep? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x<-5$ | $-$ | $-$ | $+$ | yes |
| $-5<x<\frac92$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ | no |
| $x>\frac92$ | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ | yes |

**The bracket rule, which is the whole point of this family:**
a zero of the **numerator** makes the quotient $0$, and $\sqrt0$ is fine, so $x=-5$ is **included** with a
square bracket.
A zero of the **denominator** makes the quotient undefined, so $x=\frac92$ is **excluded** with a round
bracket, even though the inequality is "$\geq$".
Getting one of these two brackets wrong is the standard way to lose the mark after doing all the real work.

### Family 7 - nested even roots

**Trigger:** a root inside a root.
**Tool:** two inequalities, solved from the outside in. The outer condition is usually the binding one.

| Composite | Conditions | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{\sqrt{x}-1}$ | $x\geq0$ **and** $\sqrt{x}\geq1$ | $[1,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt{\sqrt{x-1}}=(x-1)^{1/4}$ | $x-1\geq0$ | $[1,\infty)$ |
| $\sqrt{\sqrt{x}-5}$ | $x\geq0$ **and** $\sqrt{x}\geq5$ | $[25,\infty)$ |

Row 1 and row 3 show the pattern: the inner condition $x\geq0$ is swallowed by the outer condition, so
the answer is set entirely by $\sqrt{x}\geq k$, which squares to $x\geq k^2$.
The number that appears in the answer is the **square** of the number in the formula, and that is the
step people skip.

### Family 8 - logs and absolute values

**Trigger:** $\ln$, or $|\ \cdot\ |$ inside a denominator.
**Tool:** same as families 5 and 6, but $\ln$ uses a **strict** inequality.

| Composite | Domain |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\ln(x-3)$ | $(3,\infty)$ |
| $\ln(x^2-4)$ | $(-\infty,-2)\cup(2,\infty)$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{\lvert x\rvert-3}$ | $x\neq\pm3$ |
| $\ln\left(\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)$ | $(-\infty,-5)\cup\left(\frac92,\infty\right)$ |

Compare the last row against Family 6 row 2, which is the same inner function under a square root
instead of a log.
The square root gives $(-\infty,-5]$ with a closed bracket, because $\sqrt0=0$ exists.
The log gives $(-\infty,-5)$ with an open bracket, because $\ln 0$ does not.
One character of difference, produced by one word of difference in the question.

The absolute-value row is a reminder that $|x|=3$ has two solutions.
Any equation with $|\ \cdot\ |$ or a square in it produces a $\pm$, exactly like part (b) did.

### Family 9 - self-composition

**Trigger:** the question asks for $f\circ f$ or $g\circ g$.
**Tool:** whichever family the function belongs to, applied to itself. Nothing new, but the algebra is
heavier and the Family 3 cancellation is much more likely.

| Composite | Simplified | Domain |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(f(x))$ | $8x^4-40x^2+45$ | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $g(g(x))$ | $\dfrac{11x-40}{91-16x}$ | $x\neq\frac92,\ x\neq\frac{91}{16}$ |

Verified spot values: $f(f(1))=13$ and $g(g(0))=-\frac{40}{91}$.

$g\circ g$ is the most likely part (c) this question could grow, and it is a Family 3 case, so it is
where Route B would cost you the mark.

### Family 10 - the reverse question: decompose

**Trigger:** "write $h$ as a composition $f\circ g$", or "identify the inner function".
**Tool:** the inner function is whatever sits inside the outermost operation. Ask "what would I do last
if I were evaluating this by hand?" That last operation is $f$; everything under it is $g$.

| $h(x)$ | $g$ (inner) | $f$ (outer) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{3x^2+1}$ | $3x^2+1$ | $\sqrt{u}$ |
| $(2x-7)^5$ | $2x-7$ | $u^5$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^2+4}$ | $x^2+4$ | $\dfrac1u$ |
| $\sin^2 x$ | $\sin x$ | $u^2$ |
| $2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2-5$ | $\frac{x+5}{2x-9}$ | $2u^2-5$ |

The last row is Q1.2(a) read backwards, which is worth doing once so you recognise your own answer as a
composition.

Decompositions are not unique, and any correct pair is accepted.
This family is the direct bridge to the chain rule: once you can name $f$ and $g$, $\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x))
= f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ writes itself.

### Family 11 - the range of the composite

**Trigger:** the question says "range", not "domain".
**Tool:** track what values the inner function can produce, then push that set through the outer function.
This is the hardest form the question can take and it is rare at 6 points, but it is listed as a variation
in the course notes, so it is fair game.

**$f(g(x))$ has range $[-5,\infty)$.**
$g$ is a Möbius transformation with horizontal asymptote $y=\frac12$, so $g$ produces every real value
except $\frac12$.
Squaring that set gives $[0,\infty)$ **entirely**, because although $g$ never outputs $+\frac12$, it does
output $-\frac12$ at $x=-\frac14$, and $\left(-\frac12\right)^2=\frac14$ is recovered.
So $2g^2-5$ ranges over $[-5,\infty)$, with the minimum $-5$ attained at $x=-5$ where $g=0$.

That recovery is the subtle bit and it was verified explicitly: $g(-\tfrac14)=-\tfrac12$ and
$f(g(-\tfrac14))=-4.5$, so there is **no** hole at $-4.5$ in the range.
An even power heals a gap that is not symmetric about zero.

**$g(f(x))$ has range $(-\infty,0]\cup\left(\frac12,\infty\right)$.**
Write $t=x^2\geq0$, so the composite is $h(t)=\frac{2t}{4t-19}$ with $h'(t)=\frac{-38}{(4t-19)^2}<0$,
decreasing on each branch.
On $t\in[0,\frac{19}{4})$ it runs from $h(0)=0$ down to $-\infty$, giving $(-\infty,0]$.
On $t\in(\frac{19}{4},\infty)$ it runs from $+\infty$ down towards $\frac12$ without reaching it, giving
$\left(\frac12,\infty\right)$.
The value $\frac12$ is unattainable because $\frac{2x^2}{4x^2-19}=\frac12$ reduces to $0=-19$.

A sweep of 400,000 sample points found no value in the claimed gap $(0,\frac12]$, and confirmed $0$ is
attained at $x=0$.

### Family 12 - evaluate at a point, or test membership

**Trigger:** "compute $(f\circ g)(2)$", or "is $x=a$ in the domain of $f\circ g$?"
**Tool:** evaluate inside-out. Never substitute into the simplified formula without checking the domain
first, for exactly the Family 3 reason.

| Ask | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| $(f\circ g)(0)$ | $-\frac{355}{81}\approx-4.3827$ |
| $(f\circ g)(2)$ | $-\frac{27}{25}=-1.08$ |
| $(g\circ f)(0)$ | $0$ |
| $(g\circ f)(3)$ | $\frac{18}{17}$ |
| $(g\circ g)(0)$ | $-\frac{40}{91}$ |
| $(f\circ f)(1)$ | $13$ |
| $(f\circ g)\left(\frac92\right)$ | **does not exist** |
| $(g\circ f)\left(\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)$ | **does not exist** |

The last two rows are the ones being tested.
A "does not exist" answer is a correct answer, and the question is checking whether you evaluate blindly
or check the domain first.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks, worst first.

1. **Reading the domain off the simplified formula.** The cancellation destroyed the evidence. Write $D_g$ down before you simplify. This is the only error in the list that can cost the whole domain mark on a part you otherwise did perfectly. See Family 3.
2. **Applying the outer's restriction to $x$ instead of to the inner formula.** In part (b), writing "$2x-9\neq0$ so $x\neq\frac92$" is the classic. $g$'s denominator is $2\cdot(\text{its input})-9$, and its input is $f(x)$, not $x$.
3. **Dropping the $\pm$.** Part (b)'s exclusion came from $x^2=\frac{19}{4}$, which has two roots. Any exclusion arising from a square or an absolute value has two.
4. **Composing in the wrong order.** $f\circ g$ means $g$ runs first. If your part (a) answer has $x^2$ in the numerator, you did part (b).
5. **Giving a decimal where the answer is irrational.** $\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$, not $\pm2.179$.
6. **Bracket errors when squaring a fraction.** $\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2$ squares both parts. Typing `(x+5)/(2*x-9)^2` squares only the bottom.
7. **Wrong bracket type on an interval boundary.** A numerator zero under a square root is **included**; a denominator zero is **excluded**, even under "$\geq$". A log boundary is always excluded. See Families 6 and 8.
8. **Arithmetic in the substitution.** $2(2x^2-5)-9 = 4x^2-19$, not $4x^2-1$ and not $4x^2-14$. Distribute the $2$ over both terms, then subtract the $9$.
9. **Inventing a restriction that is not there.** $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ has domain $\mathbb{R}$. A denominator gives you an equation to solve, not a guaranteed exclusion.
10. **Forgetting that the answer to "is this in the domain" can be "no".** "Does not exist" is a valid final answer.

## 10. Drill set

Twelve problems, one per family.
Do them without the answer key, then score **domain separately from formula**.
Those two failure modes need different fixes: a wrong formula means slow down on the substitution, a
wrong domain means you skipped step 1 of the procedure.

1. $f(x)=x^2+3$, $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{x-1}$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
2. $f(x)=\dfrac{1}{x-4}$, $g(x)=x^2+1$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
3. $f(x)=\dfrac1x$, $g(x)=\dfrac{1}{x+2}$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
4. $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $g(x)=x^2-16$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
5. $f(x)=x^2-2$, $g(x)=\sqrt{x+7}$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
6. $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $g(x)=\dfrac{x-3}{x+1}$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
7. $g(x)=\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$. Find $g(g(x))$ and its domain.
8. $f(x)=2x^2-5$. Find $f(f(x))$ and its domain.
9. $f(x)=\ln x$, $g(x)=9-x^2$. Find $f(g(x))$ and its domain.
10. Write $h(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4x+1}}$ as $f(g(x))$, and give its domain.
11. With $f(x)=2x^2-5$ and $g(x)=\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}$, compute $(f\circ g)(-5)$ and $(g\circ f)(2)$.
12. $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$, $g(x)=\sqrt{x-5}$. Find $g(f(x))$ and its domain.

### Answer key

| # | Composite | Domain | Family and method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}+3$ | $x\neq1$ | **F1** polynomial outer, inner's restriction only |
| 2 | $\dfrac{1}{x^2-3}$ | $x\neq\pm\sqrt3$ | **F2** solve $g(x)=4$, the outer's bad input |
| 3 | $x+2$ | $x\neq-2$ | **F3** the $\frac{1}{x+2}$ cancels; exclusion is invisible |
| 4 | $\sqrt{x^2-16}$ | $(-\infty,-4]\cup[4,\infty)$ | **F5** upward parabola, non-negative outside its roots |
| 5 | $x+5$ | $[-7,\infty)$ | **F4** squaring kills the root, not the domain |
| 6 | $\sqrt{\dfrac{x-3}{x+1}}$ | $(-\infty,-1)\cup[3,\infty)$ | **F6** sign chart; numerator zero closed, denominator zero open |
| 7 | $\dfrac{11x-40}{91-16x}$ | $x\neq\frac92,\ x\neq\frac{91}{16}$ | **F3/F9** self-composition, $x=\frac92$ vanishes |
| 8 | $8x^4-40x^2+45$ | $\mathbb{R}$ | **F0/F9** polynomial, nothing to exclude |
| 9 | $\ln(9-x^2)$ | $(-3,3)$ | **F8** log is strict, so the brackets are open |
| 10 | $g(x)=4x+1$, $f(u)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt u}$ | $x>-\frac14$ | **F10** decompose; root **and** denominator makes it strict |
| 11 | $(f\circ g)(-5)=-5$, $(g\circ f)(2)=-\frac83$ | n/a | **F12** evaluate inside-out |
| 12 | $\sqrt{\sqrt{x}-5}$ | $[25,\infty)$ | **F7** nested; the boundary is $5^2$, not $5$ |

Three of these are deliberately nasty.
Number 3 and number 7 are Family 3, where the simplified formula lies about the domain.
Number 10 stacks two triggers at once, so the boundary is strict rather than closed: the radicand needs
$4x+1\geq0$ but the denominator needs $\sqrt{4x+1}\neq0$, and together those give $4x+1>0$.

If you got the formula right and the domain wrong on more than two of these, the fix is not more
practice on algebra.
The fix is mechanical: write $D_f$ and $D_g$ as the first line of every answer, before touching the
substitution.
