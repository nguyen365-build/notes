## 0. The 30-second version

```
BOTH PARTS ARE THE SAME FIVE MOVES.  Only f changes.

    f(a + dx)  ~  f(a) + f'(a) dx

(a)  f = cos,  a = 60 deg = pi/3,  dx = +2 deg = pi/90   <- RADIANS, always
     f(a)  = 1/2          f'(a) = -sin(60) = -sqrt3/2
     est   = 1/2 - (sqrt3/2)(pi/90) = 1/2 - sqrt3 pi/180 = 0.4698

(b)  f = sqrt,  a = 16,  dx = +0.4
     f(a)  = 4            f'(a) = 1/(2*4) = 1/8
     est   = 4 + (1/8)(0.4) = 4 + 1/20 = 81/20 = 4.0500

DIRECTION, both parts:  f'' < 0, concave down, tangent above the curve,
                        so BOTH answers are TOO BIG.

FOUR DECIMAL PLACES means WRITE four digits.  It does not mean four of
them are right.  Only two of part (a)'s are, and NONE of part (b)'s.
```

## 2. The four things you must have cold

**1. The formula, in the wording this stem uses.**

The stem says "the method of differentials", so write it that way.

$$dy = f'(a)\,dx \qquad\text{then}\qquad f(a + dx) \approx f(a) + dy$$

That is the same object as the linearization $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$, which is what Q12.2's stem asks for.
One formula, three wordings, and the exam uses all three across the three papers.

**2. The two derivatives, and their values at the two anchors.**

| $f$ | $f'$ | $f''$ | at the anchor |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | $-\cos x$ | $f(\pi/3) = \tfrac12$, $f'(\pi/3) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $f''(\pi/3) = -\tfrac12$ |
| $\sqrt x$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}$ | $f(16) = 4$, $f'(16) = \tfrac18$, $f''(16) = -\tfrac1{256}$ |

Read the anchor column as **structure**.
$f'(16) = 1/8$ is a **unit fraction**, and that is the signature of a correctly chosen root anchor: for $f = x^{1/n}$ at $a = m^n$, the slope is always $\dfrac{1}{n\,m^{n-1}}$.
If your $f'(a)$ is not a unit fraction on a root question, you picked the wrong $a$.
Verified on all ten entries of Family 0.

**3. The conversion, which is the whole of part (a)'s difficulty.**

$$1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.01745 \qquad 2^\circ = \frac{\pi}{90} \approx 0.03491$$

Not converting gives $\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}(2) = -1.2321$.
That is $5701$ times the method's own error, and a cosine is never below $-1$, so the check is free.

**4. Concavity, and the fact that it does not care which way $dx$ points.**

| $f''$ on the interval | shape | tangent sits | estimate is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f'' < 0$ | concave down | above | too **big** |
| $f'' > 0$ | concave up | below | too **small** |

Both of this question's functions are concave down where they are used, so **both parts overshoot**.
Confirmed on 48 root pairs and 54 cosine pairs, stepping both up and down, with convex controls ($x^2$, $1/x$, $e^x$, $\tan$) proving the same test reports the opposite.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Name $f$ for each part separately.** Two parts means two functions, and nothing carries over between them except the five moves.
> 2. **Choose the anchor $a$**: the nearest point where you know $f(a)$ exactly. On a trigonometric part that means a special angle; on a root part it means a perfect power.
> 3. **Convert if the part is trigonometric.** $dx$ goes into radians before anything else touches it.
> 4. **Write $dx = \text{target} - a$**, keeping the sign.
> 5. **Differentiate once, evaluate at $a$**, and form $dy = f'(a)\,dx$.
> 6. **Add**: $f(a) + dy$.
> 7. **Round to the number of places the stem names, padding with zeros if the estimate is short.** $4.05$ is written $4.0500$.
> 8. **State the direction** from the sign of $f''$. It is one sentence and it is the only line that shows you know what an approximation is.

Step 7 is Q12.3's own step and no course file contains it.
Step 3 is the step Q12.2 exists to teach.
Steps 1 to 6 and 8 are Q12.1.

## 4. Q12.3 worked four ways

### Route A - differentials, the wording the stem names

**Part (a).**
$f(x) = \cos x$, $a = \pi/3$, $dx = 2^\circ = \pi/90$.
$f'(x) = -\sin x$, so $f'(\pi/3) = -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
$dy = -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{90} = -\dfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{180}$.
$\cos 62^\circ \approx \dfrac12 - \dfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{180} = 0.46977001 \to \mathbf{0.4698}$.

**Part (b).**
$f(x) = \sqrt x$, $a = 16$, $dx = 0.4$.
$f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$, so $f'(16) = \dfrac18$.
$dy = \dfrac18 \cdot \dfrac25 = \dfrac1{20}$.
$\sqrt{16.4} \approx 4 + \dfrac1{20} = \dfrac{81}{20} = \mathbf{4.0500}$.

### Route B - linearization

Identical arithmetic with an extra $(x - a)$ carried along.
$L(x) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{3}\right)$ evaluated at $x = \tfrac{31\pi}{90}$, and $L(x) = 4 + \tfrac18(x - 16)$ evaluated at $x = 16.4$.
Both reproduce Route A to the last bit.
Worth it only if the examiner asks for several targets off one anchor.

### Route C - the structural routes, one per part

Part (a) has the **angle-sum route**, and it is the one that explains *why* the method works:
$$\cos(60^\circ + 2^\circ) = \cos 60^\circ \cos 2^\circ - \sin 60^\circ \sin 2^\circ$$
Now put $\cos 2^\circ \approx 1$ and $\sin 2^\circ \approx dx$, and you get $\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}dx$ exactly.
**Dropping $\cos dx$ to 1 is precisely dropping the quadratic term**, so this route shows you where the error lives rather than merely producing it.
Keeping $\cos dx$ instead recovers the true value to $10^{-9}$, which is the proof.

Part (b) has the **binomial route**:
$$\sqrt{16.4} = 4\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{0.4}{16}} \approx 4\left(1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.025\right) = 4.05$$
Same number, and it generalises to percentage-change wording.

### Route D - one Newton step, which is a check rather than an answer

Part (b) only.
$$\tfrac12\left(4 + \tfrac{16.4}{4}\right) = \tfrac12(4 + 4.1) = 4.05$$
This uses none of the differential algebra, so agreement is real evidence.
**Part (a) has no such route**, because inverting a cosine is not arithmetic.

### Route comparison

| Route | Parts it serves | Steps | Where it goes wrong | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| A differentials | both | 4 | none, and the stem names it | **use this one under pressure** |
| B linearization | both | 5 | an extra $(x-a)$ to mis-sign | when several targets share an anchor |
| C angle-sum / binomial | one each | 5 | needs the identity recalled | use to *explain*, or for percentage wording |
| D Newton step | (b) only | 4 | must invert to $x^2 = 16.4$ | use as a **check** |
| Calculator | both | 1 | the stem says "method of differentials" | **scores zero, and gives a different number** |

**Use Route A on both parts and Route D to check part (b).**
Note the last row carefully: on this question the calculator does not merely lose method marks, it produces a **different answer** from the one being marked.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

There is **no published marking scheme** for this course.
Zero occurrences of "rubric", "marking scheme" or "part marks" across all four course files, re-verified this run for the seventh consecutive time.
So what follows is inference about which sentences make the process visible, not a claim about points.

The stem says **"(show the process)"**, which is the only place on F1 that phrase appears, so the working is being asked for explicitly.

> **(a)** Let $f(x) = \cos x$ and take $a = 60^\circ = \pi/3$, since $\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12$ exactly and $60^\circ$ is the nearest special angle to $62^\circ$.
> Then $dx = 2^\circ = \pi/90$ radians.
> $f'(x) = -\sin x$, so $f'(a) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
> $dy = f'(a)\,dx = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{90} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{180}$.
> Therefore $\cos 62^\circ \approx \tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{180} \approx 0.4698$.
> Since $f''(x) = -\cos x < 0$ on this interval, $f$ is concave down, so the tangent line lies above the curve and this estimate is slightly too large.
>
> **(b)** Let $f(x) = \sqrt x$ and take $a = 16$, since $\sqrt{16} = 4$ exactly and $16$ is the nearest perfect square to $16.4$.
> Then $dx = 0.4$.
> $f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$, so $f'(16) = \tfrac18$.
> $dy = \tfrac18(0.4) = \tfrac1{20}$.
> Therefore $\sqrt{16.4} \approx 4 + \tfrac1{20} = 4.0500$.
> Since $f''(x) = -\tfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0$, $f$ is concave down, so this estimate is slightly too large as well.

Twelve lines, six per part, and every one of them is short.
The line that most students skip is the second in each part, the one naming *why* that anchor.
The line that costs eight seconds and shows the most understanding is the last in each part.

**Do not write a sentence apologising for the fourth decimal.**
Write $0.4698$ and $4.0500$ and move on.
If you have room and want to show you know what you are doing, one clause is enough, and it should quote the bound rather than a place count: "the error is at most $\tfrac{|f''(a)|}{2}(dx)^2 \approx 3\times10^{-4}$, so only the first two or three decimals are reliable".
That is defensible on both parts.
A bare "correct to four decimal places" is not, and section 6.2 is why.

## 6. What the estimate is actually worth

This section is Q12.3's real content, and it is the reason the question is worth 8 points rather than 5.

### 6.1 The measured errors

| | Part (a), $\cos 62^\circ$ | Part (b), $\sqrt{16.4}$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Estimate | $0.46977001059609635$ | $4.05$ exactly |
| True value | $0.46947156278589075$ | $4.049691346263318$ |
| Error (estimate $-$ true) | $+2.9845\times10^{-4}$ | $+3.0865\times10^{-4}$ |
| Relative error | $6.357\times10^{-4}$ | $7.622\times10^{-5}$ |
| Direction | over | over |

Both true values were computed in 60-digit decimal arithmetic.
The cosine was confirmed a second way through the angle-sum identity and a third against the Pythagorean identity; the root was confirmed by squaring back and again by Newton's method.

**The two rankings disagree, and that is worth noticing.**
Part (b) has the **larger absolute** error and the **smaller relative** one.
Part (a) is $8.34$ times worse in relative terms.
If a question ever asks "which estimate is better", ask which kind of better it means.

### 6.2 How many decimal places are genuinely correct

Round both the estimate and the truth to $k$ places and ask whether the two strings agree.

| $k$ | (a) estimate | (a) true | agree? | (b) estimate | (b) true | agree? |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | :---: | ---: | ---: | :---: |
| 0 | 0 | 0 | yes | 4 | 4 | yes |
| 1 | 0.5 | 0.5 | yes | **4.1** | **4.0** | **no** |
| 2 | 0.47 | 0.47 | yes | 4.05 | 4.05 | yes |
| 3 | **0.470** | **0.469** | **no** | 4.050 | 4.050 | yes |
| 4 | **0.4698** | **0.4695** | **no** | **4.0500** | **4.0497** | **no** |

**Part (a) is genuinely correct to two decimal places. Part (b) to none at all.**

Part (b)'s row is the more interesting one, and it is the sharpest instance of a trap the queue has recorded twice before.
Its agreement profile is not nested: it agrees at 0, disagrees at 1, agrees again at 2 and 3, then disagrees at 4.
The cause is a **rounding tie**.
The estimate is *exactly* $4.05$, which rounds half-up to $4.1$, while the true $4.0497$ rounds to $4.0$.
So an honest count of "correct places" - the largest $k$ such that *every* $j \le k$ agrees - is **zero**, even though the number looks right to three places if you only check three.

**Never infer a place count from the size of the error.**
Part (b)'s error is larger than part (a)'s and its relative error is eight times smaller, and it scores zero where part (a) scores two.

### 6.3 So what does "to four decimal places" mean?

It can only mean **write four decimal places down**.

The census, measured this run rather than quoted:

| Catalog | Entries | Reaching four genuinely correct places |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| Square and cube roots off perfect powers | 9 | 2 |
| Trigonometric values off special angles | 8 | **0** |
| The drill set in section 10 | 12 | 4 |

Of the nine root entries, two score **zero** correct places, and both are rounding ties exactly like part (b).
Of the eight trigonometric entries, the best manages three, and $\cos 62^\circ$ - this question's own part (a) - is the **worst of the eight**.

The method is not incapable of four places.
Four of the twelve drills reach four or more.
What separates them is a single quantity.

> **The discriminator is $\dfrac{|f''(a)|\,(dx)^2}{2}$.**
> Every drill reaching four places has it below $5\times10^{-5}$.
> Both of Q12.3's own parts sit at about $3\times10^{-4}$, roughly ten times too coarse.

That is the honest sentence, and it takes one line to compute.

**Therefore: the marker's answer is the differential value.**
Write $0.4698$, not $0.4695$.
Write $4.0500$, not $4.0497$.
Correcting your answer toward the true value is the second most expensive thing you can do on this question, after forgetting the radians.

### 6.4 Could a better choice have closed the gap?

Tested, not assumed. No.

| Attempt | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| The best legal anchor for part (a) | $60^\circ$ already. Two places. |
| A **nearer** anchor, $61^\circ$ (not exactly known, so illegal) | $4.08\times$ better, and **still only two places** |
| The best legal anchor for part (b) | $16$ already |
| A nearer exact anchor, $16.81 = 4.1^2$ | gives the **identical** estimate $4.05$, so still zero places |
| Shrinking $dx$ | impossible; the targets $62^\circ$ and $16.4$ are given |
| Adding the quadratic Taylor term | reaches **five** places on both parts, $48\times$ and $80\times$ better, but it is a different method |

The $16.81$ row is a small piece of teaching gold.
$4.1 - \tfrac{0.41}{8.2} = 4.1 - 0.05 = 4.05$, exactly the same number the anchor at $16$ produced.
Two different anchors, one estimate, and the coincidence is exact in rational arithmetic.

So the stem is asking for a number the method cannot supply to the stated precision, on **both** parts.
It is asking you to write four digits.

## 7. Verify before you commit

### 7.1 The four-second checks

| Part | Check | Why it works |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | Is the answer in $[-1, 1]$? | this alone kills the degrees blunder, which gives $-1.2321$ |
| a | Is it a bit **below** $\cos 60^\circ = 0.5$? | you stepped further from $0^\circ$, so the cosine fell |
| a | Is the correction about $0.03$? | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{90} \approx 0.866 \times 0.0349$ |
| b | Is it between $4$ and $4.1$? | $4^2 = 16$ and $4.1^2 = 16.81$, and $16 < 16.4 < 16.81$ |
| b | **Square it back.** $\left(\tfrac{81}{20}\right)^2 = 16.4025$ | a little **above** $16.4$, confirming the overshoot, and it checks the value and the direction at once |

The squaring check is the best single check in the question and it exists only on part (b).
**The two parts need different self-checks**, which is worth knowing in advance because reaching for the wrong one wastes the thirty seconds you have.

### 7.2 The check that cannot fail

A spot-check at $dx = 0$ agrees with the truth for **five of the eight** standard wrong answers, because all five differ from the truth only by a factor multiplying $dy$, and $dy$ vanishes there.
The three that survive are exactly the ones corrupting the $f(a)$ term instead.
**Never sanity-check a linearization at its own anchor.**

The good news is specific to this question: at the exam's own numbers - $60^\circ$ with $dx = 2^\circ$, and $16$ with $dx = 0.4$ - **not one of the eight slips coincides with the truth**.
Q12.3's own values are a safe place to check yourself.

### 7.3 The error bound, settled

The queue has recorded this bound "violated" twice, in opposite directions, and closing category 12 is the right place to settle it.

The Lagrange remainder says
$$\text{true} - \text{estimate} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(dx)^2 \quad\text{for some } \xi \text{ strictly between } a \text{ and } a + dx.$$

**Neither endpoint is "the bound".**
If $f''$ keeps one sign and $|f''|$ is monotone on the interval, then because $\xi$ is strictly interior the error is strictly **bracketed**:

$$\frac{\min\big(|f''(a)|,\,|f''(b)|\big)}{2}(dx)^2 \;<\; |\text{error}| \;<\; \frac{\max\big(|f''(a)|,\,|f''(b)|\big)}{2}(dx)^2$$

The larger endpoint value is an upper bound.
The smaller one is a **lower** bound.

Every instance in category 12, recomputed here:

| Instance | $dx$ | $\lvert f''\rvert$ | Anchor value | Target value | Actual error | Upper bound is |
| :--- | ---: | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| Q12.1 $\sqrt{9.2}$ from 9 | $+0.2$ | falling | $1.8519\text{e-}4$ | $1.7918\text{e-}4$ | $1.8316\text{e-}4$ | anchor |
| Q12.1 $\sqrt{48.6}$ from 49 | $-0.4$ | falling | $5.8309\text{e-}5$ | $5.9030\text{e-}5$ | $5.8548\text{e-}5$ | **target** |
| Q12.2 $\sin 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | $+2^\circ$ | rising | $5.2761\text{e-}4$ | $5.3792\text{e-}4$ | $5.3110\text{e-}4$ | **target** |
| **Q12.3(a)** $\cos 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | $+2^\circ$ | falling | $3.0462\text{e-}4$ | $2.8602\text{e-}4$ | $2.9845\text{e-}4$ | anchor |
| **Q12.3(b)** $\sqrt{16.4}$ from 16 | $+0.4$ | falling | $3.1250\text{e-}4$ | $3.0114\text{e-}4$ | $3.0865\text{e-}4$ | anchor |

**In all five the error sits strictly between the two endpoint values.**
Nothing was ever violated.
A lower bound was read as an upper bound.

Two consequences, both measured:

- **The sign of $dx$ does not decide which endpoint is larger.** $\sqrt{9.2}$ steps up and $\sqrt{48.6}$ steps down on the *same* falling $|f''|$, and the larger endpoint differs between them.
- **What decides it is whether $|f''|$ grows along the direction of travel.** Four instances, four confirmations.

The bracket was checked on 56 root pairs, 54 sine pairs and 54 cosine pairs, and it held on all 164.
It has a limit, and it is seeded: it needs $|f''|$ **monotone and of one sign** on the interval.
An interval straddling $90^\circ$, where $|\sin|$ has an interior maximum, exceeds *both* endpoint values at once, and only the interior maximum $|f''| = 1$ bounds it.
An interval straddling an inflection breaks it too.
Neither of Q12.3's intervals does either thing.

**The rule to carry into the exam:** take $M = \max|f''|$ over the whole closed interval, and quote $\dfrac{M}{2}(dx)^2$.
On this question that maximum sits at the **anchor** for both parts, so the anchor bound is the one to write:
$$|\text{error}_a| \le \frac{|\cos 60^\circ|}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{90}\right)^2 = 3.05\times10^{-4} \qquad |\text{error}_b| \le \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4\cdot 64}\,(0.4)^2 = 3.125\times10^{-4}$$
Both hold, and both are within 2 percent of the observed error, so they are worth quoting.

### 7.4 The two points inside the interval

Both were found by root-finding rather than assumed, which is what makes them evidence.

| Part | Mean value theorem names | Taylor's Lagrange remainder names |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | $\xi = 60.99475^\circ$ | $\eta = 60.66775^\circ$ |
| b | $\xi = 16.19938$ | $\eta = 16.13265$ |

They are different points, and both are strictly interior, which is exactly *why* the bracket in 7.3 is strict.
Note the contrast with Q12.2: there the mean-value point sat $0.0016^\circ$ **above** the midpoint of the interval, and here it sits $0.0052^\circ$ **below** it.
"The midpoint" is a heuristic, not a theorem.

| $\sqrt[3]{8.3}$ | 8 | 2 | $1/12$ | $+0.3$ | $81/40$ | - |
| $\sqrt[3]{26.8}$ | 27 | 3 | $1/27$ | $-0.2$ | $404/135$ | - |
| $(32.2)^{1/5}$ | 32 | 2 | $1/80$ | $+0.2$ | $801/400$ | - |
| $(15.9)^{1/4}$ | 16 | 2 | $1/32$ | $-0.1$ | $639/320$ | - |

**Read this as structure, not as ten facts.**
Every $f'(a)$ is a unit fraction.
Every entry overshoots, in both directions of travel, because a root is concave down everywhere.
And the correct-place column has **no relationship to how tidy the row looks**: $\sqrt{101}$ scores zero and $\sqrt{48.6}$ scores four.

### Family 1 - a trigonometric value near a special angle, given in degrees

**Trigger.** $\sin$ or $\cos$ of a whole number of degrees within a few degrees of $0, 30, 45, 60, 90$.
**Tool.** Anchor at the special angle **in radians**, convert $dx$, then $f(a) + f'(a)\,dx$.
**This is Q12.3(a) and Q12.2.**

| Target | $a$ | Exact estimate | Correct places |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\cos 62^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3\pi}{180}$ | **2** |
| $\sin 62^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{180}$ | 3 |
| $\cos 31^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} - \tfrac{\pi}{360}$ | 3 |
| $\sin 29^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3\pi}{360}$ | 3 |
| $\cos 44^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{180}\right)$ | 3 |
| $\sin 46^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\left(1 + \tfrac{\pi}{180}\right)$ | 3 |
| $\cos 58^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3\pi}{180}$ | 3 |
| $\sin 91^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $1$ | - |

Every one of the eight overshoots, because $\sin$ and $\cos$ are both concave down on the first quadrant.
**Not one reaches four correct places**, and Q12.3's own $\cos 62^\circ$ is the worst of the eight.

Two rows are worth staring at.
$\cos 44^\circ$ and $\sin 46^\circ$ have the same exact estimate, because reflecting through $45^\circ$ maps one problem onto the other.
$\sin 91^\circ$ has $f'(90^\circ) = 0$, so the estimate is just "1" with no work at all - cheap, and the least informative row in the table.

### Family 2 - the other four trigonometric functions

**Trigger.** $\tan$, $\cot$, $\sec$ or $\csc$ near a special angle.
**Tool.** Same five moves, different derivative - and **the direction flips**.

| Target | $a$ | $f'(a)$ | Estimate | Direction |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $\tan 46^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $\sec^2 = 2$ | $1 + \tfrac{\pi}{90}$ | **under** |
| $\tan 44^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $2$ | $1 - \tfrac{\pi}{90}$ | **under** |
| $\sec 61^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\sec\tan = 2\sqrt3$ | $2 + \tfrac{2\sqrt3\,\pi}{180}$ | **under** |
| $\csc 31^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $-\csc\cot = -2\sqrt3$ | $2 - \tfrac{2\sqrt3\,\pi}{180}$ | **under** |
| $\cot 46^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $-\csc^2 = -2$ | $1 - \tfrac{\pi}{90}$ | **under** |

All five are **concave up** on $(0, \pi/2)$, so all five undershoot.
Note $\tan 44^\circ$ and $\tan 46^\circ$: one steps down and one steps up and **both undershoot**.
Convexity fixes the direction; the sign of $dx$ has no say, and neither does the sign of $f'$ - $\cot$ and $\csc$ have negative slopes and still undershoot.

### Family 3 - powers, reciprocals and mixed exponents

**Trigger.** $(a + dx)^p$ for any real $p$.
**Tool.** $f' = p\,a^{p-1}$. Direction from the sign of $p(p-1)$.

| Target | $a$ | $f'(a)$ | Estimate | Direction |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $(2.01)^5$ | 2 | 80 | $164/5$ | under |
| $(1.97)^3$ | 2 | 12 | $191/25$ | under |
| $1/1.002$ | 1 | $-1$ | $499/500$ | under |
| $(8.06)^{2/3}$ | 8 | $1/3$ | $201/50$ | **over** |
| $(1.02)^{10}$ | 1 | 10 | $6/5$ | under |

$p > 1$ is convex and undershoots; $0 < p < 1$ is concave and overshoots, exactly like a root; $p < 0$ on $x>0$ is convex and undershoots.

**$(1.02)^{10}$ is the row that kills "a small $dx$ means a good estimate".**
Its step is $0.02$ and it is still 1.6 percent low, because $f''(1) = 90$.
The error is $\tfrac12 f''(dx)^2$, and $f''$ gets a vote.

### Family 4 - "estimate to $N$ decimal places"

**Trigger.** Any precision demand attached to the stem. **This is Q12.3's own family and no course file covers it.**
**Tool.** Compute the estimate, round it to $N$ places padding with zeros, and separately compute $\tfrac{|f''(a)|}{2}(dx)^2$ to say what it is worth.

| What is asked | What you write for part (b) | Is it right? |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| to 1 place | 4.1 | no - the truth is 4.0 |
| to 2 places | 4.05 | yes |
| to 3 places | 4.050 | yes |
| to 4 places | 4.0500 | no - the truth is 4.0497 |

> **The rule.** What you write is the **estimate**, padded with zeros if it is short.
> $4.05$ becomes $4.0500$, never $4.0497$.
> Then, if there is room, one clause about what it is worth.

### Family 5 - "is your estimate too big or too small? justify"

**Trigger.** The direction is asked, with or without the number.
**Tool.** The sign of $f''$ on the interval, and one sentence about the tangent line.

| $f$ | $f''$ | Estimate is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt x$, $\sqrt[3]{x}$, $x^p$ for $0<p<1$, $\ln x$ | $<0$ | too big |
| $\sin x$, $\cos x$ on the first quadrant | $<0$ | too big |
| $x^n$ for $n>1$, $e^x$ | $>0$ | too small |
| $1/x$ on $x>0$, $\tan$, $\sec$, $\csc$, $\cot$ on $(0,\pi/2)$ | $>0$ | too small |

Answer it with the tangent line, never with the arithmetic:
"$f'' < 0$ here, so $f$ is concave down, so the tangent line lies above the graph, so the estimate exceeds the true value."
That sentence works for negative $dx$ too, and it works without computing anything.

**Four of the eight standard functions overshoot and four undershoot**, so "linearization overestimates" is a coin flip as a rule.

### Family 6 - bound the error

**Trigger.** "How accurate is your estimate", "bound the error", or a precision demand you want to justify.
**Tool.** $|\text{error}| \le \dfrac{M}{2}(dx)^2$ with $M = \max|f''|$ over the **whole interval**.

| Instance | Max endpoint | Bound | Actual | Ratio |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\sqrt{16.4}$ from 16 | anchor | $3.1250\text{e-}4$ | $3.0865\text{e-}4$ | 0.988 |
| $\cos 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | anchor | $3.0462\text{e-}4$ | $2.9845\text{e-}4$ | 0.980 |
| $\sqrt{9.2}$ from 9 | anchor | $1.8519\text{e-}4$ | $1.8316\text{e-}4$ | 0.989 |
| $\sqrt{48.6}$ from 49 | **target** | $5.9030\text{e-}5$ | $5.8548\text{e-}5$ | 0.992 |

The bound is tight - within 2 percent every time - so quote it.
Row four is the one that needs the "whole interval" wording: the step goes **down** a falling $|f''|$, so the maximum sits at the target.
See section 7.3 for why the *other* endpoint is not a failed bound but a working lower one.

### Family 7 - a two-part question whose parts disagree

**Trigger.** Two lettered parts under one stem, which is exactly Q12.3's shape.
**Tool.** Treat them as two independent questions that happen to share five moves.

Q12.3's own two parts **do** agree: both are concave-down functions and both overshoot.
That is a coincidence of this stem, not a rule.

| A pairing like | Part 1 | Part 2 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{16.4}$ and $\tan 46^\circ$ | over | **under** |
| $\sqrt{16.4}$ and $(2.01)^5$ | over | **under** |
| $\cos 62^\circ$ and $\sec 61^\circ$ | over | **under** |

The parts also need **different self-checks**.
Part (b) squares back; part (a) has no free check of that kind, because $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ needs the sine too.
Reaching for the wrong check is a real way to waste the last thirty seconds.

### Family 8 - differentials as a change rather than a value

**Trigger.** "By approximately how much does ... change", or an error-propagation stem.
**Tool.** $dy = f'(a)\,dx$ **alone**. Do not add $f(a)$.

| Question | $dy$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| Change in $\sqrt x$ from 16 to 16.4 | $0.05$ |
| Change in $\cos$ from $60^\circ$ to $62^\circ$ | $-\tfrac{\sqrt3\pi}{180} \approx -0.0302$ |
| Change in a circle's area, $r = 10$, $dr = 0.1$ | $2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi$ |
| Change in a cube's volume, $s = 5$, $ds = 0.02$ | $3s^2\,ds = 1.5$ |

For a power, the **relative** change is clean: $\dfrac{dy}{y} = n\,\dfrac{dx}{x}$.
Reporting $dy$ where the value was wanted gives $0.05$ on Q12.3(b), which the magnitude check kills instantly.

## 10. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks.

1. **Not converting degrees to radians on part (a).** Gives $-1.2321$, which is $5701$ times the method's own error. The tell is free: a cosine is never below $-1$. This is the most expensive single mistake in category 12 and it is available on exactly one of the two parts.

2. **Writing the true value.** $0.4695$ and $4.0497$ are the *true* values, not the estimates. The question asked for the differential estimate, and the marker's answer is $0.4698$ and $4.0500$. Section 6 is entirely about this.

3. **Evaluating $f'$ at the target instead of the anchor.** The error it produces is about the same size as the correct answer's error and points the other way, so it looks completely plausible - and it becomes invisible as $dx \to 0$, so shrinking the step cannot expose it.

4. **Not padding to the requested number of places.** $4.05$ where $4.0500$ was asked for. Free marks, and it costs one keystroke.

5. **Carrying part (a)'s setup into part (b).** They share nothing but the five moves. Different $f$, different anchor, different units.

6. **Choosing a "prettier" anchor rather than a better one.** See section 11 - this is subtler than Q12.1 made it look.

7. **Spot-checking at the anchor.** $dx = 0$ hides five of the eight standard slips. Check at the exam's own numbers instead, where none of the eight hides.

8. **Reporting $dy$ instead of $f(a) + dy$.** Answers $-0.0302$ and $0.05$. The magnitude check catches it instantly.

9. **Getting the direction backwards** by reasoning from the sign of $dx$ rather than from the sign of $f''$. Both parts step *up* and both overshoot; $\sqrt{24.6}$ steps *down* and also overshoots.

10. **Assuming both parts lean the same way.** Here they do. Change part (a) to a tangent or part (b) to a cube and they do not.

11. **The invisible ones.** For a root, $f'(a) = 1$ exactly at $a = 1/4$, so on a question anchored there, forgetting to multiply by $f'(a)$ is undetectable. For a cosine, $f'(0) = 0$, so at that anchor a sign flip, a doubled correction and a target-for-$dx$ slip are all bit-identical to the truth. Neither hiding point is $16$ or $60^\circ$, so Q12.3 itself is clean - but the reworded version might not be.

## 11. Choosing the anchor, and the rule that finally survives

Q12.1 concluded "nearest exact point wins, always".
Q12.2 refuted that on a trigonometric question.
**Q12.3 refutes it on both of its parts, including the root family the rule was drawn from.**

### 11.1 Part (a): ranking the nine legal anchors for $\cos 62^\circ$

| Anchor | Degrees away | Times worse than $60^\circ$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $60^\circ$ | 2 | 1.00 |
| $90^\circ$ | 28 | **64.40** |
| $45^\circ$ | 17 | **93.26** |
| $120^\circ$ | 58 | **310.95** |
| $30^\circ$ | 32 | **393.04** |
| $135^\circ$ | 73 | 923.65 |
| $0^\circ$ | 62 | 1777.63 |
| $150^\circ$ | 88 | 1901.68 |
| $180^\circ$ | 118 | 4923.71 |

**Two inversions.**
$90^\circ$ is eleven degrees *further* than $45^\circ$ and $1.45$ times better.
$120^\circ$ is twenty-six degrees further than $30^\circ$ and better again.

The mechanism is the corrected rule: the error goes as $\dfrac{|f''(a)|(dx)^2}{2}$, and here $f'' = -\cos$, so an anchor with a small $|\cos a|$ buys back distance.
$|\cos 90^\circ| = 0$ against $|\cos 45^\circ| = 0.707$.

**Note the reversal from Q12.2.**
There $f'' = -\sin$, so a small $|\sin a|$ helped and $30^\circ$ beat $90^\circ$.
Here $f'' = -\cos$, so a small $|\cos a|$ helps and $90^\circ$ beats $30^\circ$.
The *same* rule, applied to a different second derivative, points the other way - which is why the rule has to be the thing you remember, not the ranking.

One caution, measured: the quadratic model predicts $90^\circ$ would be **perfect**, because $\cos 90^\circ = 0$ kills the quadratic term entirely.
The real error there is $1.9\times10^{-2}$.
The cubic term takes over and predicts it to within 1.2 percent.
So the quadratic model is wrong by twelve orders of magnitude at that one anchor, which is exactly why "nearest" stays the working rule and $|f''|$ is a tie-breaker rather than a replacement.

### 11.2 Part (b): the same inversion, on the root family

| Anchor | Distance from 16.4 | Times worse than 16 |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 16 | 0.4 | 1.00 |
| 25 | 8.6 | **292.59** |
| 9 | 7.4 | **594.98** |
| 36 | 19.6 | 1026.96 |
| 49 | 32.6 | 2014.35 |
| 4 | 12.4 | 3402.87 |
| 100 | 83.6 | 5735.58 |
| 1 | 15.4 | 15066.43 |

**25 is further from 16.4 than 9 is, and it is twice as good.**
So are 36 and 49 compared with 4, and so is 100 compared with 1.

The mechanism is identical: $|f''| = \dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}$ **falls** as $x$ grows, so a larger anchor is more forgiving.
Q12.1's own census only ranked anchors close together, where $|f''|$ barely varies, which is precisely why it saw distance and error agree and drew the wrong rule.

### 11.3 The rule that survives both

> **Minimise $|f''(a)|\,(dx)^2$.**
> "Nearest" is the special case that holds when $|f''|$ is roughly constant across the candidate anchors, which is most of the time and is why it is still the right thing to do at speed.
> Reach for $|f''|$ only to break a tie, or when one candidate is dramatically flatter than another.

And the practical version, which is what you actually do in the exam:
**take the nearest anchor where $f(a)$ is exact**, and if two candidates are close in distance, take the flatter one.
On both of Q12.3's parts that gives the right answer, $60^\circ$ and $16$, and both are also the nearest.

### Answer key

| 12 | $\tfrac{51}{100}$ | 0.5100 | over | 3 | F0 trap, $f'(a) = 1$ exactly |

**Question 7 is the one to study.**
At $a = 90^\circ$ the second derivative $f'' = -\cos(90^\circ)$ is exactly **zero**, so the concavity test says nothing at all.
The direction is decided by the *cubic* term instead: $\cos(90^\circ - h) = \sin h = h - h^3/6$, which is less than $h$, so the estimate overshoots.
Step the *other* way from $90^\circ$ and the same estimate undershoots.
An inflection is the only place in this whole catalog where the sign of $dx$ decides the direction.

**Question 12 is the invisible trap.**
$f'(1/4) = 1$ exactly, so a student who forgets to multiply by $f'(a)$ writes $0.5 + 0.01 = 0.51$ and is right for the wrong reason.
Nothing catches this except doing it correctly.

**Score magnitude and direction separately.**
They are different failure modes needing different fixes.
A wrong **magnitude** means you mis-differentiated or mis-anchored: go back to Family 0 or 3.
A wrong **direction** means you reasoned from the sign of $dx$: go back to Family 5, which is a five-minute fix.

Eight of the twelve overshoot and four undershoot, so answering "over" every time scores 8 out of 12.
That looks like competence and is not, which is exactly why the two scores are kept apart.

**And read the place-count column across.**
It runs $0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5$.
Four of the twelve *do* reach four correct decimal places.
What separates them is $\tfrac{|f''(a)|(dx)^2}{2}$: below $5\times10^{-5}$ they reach four, and Q12.3's own two parts sit ten times higher.

## 13. Closing category 12

### 13.1 One procedure, three questions

All three are the same five moves.

```
NAME f       ->  CHOOSE a (nearest point where f(a) is EXACT)
             ->  CONVERT if trigonometric        <- Q12.2's contribution
             ->  dx = target - a  (keep the sign)
             ->  dy = f'(a) dx    (f' at the ANCHOR)
             ->  answer = f(a) + dy
             ->  round to what was asked, padding with zeros
             ->  direction from the SIGN OF f''  <- Q12.1's contribution
             ->  say what it is worth            <- Q12.3's contribution
```

### 13.2 Which question teaches what

| | Q12.1 | Q12.2 | Q12.3 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Paper and item | MT1 item 9 | MT2 item 8 | **F1 item 2** |
| Points | 5 | 6 | **8** |
| Stem wording | "use differentials" | "use linearization" | "the method of differentials" |
| Target | $\sqrt{9.2}$ | $\sin 62^\circ$ | $\cos 62^\circ$ **and** $\sqrt{16.4}$ |
| Its one idea | the anchor is the question | the unit is the question | **what the answer is worth** |
| What it adds | $f'$ at the anchor; direction from $f''$ | radians; the anchor rule needs $\lvert f''\rvert$ | precision; two parts; the error bound settled |
| Extra demand in the stem | none | none | **"(show the process)"** |

Nineteen points across three papers, and the three stems use three different wordings for one formula.

### 13.3 The three things the category corrected about itself

- **"Nearest anchor wins, always" is false.** Q12.1 stated it, Q12.2 refuted it on trigonometry, and Q12.3 refutes it on roots too. The surviving rule is: minimise $|f''(a)|(dx)^2$, of which "nearest" is the common special case.
- **The error bound was never violated.** Two runs recorded an "anchor bound violated" with the inequality pointing opposite ways. Both were a **lower** bound read as an upper one. The true statement brackets the error strictly between the two endpoint values of $\tfrac{|f''|}{2}(dx)^2$, whenever $f''$ keeps one sign and $|f''|$ is monotone.
- **"To four decimal places" means write four digits.** The method can reach four places, and does on four of twelve drills. It cannot reach four on either of the two parts F1 actually asks for.

### 13.4 The one-page decision tree

```
"estimate / approximate / use differentials / use linearization"
    |
    +-- A NUMBER, or a CHANGE?
    |     +-- a CHANGE     ->  dy = f'(a) dx alone            (Family 8)
    |     +-- a PERCENT    ->  dy/y = n dx/x for a power      (Family 8)
    |     +-- a NUMBER     ->  continue
    |
    +-- Is the argument an ANGLE IN DEGREES?
    |     +-- YES  ->  CONVERT dx to radians FIRST.  Anchor at a
    |     |            special angle.  Check the answer is in [-1,1].
    |     +-- NO   ->  anchor at the nearest perfect power / round base.
    |                  f'(a) should come out a UNIT FRACTION.
    |
    +-- dy = f'(a) dx   ...   f' AT THE ANCHOR, never at the target
    +-- answer = f(a) + dy
    |
    +-- DIRECTION:  sign of f''  (not the sign of dx)
    |     f'' < 0 concave down -> tangent above -> TOO BIG
    |     f'' > 0 concave up   -> tangent below -> TOO SMALL
    |     f'' = 0 at the anchor -> the cubic decides, and dx's sign
    |                              does matter there
    |
    +-- HOW MANY PLACES?
    |     write the ESTIMATE to the requested places, padding zeros.
    |     NEVER write the true value.
    |     worth = |f''| dx^2 / 2, with |f''| the MAX over the interval.
    |     below 5e-5 -> four places are safe.  Around 3e-4 -> two or
    |     three, and say so in a clause.
    |
    +-- CHECK
          root  -> square it back; a little high confirms the overshoot
          trig  -> is it in [-1,1], and did the value move the right way
          both  -> DO NOT spot-check at dx = 0.  Use the stem's own
                   numbers, where none of the eight standard slips hides.
          then PREVIEW.
```
