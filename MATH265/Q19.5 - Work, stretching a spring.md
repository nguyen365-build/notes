# Q19.5 - Work, stretching a spring

**The question (Final Exam 2, item 9, 6 points).**
"Assume that $20$ ft-lb of work is required to stretch a spring $1$ ft beyond its natural length.
a. What is the spring constant?
b. How much work is required to stretch the spring $2$ ft beyond its natural length?"

**The answer.** (a) $k = 40$ lb/ft. (b) $W = 80$ ft-lb.

**What to type into Möbius:** `40` for part a, `80` for part b.

## 0. The 30-second version

```
Hooke:  F(x) = k x,  x measured from the NATURAL length
step 1  find k from the given work:   W1 = (1/2) k a1^2   ->  k = 2 W1 / a1^2
step 2  integrate the force over the path:  W = int_b^a k x dx = (1/2) k (a^2 - b^2)

Q19.5:  k = 2(20) / 1^2 = 40 lb/ft
        W = int_0^2 40 x dx = 20 x^2 from 0 to 2 = 80 ft-lb
```

Two lines of arithmetic, and both of them are step 1.
If you get $k$ wrong, part b is wrong by exactly that factor, because $W$ is linear in $k$.

## 1. What this question is really testing

Q19.4 asked you to build an integrand out of a picture of a chain.
This one asks you to build an integrand out of a physical law, and it is the mirror image of the chain.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| Read $x$ as the extension from the natural length | every spring question ever set, and it is the classic lost mark |
| **Find a constant from a data point before using it** | Q19.7 (find $k$ from an average value), and any "given that ..., find ..." stem |
| Recognise that work is $\int F\,dx$, not $F \times d$ | Q19.4, Q19.8, Q19.9 |
| Keep force units and mass units apart | Q19.4 in reverse, where the units are metric and $g$ is required |
| Sanity-check against a bracket rather than a sign | every work question, because work as asked here is unsigned |

**The one sentence.**
Q19.4 had a constant force per slice and a distance that varied from slice to slice; Q19.5 has a force that varies along the path while every part of the spring moves through the same $dx$, so the two questions are the same integral with the varying factor swapped.

### The mirror, stated precisely

Both answers are $\int (\text{force}) \times (\text{distance})$.
What differs is which factor is the function.

| | Q19.4, the chain | Q19.5, the spring |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Slicing variable | height $y$ of a slice of chain | extension $x$ along the stretch |
| Force factor | $\lambda g\,dy = 78.4\,dy$ N, the **same for every slice** | $F(x) = 40x$ lb, **different at every $x$** |
| Distance factor | $y$ metres, **different for every slice** | $dx$, the **same for every element** |
| What the integral sums | pieces of the object | pieces of the path |
| Answer | $1411.2$ J | $80$ ft-lb |

This was measured rather than asserted.
Differentiating each factor with respect to its own slicing variable: for the chain the force factor has derivative $0$ and the distance factor has derivative $1$; for the spring the force factor has derivative $40$ and the displacement element has derivative $0$.
Exactly one factor varies in each problem, and it is a different one.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2a. Hooke's law, and what $x$ is

$$F(x) = kx$$

$x$ is the **extension beyond the natural length**, not the length of the spring.

This is the single most expensive misreading in the topic, so state it as an equation before you use it:

$$x = (\text{current length}) - (\text{natural length})$$

Q19.5 never gives you a natural length, which is deliberate and worth noticing: the stem says "1 ft beyond its natural length" and "2 ft beyond its natural length", so both numbers are already extensions and the subtraction has been done for you.
A variation that gives lengths instead of extensions is Family 3 below, and it is where the mark is actually lost.

### 2b. Work is an integral because the force varies

For a constant force along a straight path, work is force times distance.
Hooke's force is not constant, so:

$$W = \int_b^a F(x)\,dx = \int_b^a kx\,dx = \frac{1}{2}k\left(a^2 - b^2\right)$$

The limits are **extensions**, and they are extensions even when the question is phrased in lengths.

### 2c. Find $k$ first, and know what its units are

$k$ has units of force per length.
Here the force unit is the pound, so $k$ is in **lb/ft**, and $k = 40$ lb/ft.

The formula that gets you there depends on what kind of datum you were given, and this is where most wrong answers are born:

| The datum you were given | $k$ from it | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **work** $W_1$ over extension $a_1$ | $k = \dfrac{2W_1}{a_1^{\,2}}$ | invert $W = \tfrac12 k a^2$ |
| **force** $F_1$ at extension $a_1$ | $k = \dfrac{F_1}{a_1}$ | invert $F = kx$ directly |
| **work** $W$ between $b$ and $a$ | $k = \dfrac{2W}{a^2 - b^2}$ | invert the general form |
| a **mass** $m$ hanging in equilibrium at $x$ | $k = \dfrac{mg}{x}$ | the weight is $mg$, not $m$ |

Q19.5 is the first row: $k = 2(20)/1^2 = 40$ lb/ft.

**$k$ is a multiplicative constant, so getting it wrong scales the whole answer.**
$W = \tfrac12 k a^2$ is linear in $k$, so a $k$ that is half the truth gives a part-b answer that is half the truth.
There is no partial recovery from a wrong $k$: the error passes through the integral untouched.

### 2d. The pound is a force, so $g$ must not appear

This is Q19.4's units rule running the other way.
Q19.4 gave a **mass** in kilograms, so $g = 9.8$ had to be inserted to turn it into a weight.
Q19.5 gives **work in ft-lb**, and the pound is already a force, so inserting $g = 32.2$ ft/s$^2$ anywhere is the same mistake reflected.

The numbers this produces are worth recognising on sight: multiplying by $32.2$ gives $2576$, and dividing by it gives $2.484472049689441$.
Neither is close to $80$, so this error cannot hide, which is unusual for this question.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Name the variable.** Write "$x$ = extension beyond the natural length, in feet" on the page. If the stem gives lengths, subtract the natural length now and write down both extensions.
> 2. **Classify the datum** you were handed, using the table in 2c, and compute $k$ with its units.
> 3. **Check $k$ against the stem.** Put it back into the work integral over the *given* extension and confirm you get the *given* work. This costs one line and it catches every wrong-formula error.
> 4. **Write the integral** with extension limits: $W = \int_b^a kx\,dx$.
> 5. **Evaluate**: $W = \tfrac12 k (a^2 - b^2)$.
> 6. **Bracket the answer**: it must lie strictly between $F(b)(a-b)$ and $F(a)(a-b)$, and for a linear force it lands exactly halfway.
> 7. **Check the units** are force times length, with no $g$ in an imperial problem and a $g$ in any metric problem whose datum is a mass.

### The classify table

| The stem says ... | You are in | The tool |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "$W_1$ of work stretches it $a_1$ beyond natural length; how much for $a_2$" | Family 0 | $k = 2W_1/a_1^2$, then $\tfrac12 k a_2^2$ |
| "the spring constant is $k$" | Family 1 | straight to $\tfrac12 k a^2$ |
| "**additional** work", or two nonzero extensions | Family 2 | $\tfrac12 k(a^2-b^2)$ |
| natural length plus two total **lengths** | Family 3 | subtract first, then Family 2 |
| "a force of $F$ holds it at $a$" | Family 4 | $k = F/a$ |
| newtons, joules, metres | Family 5 | identical, no $g$ |
| a **mass** in kg hanging on it | Family 6 | $k = mg/x$ |
| "compress" | Family 7 | same formula, magnitude of $x$ |
| "how far", "what extension" | Family 8 | invert: $a = \sqrt{2W/k}$ |
| work given between two nonzero extensions, $k$ asked | Family 9 | $k = 2W/(a^2-b^2)$ |
| a force law that is not $kx$ | Family 10 | integrate it; no shortcut survives |
| two springs | Family 11 | combine to an effective $k$ first |
| "energy stored", "work released" | Family 12 | same magnitude, mind the sign |
| any $F(x)$ at all | Family 13 | $\int F\,dx$ |
| a chain, a rope, a tank | Family 14 | the varying factor is the distance; see Q19.4 |

## 4. Q19.5 worked five ways

Every route below was evaluated numerically, and all five returned $80$ ft-lb.
Two of them do not need $k$ at all, which matters because $k$ is where the errors are.

### Route A - slice the path by extension (the standard route)

Let $x$ be the extension beyond the natural length.
To increase the extension from $x$ to $x + dx$ you push against $F(x) = kx$ through a distance $dx$, so $dW = kx\,dx$.

From the datum: $\displaystyle 20 = \int_0^1 kx\,dx = \frac{k}{2}$, so $k = 40$ lb/ft.

$$W = \int_0^2 40x\,dx = \Big[20x^2\Big]_0^2 = 80 \ \text{ft-lb}$$

### Route B - slice by total length, with the origin moved

This is the independent derivation of the integrand, and it is the check that matters on a modelling question.
Let $L_0$ be the natural length and let $\ell$ be the total length, so the extension is $\ell - L_0$ and Hooke's law reads $F = k(\ell - L_0)$.

$$W = \int_{L_0}^{L_0 + 2} k(\ell - L_0)\,d\ell$$

Substituting $u = \ell - L_0$ returns Route A exactly, so the answer cannot depend on $L_0$.
That was verified rather than argued: the integral was evaluated for $L_0 = 0$, $0.5$, $1$, $\sqrt{3}$, $3$, $7$, $12.5$, $100$ and $1000$, and every one returned $80$ ft-lb to within $10^{-10}$.

**The rejection arm is the point of the exercise.**
Forgetting the shift, that is integrating $\int_{L_0}^{L_0+2} k\ell\,d\ell$ with $L_0 = 1$, returns $160$ ft-lb.
That is exactly double, and it is the error Family 3 punishes.

### Route C - the antiderivative as stored energy

$U(x) = \tfrac12 kx^2$ is the potential energy, so the work is the difference:

$$W = U(2) - U(0) = \tfrac12(40)(4) - 0 = 80 \ \text{ft-lb}$$

### Route D - scale from the datum, never computing $k$

$W$ is proportional to $a^2$, so doubling the extension quadruples the work:

$$W(2) = W(1)\left(\frac{2}{1}\right)^2 = 20 \times 4 = 80 \ \text{ft-lb}$$

This is the fastest route on the page and it is immune to every $k$ error, because it never forms $k$.
The trap it invites is the linear version, $20 \times 2 = 40$, which is wrong.

### Route E - average force times displacement

$F$ is linear, so its average over $[0, 2]$ is its value at the midpoint:

$$\bar{F} = \tfrac12 k(a + b) = \tfrac12 (40)(2 + 0) = 40 \ \text{lb}, \qquad W = \bar{F}\,(a-b) = 40 \times 2 = 80 \ \text{ft-lb}$$

**This is exactly right here, not approximately right.** See section 6.

### Route comparison

| Route | Steps | Needs $k$ | Fails when | Speed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, integrate in extension | 3 | yes | never, if $x$ is read right | fast |
| B, integrate in total length | 4 | yes | you forget the shift | slow |
| C, antiderivative as energy | 2 | yes | never | fast |
| D, quadratic scaling | 1 | **no** | the force is not $kx$ | fastest |
| E, average force | 2 | yes | the force is not linear | fast |

**Use Route A under pressure, and use Route D as the eight-second check.**
Route A is what a marker expects to see and it generalises to every variation in the catalog.
Route D takes one multiplication and disagrees with Route A the moment your $k$ is wrong, which makes it the cheapest independent check available.
Route B is the one to reach for only when the stem hands you lengths rather than extensions.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Möbius marks the answer only, but write the method anyway: it is what makes the eight-second checks possible.

> Let $x$ be the extension of the spring beyond its natural length, in feet.
> By Hooke's law the force required is $F(x) = kx$ pounds.
>
> **(a)** The work to stretch from $0$ to $1$ ft is
> $\int_0^1 kx\,dx = \tfrac12 k$.
> Setting this equal to the given $20$ ft-lb gives $k = 40$ lb/ft.
>
> **(b)** The work to stretch from $0$ to $2$ ft is
> $\int_0^2 40x\,dx = \big[20x^2\big]_0^2 = 80$ ft-lb.

Three sentences, both parts, and the units on both answers.
The line "let $x$ be the extension beyond its natural length" is doing real work: it is the only sentence in the write-up that a marker can read as evidence you did not use the total length.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### Is the average-force shortcut exactly right here?

**Yes, exactly.**
$F(x) = kx$ is a linear function of $x$, and the mean value of a linear function over an interval is its value at the midpoint, which equals the mean of its endpoint values.
So

$$\frac{1}{a-b}\int_b^a kx\,dx = \frac{k(a+b)}{2} = \bar{F}$$

is an identity, not an approximation, and $\bar{F}(a-b) = \tfrac12 k(a^2-b^2)$ exactly.

This was tested rather than asserted, over 24 different intervals $[b, a]$ with $b$ from $0$ to $1.3$ and widths from $0.1$ to $5.5$.
The worst disagreement between the exact integral and the shortcut was $2.274 \times 10^{-13}$, which is roundoff.

**It stops being exact the moment the force stops being linear**, and that is the reason to know why it works rather than that it works:

| Force law on $[0,2]$ | Exact work | Endpoint-average shortcut | Midpoint shortcut |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $40x$ (this question) | $80$ | $80$ | $80$ |
| $40x^2$ | $106.666667$ | $160$ | $80$ |
| $40x + 5x^3$ | $100$ | $120$ | - |
| $40x + 6x^2$ | $96$ | $104$ | - |

Note the third column on the $40x^2$ row: the midpoint form accidentally returns $80$ there, which is the right answer to a different question.
That is a coincidence of these numbers and not a reason to trust it.

**The rule to carry.** The average-force shortcut is exact if and only if the force is an affine function of position, and it is the same theorem as Q19.4's centre-of-mass shortcut, which is exact if and only if the rise is linear in the slicing variable.
One theorem, two disguises.

### Why the answer is not force times distance

The force at the end of the stretch is $F(2) = 80$ lb and the distance is $2$ ft, so "force times distance" gives $160$ ft-lb.
That is exactly double the truth, and the factor of two is the whole content of the integral: you are averaging a force that ramped from $0$, not applying its final value throughout.

### Where the answer sits in its range

The trivial bounds are the smallest force times the distance and the largest force times the distance:

$$F(0)\times 2 = 0 \quad < \quad W = 80 \quad < \quad F(2) \times 2 = 160$$

The answer sits at exactly $0.5$ of that range, and it is at the midpoint **because the force is linear**.
That is not a general fact: for $F = 40x^2$ on the same interval the answer sits at $0.3333$ of its own range.

A tighter and still-mental bracket comes from two Riemann panels of width $1$ ft.
The lower sum is $F(0)(1) + F(1)(1) = 40$ and the upper sum is $F(1)(1) + F(2)(1) = 120$, so

$$40 < W < 120$$

and $80$ is comfortably inside.
That bracket does far more work than the trivial one, as section 9 measures.

### The check that is inert here, and why you should know that

Q19.1 and Q19.3 could be checked by "an area is positive" and "an amount is positive".
**Work, as this question asks for it, is unsigned, so there is no sign to check.**
Of the 20 wrong-answer candidates in section 9, 19 are strictly positive and 1 is zero.
A positivity check rejects **zero of them**.

This is Q19.4's finding confirmed on a second question of the same species: on a work problem the discriminator has to be a **magnitude bracket**, and the sign is worth nothing.

### Numbers worth memorising

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| $k$ | $40$ lb/ft |
| $W$ from $0$ to $1$ ft | $20$ ft-lb (the given datum) |
| $W$ from $0$ to $2$ ft | $80$ ft-lb (the answer) |
| $W$ from $1$ to $2$ ft | $60$ ft-lb (the other reading, section 7) |
| $W$ from $0$ to $3$ ft | $180$ ft-lb |
| $F(2)$ | $80$ lb |
| trivial bracket | $0 < W < 160$ |
| two-panel bracket | $40 < W < 120$ |
| the same spring in SI | $k = 583.756117$ N/m, $W = 108.465436$ J |
| the same spring in inch-pounds | $k = 3.3333333$ lb/in, $W = 960$ in-lb |

The consecutive one-foot intervals cost $20$, $60$ and $100$ ft-lb, in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5$.
That odd-number pattern is a property of $x^2$ and it is the fastest way to feel whether an answer is plausible.

## 8. The variation catalog

Fifteen families, derived from the mathematics rather than from the course files: `MATH265_Categorized.md` contains **zero** occurrences of "spring" and **zero** of "Hooke", so there was nothing to extend.
`MATH265_Review.md` does carry Hooke's law, which is the control confirming the search works.

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
There are only four distinct tools in the whole catalog: invert a datum to get $k$, integrate $kx$ between two extensions, subtract a natural length, and integrate something that is not $kx$.
Everything else is which datum you were handed.

### Family 0 - a work datum at one extension, another asked (this question)

**Trigger.** "$W_1$ of work is required to stretch it $a_1$ beyond its natural length. How much for $a_2$?"
**Tool.** $k = 2W_1/a_1^2$, then $W = \tfrac12 k a_2^2$. Or Route D: $W = W_1 (a_2/a_1)^2$.

| $W_1$, $a_1$ | $k$ | asked $a$ | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $20$ ft-lb, $1$ ft | $40$ lb/ft | $2$ ft | $80$ ft-lb |
| $20$ ft-lb, $1$ ft | $40$ | $3$ ft | $180$ ft-lb |
| $20$ ft-lb, $1$ ft | $40$ | $0.5$ ft | $5$ ft-lb |

Three answers, one structure: $W$ is $20a^2$, so the numbers are $20$ times a perfect square.
Quadrupling from $80$ to $320$ would need $a = 4$, not $a = 3$.

### Family 1 - $k$ given directly

**Trigger.** The stem states the spring constant.
**Tool.** Skip step 1 entirely.

| $k$ | extension | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $30$ lb/ft | $0 \to 3$ ft | $135$ ft-lb |
| $30$ lb/ft | $0 \to 1$ ft | $15$ ft-lb |
| $100$ N/m | $0 \to 0.3$ m | $4.5$ J |

### Family 2 - between two nonzero extensions

**Trigger.** "additional work", "further", or two nonzero extensions named.
**Tool.** $W = \tfrac12 k(a^2 - b^2)$. The limits are extensions, and $b \ne 0$.

| $k$ | from | to | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $40$ lb/ft | $1$ ft | $2$ ft | $60$ ft-lb |
| $40$ lb/ft | $2$ ft | $3$ ft | $100$ ft-lb |
| $30$ lb/ft | $1$ ft | $3$ ft | $120$ ft-lb |

For $k = 40$ the consecutive foot-intervals are $20$, $60$, $100$: an arithmetic progression with common difference $40 = k$.
That is because $\tfrac12 k(a^2 - (a-1)^2) = \tfrac12 k(2a-1)$.

### Family 3 - total lengths given, natural length must be subtracted

**Trigger.** A natural length, plus two lengths that are not extensions.
**Tool.** Subtract the natural length from both, *then* Family 2. This is the family the whole topic exists to test.

| Natural length | stretched from | to | $k$ | extensions | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| $8$ in | $10$ in | $14$ in | $24$ lb/ft | $\tfrac16$ ft, $\tfrac12$ ft | $\tfrac83 = 2.666667$ ft-lb |

Using the lengths $\tfrac{10}{12}$ and $\tfrac{14}{12}$ without subtracting gives $8$ ft-lb, wrong by a factor of exactly $3$.
**Watch the inches.** If $k$ is in lb/ft, every length in the integral has to be in feet.

### Family 4 - a force datum rather than a work datum

**Trigger.** "a force of $F$ pounds is required to hold it at $a$ ft".
**Tool.** $k = F/a$. There is no factor of $2$ and no square.

| datum | $k$ | asked | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $40$ lb holds it at $2$ ft | $20$ lb/ft | $0 \to 3$ ft | $90$ ft-lb |

**A collision to be aware of.** With these numbers, "$40$ lb at $2$ ft" and "$40$ ft-lb over $2$ ft" both give $k = 20$, so the two routes cannot be told apart here.
Change the numbers to "$40$ over $1$ ft" and they separate immediately: $40$ from the force route, $80$ from the work route.
So read the units on the datum; the arithmetic will not warn you.

### Family 5 - SI units, with a force datum

**Trigger.** Newtons, joules, metres, and no mass mentioned.
**Tool.** Identical formulas. No $g$, because the newton is already a force.

| datum | $k$ | asked | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $12$ J over $0.4$ m | $150$ N/m | $0 \to 0.8$ m | $48$ J |

Doubling the stretch quadrupled the work, $12 \to 48$, exactly as in Family 0.

### Family 6 - a mass datum, so $g$ is required

**Trigger.** "a $4$ kg mass hung from the spring stretches it $5$ cm".
**Tool.** The hanging mass is in equilibrium, so $kx = mg$ and $k = mg/x$. This is the only family where $g$ belongs.

| datum | $k$ | asked | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $4$ kg stretches it $0.05$ m | $784$ N/m | $0 \to 0.1$ m | $3.92$ J |

Omitting $g$ gives $k = 80$ N/m, wrong by exactly the factor $9.8$.
This is Q19.4's rule in its original direction, and it is the reason 2d matters: **the datum's units decide whether $g$ appears, not the country the question was written in.**

### Family 7 - compression

**Trigger.** "compress", "push in", "shorten".
**Tool.** The same formula with the magnitude of the displacement. Compressing by $d$ costs exactly what stretching by $d$ costs.

| $k$ | compression | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $50$ N/m | $0.2$ m | $1$ J |

A subtlety that is worth one line: the signed integral $\int_0^{-0.2} kx\,dx$ comes out **positive**, because both the force and $dx$ change sign.
So the formula does the right thing without your help, and the answer is $+1$ J rather than $-1$ J.

### Family 8 - the question run backwards

**Trigger.** "how far", "what extension", "to what length".
**Tool.** Invert: $a = \sqrt{2W/k}$ from natural length, or $a = \sqrt{b^2 + 2W/k}$ from extension $b$.

| $k$ | $W$ | extension |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $40$ lb/ft | $45$ ft-lb | $1.5$ ft |

Check it forwards: $\tfrac12 (40)(1.5)^2 = 45$.
Forgetting the $2$ gives $\sqrt{45/40} = 1.0606602$, which is not a round number, and that is itself a warning sign.

### Family 9 - $k$ from a work over a nonzero interval

**Trigger.** The work between two nonzero extensions is given and $k$ is asked.
**Tool.** $k = 2W/(a^2 - b^2)$. Note $a^2 - b^2$, not $(a-b)^2$.

| datum | $k$ |
| :--- | ---: |
| $60$ ft-lb from $1$ ft to $2$ ft | $40$ lb/ft |

Using $2W/a^2$ gives $30$; using $2W/(a-b)^2$ gives $120$.
This family recovers exactly this question's spring, which is the neatest way to see that Family 2 and Family 9 are the same equation solved for different unknowns.

### Family 10 - a nonlinear force law

**Trigger.** The force is not proportional to $x$: a hardening spring, a stiff rubber band, a stated $F(x)$.
**Tool.** Integrate. Every shortcut in this document dies here.

| $F(x)$ | interval | exact $W$ | shortcut | gap |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $40x + 6x^2$ | $0 \to 2$ | $96$ | $104$ | $8$ |
| $40x + 5x^3$ | $0 \to 2$ | $100$ | $120$ | $20$ |

The shortcut is the endpoint-average form.
It overestimates in both cases because both forces are convex, which is the general rule and the reason a convexity glance is worth having.

### Family 11 - two springs combined

**Trigger.** "two identical springs side by side", "in series", "end to end".
**Tool.** Combine to an effective constant first, then Family 1.

| Combination | effective $k$ from $40$ and $60$ lb/ft | $W$ to $1$ ft |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| parallel (side by side) | $100$ lb/ft | $50$ ft-lb |
| series (end to end) | $24$ lb/ft | $12$ ft-lb |

Parallel adds the constants; series adds the reciprocals and then inverts.
The series constant is **smaller than either spring**, which is the sanity check: a longer spring is easier to stretch.
Adding the reciprocals and forgetting to invert gives $0.0416667$, which is dimensionally ft/lb and should be caught by the units gate.

### Family 12 - stored energy and released work

**Trigger.** "energy stored", "work the spring does as it relaxes", "how much work is recovered".
**Tool.** The same integral. The magnitude is the same; only the sign of the signed integral flips.

| Question | Value |
| :--- | ---: |
| energy stored at $2$ ft with $k = 40$ | $80$ ft-lb |
| work released relaxing from $2$ ft to $1$ ft | $60$ ft-lb |
| the signed integral from $2$ to $1$ | $-60$ ft-lb |

### Family 13 - a general variable force, no spring at all

**Trigger.** A force law is handed to you and no spring is mentioned.
**Tool.** $W = \int_b^a F(x)\,dx$, and that is the whole of it.

| $F(x)$ | interval | $W$ |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $3x^2 + 2$ | $0 \to 4$ | $72$ |
| $(1+x)^{-2}$ | $0 \to 3$ | $0.75$ |

This is the family that shows Hooke's law was never the point: the point is that work is the integral of force along the path.

### Family 14 - the mirror, where the distance varies instead

**Trigger.** A chain, a rope, a cable, a tank being pumped out.
**Tool.** Q19.4's slice method. The force per slice is constant and the distance varies, so you integrate over the object rather than along the path.

| Problem | $W$ |
| :--- | ---: |
| $10$ m, $80$ kg chain, one end raised to $6$ m (Q19.4) | $1411.2$ J |
| $12$ m, $60$ kg chain, one end raised to $12$ m | $3528$ J |

Read `notes\Q19.4 - Work, raising one end of a chain.md` for this family rather than re-deriving it.
It carries the four-move slice procedure, the centre-of-mass shortcut and the condition under which that shortcut is valid, which is that the rise is **linear in the slicing variable** and not that the density is uniform.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs a mark.

1. **Using the total length instead of the extension.** Fatal, silent, and the reason Family 3 exists. On this stem it cannot be committed, because the stem gives extensions; on any stem that gives lengths it is the default error.
2. **A wrong $k$.** $W$ is linear in $k$, so the answer is wrong by exactly that factor with no partial recovery. Always put $k$ back into the datum and check.
3. **Forgetting the $\tfrac12$.** Gives $160$ instead of $80$, exactly double.
4. **Answering part b with $k$.** Gives $40$, and $40$ is also what four other wrong methods produce.
5. **Force times distance.** $F(2) \times 2 = 160$. Double again, so $160$ is the most-arrived-at wrong answer on the page.
6. **The two readings of part b.** $0 \to 2$ gives $80$; $1 \to 2$ gives $60$. Read the preposition.
7. **Inserting $g$.** Gives $2576$. Unmissable, and therefore harmless.
8. **Linear scaling.** $2 \times 20 = 40$. Work goes as the square.
9. **Mixed units.** Inches with a lb/ft constant. Family 3's real difficulty.
10. **$2W/(a-b)^2$ instead of $2W/(a^2-b^2)$.** Family 9 only.

### The census, and which errors hide

Twenty candidates, each derived from a named mistake.
**No point was excluded as degenerate**, because on this question the degenerate points are the whole finding.

| Value | Produced by | Caught by |
| ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $0$ | force at the near end times the distance | trivial bracket |
| $2.4844720$ | dividing by $g = 32.2$ | tight bracket |
| $20$ | answering part b with the given datum; integrating to $a=1$ by reflex | tight bracket |
| $40$ | $k = W/a^2$; $k = W/a$; linear scaling; answering with $k$; $\tfrac12 ka$ | tight bracket |
| $60$ | integrating from $1$ to $2$ when $0$ to $2$ was asked | nothing cheap |
| **$80$** | **the truth**, plus three wrong methods (below) | **nothing** |
| $160$ | forgetting the $\tfrac12$; force at the far end times the distance; total length with $L_0 = 1$ | trivial bracket |
| $1600$ | swapping the roles, $\tfrac12 a k^2$ | trivial bracket |
| $2576$ | multiplying by $g = 32.2$ | trivial bracket |

**Four candidates sit exactly on the answer.**
One of them is not an error at all - the midpoint force times the distance is Route E, which is exact.
The other three are genuine wrong methods that return the right number:

- **$k = 2W_1/a_1$ instead of $2W_1/a_1^2$.** The missing square is invisible because $a_1 = 1$, and $1^2 = 1$.
- **$k \times a$, forgetting the $x$ in Hooke's law.** $40 \times 2 = 80$.
- **$\int_0^2 k\,dx$, treating the force as constant at $k$.** Also $80$.

**Both hiders come from the stem's own numbers, and there are exactly two degeneracies.**

| Degeneracy | Why | Where it breaks |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $a_1 = 1$ makes $2W/a^2$ and $2W/a$ agree | dividing by $1$ or by $1^2$ is the same | at $a_1 = 0.5$ they give $160$ and $80$; at $a_1 = 2$, $10$ and $20$ |
| $a = 2$ makes $\tfrac12 ka^2$ and $ka$ agree | $\tfrac12 a^2 = a$ exactly when $a = 2$ | at $a = 3$ they give $180$ and $120$ |

The second one was swept rather than argued: scanning $a$ over $5001$ points on $[0, 5]$, the only values where $\tfrac12 ka^2 = ka$ are $a = 0$ and $a = 2$.
**So this stem is degenerate in two independent ways, and both of them are caused by the convenient numbers $1$ and $2$.**
That is the reason to run the check in step 3 of the procedure rather than trusting an answer that looks right: on this question, three wrong methods produce the right number.

### Collisions, and why "two methods agreed" is weak evidence here

Four values are collisions, with multiplicities $2$, $5$, $4$ and $3$, so **14 of the 20 candidates sit on a collided value**.
All four were derived from the value multiset and cross-checked by an independent pairwise sweep, not typed.
The largest pile-up is at $40$, where five separate mistakes meet.

The consequence is the same as Q19.4's, and stronger: **agreement between two of your own routes is not evidence unless the two routes used different data.**
Route A and Route C share $k$, so they agree even when $k$ is wrong.
Route D shares nothing with them, which is why it is the check to actually run.

### Which magnitude pattern this is

The queue has now measured this same repair on five questions.

| Question | The repair | What it does |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Q19.1 | "an area is positive" | rescues all three wrong candidates |
| Q19.2 | "an area is positive" | produces a confident wrong answer |
| Q19.3 | "an amount is positive" | rescues the one sign error, harmlessly |
| Q19.4 | "work is positive" | inert, rejects 0 of 17 |
| **Q19.5** | **"work is positive"** | **inert, rejects 0 of 20** |

**Q19.5 follows Q19.4's pattern exactly: positivity is useless and the bracket is the only cheap discriminator.**
The trivial bracket $0 < W < 160$ rejects $6$ of $20$.
The two-panel bracket $40 < W < 120$ rejects $14$ of $20$ and is a strict superset of it.
So the tight bracket is worth the extra ten seconds, and neither bracket touches the four values sitting on the answer.

That is the honest summary: **the bracket is the best cheap check available and it still cannot separate the truth from three wrong methods.** Only step 3, putting $k$ back into the datum, does that.

## 10. Drill set

Score **method** separately from **magnitude**.
A wrong magnitude with the right method is an arithmetic slip; a right magnitude with the wrong method is what section 9 is about, and it needs a different fix.

1. $20$ ft-lb of work stretches a spring $1$ ft beyond its natural length. How much work stretches it $2$ ft?
2. The same spring. How much **additional** work is needed to go from $1$ ft to $2$ ft?
3. A spring has $k = 30$ lb/ft. How much work stretches it $3$ ft beyond natural length?
4. The same spring. How much work takes it from $1$ ft to $3$ ft?
5. A spring has natural length $8$ in and $k = 24$ lb/ft. How much work stretches it from $10$ in to $14$ in?
6. A force of $40$ lb holds a spring at $2$ ft beyond natural length. Find $k$, then the work to stretch it $3$ ft.
7. It takes $12$ J to stretch a spring $0.4$ m beyond natural length. Find $k$, then the work to stretch it $0.8$ m.
8. A $4$ kg mass hung on a spring stretches it $0.05$ m. Take $g = 9.8$. Find $k$, then the work to stretch it $0.1$ m.
9. A spring has $k = 50$ N/m. How much work compresses it $0.2$ m?
10. A spring has $k = 40$ lb/ft. What extension requires $45$ ft-lb of work?
11. It takes $60$ ft-lb to stretch a spring from $1$ ft to $2$ ft beyond natural length. Find $k$.
12. A force law is $F(x) = 40x + 6x^2$ lb. Find the work from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$ ft. Then compute the average-force shortcut and say why it is wrong.

### Answer key

| # | Answer | Family and method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $80$ ft-lb | Family 0. $k = 2(20)/1^2 = 40$, then $\tfrac12(40)(4)$. |
| 2 | $60$ ft-lb | Family 2. $\tfrac12(40)(4-1)$. Note it is $80 - 20$. |
| 3 | $135$ ft-lb | Family 1. $\tfrac12(30)(9)$. |
| 4 | $120$ ft-lb | Family 2. $\tfrac12(30)(9-1)$. |
| 5 | $\tfrac83 = 2.666667$ ft-lb | Family 3. Extensions $\tfrac16$ and $\tfrac12$ ft, then $\tfrac12(24)(\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{36})$. Not subtracting gives $8$. |
| 6 | $k = 20$ lb/ft, $W = 90$ ft-lb | Family 4. $k = F/a = 40/2$, no factor of $2$. |
| 7 | $k = 150$ N/m, $W = 48$ J | Family 5. $k = 2(12)/0.16$. No $g$. |
| 8 | $k = 784$ N/m, $W = 3.92$ J | Family 6. $k = mg/x = 4(9.8)/0.05$. $g$ belongs here. |
| 9 | $1$ J | Family 7. $\tfrac12(50)(0.04)$; compression costs what stretching costs. |
| 10 | $1.5$ ft | Family 8. $a = \sqrt{2(45)/40} = \sqrt{9/4}$. |
| 11 | $40$ lb/ft | Family 9. $k = 2(60)/(4-1)$, not $2(60)/4 = 30$. |
| 12 | $96$ ft-lb; shortcut gives $104$ | Family 10. The force is not affine, so the average-force identity fails; the gap is $8$. |

Eleven of the fifteen families are drilled here.
Families 11 through 14 appear in the catalog only, because each is a one-line adaptation once families 0 through 10 are automatic.

Of the twelve answers, one equals $80$, four are larger and seven are smaller, so a habit of expecting the answer to be near the headline number will mislead you two thirds of the time.
Exactly one answer, D8, carries a factor of $g$, and it is the only drill whose datum is a mass.

work on a spring?

 |    work over an extension ----------> k = 2W / a^2
 |    force at an extension -----------> k = F / a          (no 2, no square)
