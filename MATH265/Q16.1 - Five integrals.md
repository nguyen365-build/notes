**The question (Final Exam 1, item 8, 20 points).**
Compute the value of each of the integrals listed below.

- a. $\displaystyle\int \left(x^2 - x\right)\sqrt{3x}\,dx$

- c. $\displaystyle\int_a^b \left(x + \cos(2x)\right)dx$

## 0. The 30-second version

```
INTEGRATION IS A SEARCH, NOT A LOOKUP.
Walk the ladder from the top and STOP at the first rung that fires.

  rung 1  Can ALGEBRA make this a sum of powers?      -> power rule, term by term
          (multiply out, split the fraction, pull constants)
  rung 2  Is the inner function LINEAR (ax+b)?         -> integrate outside, divide by a
  rung 3  Is some inner function's DERIVATIVE present  -> u-substitution
          in the rest, up to a constant factor?
  rung 4  Is it a trig product with no du in sight?    -> IDENTITY first, then re-enter at 1

Then, always:   + C        (indefinite only)
Then, always:   DIFFERENTIATE YOUR ANSWER BACK.
```

Q16.1's five parts are rungs 1, 4, 2, 1, 3 in that order.
That is not a coincidence - it is the whole ladder, laid out as one question.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is the first integration question in the bank.
Everything numbered below Q16 was differential calculus.
So the examiner is not testing a clever technique - he is testing whether you own the ladder.

| the reflex | why it is here | where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Try algebra before you try a technique | parts (a) and (d) collapse to the power rule and need no technique at all | Q16.2c, which looks like a quotient rule problem and is not |
| The power rule runs backwards, including on fractional powers | $\sqrt{3x} = \sqrt3\,x^{1/2}$, so (a) is $x^{5/2}$ and $x^{3/2}$ | every antiderivative you will ever write |
| A linear inner function costs a $1/a$ | part (c)'s $\cos 2x$ integrates to $\tfrac12 \sin 2x$ | Q16.2a, and every $\int f(ax+b)$ |
| A substitution must convert $dx$ **completely** | part (e) leaves no $x$ behind | Q16.2b and Q16.2d, which are pure substitutions |
| A trig product needs an identity before it needs a technique | part (b) is invisible until $\sin 2x$ is rewritten | Q16.2d's hint does this rewriting *for* you |
| $+C$ on every indefinite integral | three of the five parts are indefinite | every indefinite integral in the course |
| Definite integrals produce a **number**, not a family | (c) and (e) take no $+C$ | Q17's area and average-value questions |

The five parts also split cleanly by answer type, and that split is the thing to notice first:

- **(a), (b), (d)** are indefinite. The answer is a **family** of functions, and it needs $+C$.
- **(c), (e)** are definite. The answer is a **number** (or, in (c), an expression in $a$ and $b$),
  and writing $+C$ on it is wrong.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

### 2.1 The power rule, backwards

$$\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \qquad (n \neq -1)$$

Read it as an instruction: **add one to the exponent, then divide by the new exponent.**
It works for every real $n$ except $-1$, and the fractional cases are where marks are lost.

| you see | rewrite as | antiderivative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{x}$ | $x^{1/2}$ | $\tfrac{2}{3}x^{3/2}$ |
| $x\sqrt{x}$ | $x^{3/2}$ | $\tfrac{2}{5}x^{5/2}$ |
| $x^2\sqrt{x}$ | $x^{5/2}$ | $\tfrac{2}{7}x^{7/2}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ | $x^{-1/2}$ | $2x^{1/2}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ | $x^{-2}$ | $-x^{-1}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^3}$ | $x^{-3}$ | $-\tfrac{1}{2}x^{-2}$ |

The pattern in the middle column is worth naming: **a half-power always produces a denominator
of an odd number**, because $n = k/2$ gives $n+1 = (k+2)/2$ and you divide by that, i.e. multiply
by $2/(k+2)$. So $\tfrac23, \tfrac25, \tfrac27$ - there are only ever these small odd denominators,
and if you write $\tfrac{2}{4}$ or $\tfrac{2}{6}$ you have made an arithmetic slip, not a new case.

### 2.2 The four antiderivatives you must know by sight

| $f(x)$ | $\int f(x)\,dx$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x + C$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x + C$ |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $\tan x + C$ |
| $\sec x \tan x$ | $\sec x + C$ |

The two signs are the only thing to remember, and the mnemonic is that **the sign appears going
"up" the list** - $\int \sin$ picks up the minus, $\int \cos$ does not.
Check it by differentiating: $\tfrac{d}{dx}(-\cos x) = \sin x$. Correct.

### 2.3 The two linearity rules, which are what make rung 1 legal

$$\int \big(f(x) + g(x)\big)\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx + \int g(x)\,dx \qquad
\int k\,f(x)\,dx = k\int f(x)\,dx$$

Note carefully what is **not** on this list.
There is no product rule for integrals, no quotient rule, and no chain rule.
That absence is the reason integration is a search rather than a lookup, and it is the single
biggest structural difference from Q8.1.

### 2.4 The three trig identities that unlock a product

| identity | turns | into |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$ | $\sin(2x)\cos x$ | $2\sin x\cos^2 x$ - now a substitution |
| $\sin^2 x = \dfrac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}$ | $\sin^2 x$ | a sum of a constant and a cosine |
| $\cos^2 x = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}$ | $\cos^2 x$ | a sum of a constant and a cosine |

### 2.5 The Fundamental Theorem, in the form you actually use

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \quad\text{where } F' = f$$

Two consequences you will be tested on:

- The constant cancels. $(F(b)+C) - (F(a)+C) = F(b)-F(a)$, which is exactly why a definite
  integral takes no $+C$.
- Swapping the limits negates the answer. Verified: on part (e), $F(2)-F(4)$ is exactly
  $-8.632817855719$.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 0 - Classify the answer type.**
> Look for limits on the integral sign. Limits present means the answer is a number and takes
> no $+C$. No limits means the answer is a function and **must** carry $+C$.
>
> **Step 1 - Try algebra (rung 1).**
> Multiply out every bracket. Split every fraction over its denominator. Pull every constant
> outside. Rewrite every root as a fractional power. Now look: is it a **sum of powers of $x$**?
> If yes, you are done thinking - apply the power rule term by term.
>
> **Step 2 - Look for a linear inner function (rung 2).**
> Is the whole integrand $f(ax+b)$ for something you can integrate? Then integrate the outside
> and divide by $a$. This is substitution's special case and it is worth recognising separately,
> because it is fast and it is where the $1/a$ gets dropped.
>
> **Step 3 - Look for a $u$ and its $du$ (rung 3).**
> Pick a candidate inner function $u$. Compute $du/dx$. Ask: **is $du/dx$ present in the rest of
> the integrand, up to a constant factor?** If yes, substitute. If no, this $u$ is wrong - try
> another, or fall to step 4.
>
> **Step 4 - Rewrite with an identity (rung 4), then go back to step 1.**
> A trig product that no $u$ fits needs an identity first. After rewriting, re-enter the ladder
> at the top; the rewritten form is usually a rung-1 or rung-3 problem.
>
> **Step 5 - Finish.**
> Indefinite: add $+C$. Definite: evaluate $F(b) - F(a)$.
>
> **Step 6 - Differentiate your answer back.**
> This is not optional and it is not slow. See section 6 for exactly what it can and cannot catch.

### The classification table

Run the integrand through this and the rung falls out.

| what you see after multiplying out | rung | tool |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a sum of powers of $x$, including fractional and negative | 1 | power rule, term by term |
| a polynomial in a bracket raised to a **small** power | 1 | expand, then power rule |
| a polynomial in a bracket raised to a **large** power, bracket linear | 2 | $1/a$ rule |
| $f(ax+b)$ for a known $f$ | 2 | $1/a$ rule |
| a bracket to a power, times (a constant multiple of) that bracket's derivative | 3 | $u$ = the bracket |
| a root of something, times that something's derivative | 3 | $u$ = the thing under the root |
| a trig product with mismatched angles | 4 | identity, then re-enter at rung 1 |
| an even power of $\sin$ or $\cos$ alone | 4 | half-angle identity |
| an odd power of $\sin$ or $\cos$ alone | 4 | peel one factor off, then rung 3 |

## 4. How this differs from Q8.1, which is the point

`notes\Q8.1 - Four derivatives, power chain and quotient rules.md` builds its whole
rule-selection method on the **last-operation test**:

> **Step 1 - Ask what the LAST operation is.** (Q8.1, section 3, line 209)

and it works because differentiation is *structural*.
Q8.1 line 270 states the key property: "The last-operation test never looks at the whole
expression."
It reads the outermost operation, names the rule, and recurses. It always terminates, it never
backtracks, and it cannot fail.

**Integration has no such test, and understanding why is worth more than any single technique.**

| | differentiation (Q8.1) | integration (Q16.1) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| what you inspect | the **last operation** only | the **whole integrand** at once |
| rule for a product | exists - the product rule | **does not exist** |
| rule for a quotient | exists - the quotient rule | **does not exist** |
| rule for a composition | exists - the chain rule | **does not exist** (substitution is a guess, not a rule) |
| the procedure | a decision, taken once | a **search**, with backtracking |
| can it fail? | no - every elementary function has an elementary derivative | yes - many elementary functions have no elementary antiderivative |
| verification | re-differentiate, or check a value | **differentiate the answer back** - and it is cheap |

Q8.1's line 296 works part (b) of that question by saying "Last operation: division. So
$\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$", and the rule is settled in one glance.
Nothing in Q16.1 can be settled in one glance.
Part (a) *looks* like a product, and the product structure is a red herring - the answer is to
multiply it out and never use a technique at all.

**So the mirror of the last-operation test is not another one-glance test. It is the ladder in
section 0, walked from the top.** The discipline it replaces "what is the last operation?" with
is: **"can algebra kill this before I reach for a technique?"** That question is answered first,
every time, and on this question it disposes of two of the five parts.

## 5. Q16.1 worked, part by part

### Part (a) - $\displaystyle\int (x^2-x)\sqrt{3x}\,dx$

**Rung 1.** It looks like a product. It is not a product problem.

**Step 1 - pull the constant out of the root.**
$\sqrt{3x} = \sqrt3\cdot\sqrt x = \sqrt3\,x^{1/2}$.
This is the move the whole part turns on, and it is pure algebra.

**Step 2 - multiply out.**

$$(x^2 - x)\cdot\sqrt3\,x^{1/2} = \sqrt3\left(x^2\cdot x^{1/2} - x\cdot x^{1/2}\right)
= \sqrt3\left(x^{5/2} - x^{3/2}\right)$$

**Step 3 - power rule, term by term.**

$$\sqrt3\left(\frac{x^{7/2}}{7/2} - \frac{x^{5/2}}{5/2}\right) + C
= \frac{2\sqrt3}{7}x^{7/2} - \frac{2\sqrt3}{5}x^{5/2} + C$$

**Domain.** $\sqrt{3x}$ requires $3x \ge 0$, so the integrand exists only for $x \ge 0$ and the
identity $F' = f$ holds on $x > 0$.
This restriction is real and it is invisible to every check you can run in the exam - see
section 6.

**Check by differentiating back.**
$\tfrac{d}{dx}\left[\tfrac{2\sqrt3}{7}x^{7/2}\right] = \tfrac{2\sqrt3}{7}\cdot\tfrac72 x^{5/2}
= \sqrt3\,x^{5/2}$, and similarly the second term gives $\sqrt3\,x^{3/2}$.
Their difference is $\sqrt3(x^{5/2}-x^{3/2})$, which is the integrand. Correct.

### Part (b) - $\displaystyle\int \sin(2x)\cos x\,dx$

**Rung 4, then rung 3.** No substitution works on this as written: if $u = \sin 2x$ then
$du = 2\cos 2x\,dx$ and there is no $\cos 2x$ anywhere; if $u = \cos x$ then $du = -\sin x\,dx$
and there is a $\sin 2x$, not a $\sin x$. Both candidates fail, which is the cue for an identity.

**Route 1 - double-angle identity.**

$$\sin 2x\cos x = (2\sin x\cos x)\cos x = 2\sin x\cos^2 x$$

Now $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\,dx$, so $\sin x\,dx = -du$:

$$\int 2\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx = \int 2u^2(-du) = -\frac{2u^3}{3} + C = -\frac{2}{3}\cos^3 x + C$$

**Route 2 - product-to-sum.**
Using $\sin A\cos B = \tfrac12[\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)]$ with $A = 2x$, $B = x$:

$$\sin 2x\cos x = \tfrac12\left[\sin 3x + \sin x\right]
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\int = -\frac{\cos 3x}{6} - \frac{\cos x}{2} + C$$

**These two answers are the same function, not merely the same family.**
Substituting $\cos 3x = 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x$ into route 2:

$$-\frac{4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x}{6} - \frac{\cos x}{2}
= -\frac{2}{3}\cos^3 x + \frac{\cos x}{2} - \frac{\cos x}{2}
= -\frac{2}{3}\cos^3 x$$

Verified numerically: the difference between the two routes is constant across seven sample
points, and that constant is $0$ to within $10^{-13}$.
This is worth seeing once, because it is the exception. Two correct antiderivatives normally
differ by a **non-zero** constant, and both are still correct.

### Part (c) - $\displaystyle\int_a^b (x + \cos 2x)\,dx$

**Rung 1 on the first term, rung 2 on the second.** A sum splits, so each term goes its own way.

$$\int x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}, \qquad \int \cos 2x\,dx = \frac{\sin 2x}{2}$$

The second one is the $1/a$ rule with $a = 2$. Differentiate $\tfrac12\sin 2x$ to check:
$\tfrac12\cdot 2\cos 2x = \cos 2x$. Correct.

$$\int_a^b (x + \cos 2x)\,dx
= \left[\frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{\sin 2x}{2}\right]_a^b
= \frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} + \frac{\sin 2b - \sin 2a}{2}$$

**No $+C$** - the limits are there, so this is a number (in terms of $a$ and $b$).
Verified against quadrature on six $(a,b)$ pairs including $a=b$ (which gives exactly $0$) and
$b < a$ (which negates).

### Part (d) - $\displaystyle\int (x^2-4)^2\,dx$

**Rung 1.** The bracket's power is $2$, which is small, so expand.

$$(x^2-4)^2 = x^4 - 8x^2 + 16
\;\Longrightarrow\;
\int = \frac{x^5}{5} - \frac{8x^3}{3} + 16x + C$$

**The trap this part exists to punish** is running the chain rule backwards: writing
$\dfrac{(x^2-4)^3}{3}$ as if the bracket were a linear inner function.
It is not - $u = x^2-4$ gives $du = 2x\,dx$, and there is no $x$ factor available.
That answer differentiates back to $(x^2-4)^2\cdot 2x$, which is wrong by a factor of $2x$, and
the check catches it immediately (measured gap $1.12\times10^{1}$ at $x=6.1$).

### Part (e) - $\displaystyle\int_2^4 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx$

**Rung 3.** A root of something, times something that is *not quite* its derivative.
$u = x - 1$ gives $du = dx$, and the leftover $x$ must be converted too, which is the whole
lesson of this part.

**Substitute completely.**

$$u = x - 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = u + 1, \quad dx = du$$

**Change the limits** (this is the faster of the two finishing routes):
$x = 2 \Rightarrow u = 1$, and $x = 4 \Rightarrow u = 3$.

$$\int_2^4 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx = \int_1^3 (u+1)\sqrt u\,du = \int_1^3\left(u^{3/2} + u^{1/2}\right)du$$

$$= \left[\frac{2}{5}u^{5/2} + \frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_1^3
= \left(\frac{2}{5}\cdot 9\sqrt3 + \frac{2}{3}\cdot 3\sqrt3\right) - \left(\frac25 + \frac23\right)$$

$$= \frac{18\sqrt3}{5} + 2\sqrt3 - \frac{16}{15}
= \frac{28\sqrt3}{5} - \frac{16}{15} = \frac{84\sqrt3 - 16}{15} \approx 8.632817855719$$

**Note the step that carries the marks:** rewriting $x$ as $u+1$.
Leaving the $x$ in place and writing $\int x\,u^{1/2}du$ is the defining error of this part, and
it is the thing the ladder's step 3 means by "convert $dx$ **completely**".

**A structural check worth knowing.** $\int_1^5 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx$ and $\int_0^4 (x+1)\sqrt{x}\,dx$
are the same integral, because the substitution $u = x-1$ maps one exactly onto the other.
Both were computed independently and both give $18.133333333333$.

### Route comparison

| part | route | when it wins | when it loses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | expand to powers | always | never - there is no competitor |
| a | try $u = 3x$ | never | $du = 3\,dx$ but the $(x^2-x)$ must then be rewritten in $u$, which is strictly more work |
| b | double angle, then $u=\cos x$ | **use this under pressure** | needs you to spot $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$ |
| b | product-to-sum | if you have the formula cold | most students do not, and it is not on the note sheet |
| d | expand | **use this under pressure** - the power is 2 | if the power were 8, expanding is 9 terms and you would want a different question |
| e | substitute, **change the limits** | **use this under pressure** | none |
| e | substitute, back-substitute to $x$, then evaluate | if you mistrust your limit conversion | two extra lines, and re-introduces the chance of a stray $x$ |

**Under exam pressure: expand whenever the bracket power is 2 or 3, substitute and change the
limits whenever there are limits, and reach for an identity only when two substitution candidates
have both failed.**

## 6. Verify before you commit - and know what the check cannot see

**Differentiating your answer back is the only verification available to you in a Möbius exam.**
It costs about eight seconds per part. Do it on all five.

It was tested exhaustively here. Twenty-six seeded wrong answers were differentiated back and
compared against the integrand at many points:

| error family | seeded cases | **DETECTED** | **HIDES** |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| dropped constant factor | 6 | **6** | 0 |
| missed chain factor from the substitution | 5 | **5** | 0 |
| other algebra slips (sign, power rule, stray $x$) | 5 | **5** | 0 |
| omitted or altered $+C$ | 7 | 0 | **7** |
| domain restriction | 2 | 0 | **2** |
| swapped limits on a definite integral | 1 | 0 | **1** |
| **total** | **26** | **16** | **10** |

The split is not a matter of degree - it is exact, and it has a one-line explanation:

> **Differentiating back detects every error that changes the function, and is structurally blind
> to every error that does not.**

### What it catches, with measured evidence

| seeded error | relative gap at the worst sampled point |
| :--- | :--- |
| (a) dropped the $\sqrt3$ entirely | $4.226\times10^{-1}$ |
| (b) dropped the $2$ from $\sin 2x = 2\sin x\cos x$ | $3.846\times10^{-1}$ |
| (c) forgot the $1/2$ on $\sin 2x$ | $1.000\times10^{0}$ |
| (d) ran the chain rule backwards, $(x^2-4)^3/3$ | $1.120\times10^{1}$ |
| (e) left a stray $x$ in the antiderivative | $1.384\times10^{0}$ |
| (a) applied the power rule as $x^n \to x^{n-1}$ | $9.944\times10^{-1}$ |
| (d) dropped the $8$ on the middle term | $4.714\times10^{0}$ |
| (c) differentiated instead of integrating | $5.000\times10^{0}$ |

These are **relative** gaps, measured against the scale of the integrand.
Even the smallest of them, $0.38$, means the differentiated expression is off by nearly forty per
cent of the integrand's own size - and symbolically it is a whole missing factor, not a rounding
difference. You do not need a calculator to see any of them: the differentiated expression simply
is not the integrand you started with.

### What it cannot catch, and what to do instead

1. **The constant.** $\tfrac{d}{dx}C = 0$, so a wrong constant, or a missing $+C$ entirely, is
   invisible. Adding $10^6$ to the answer to part (d) still differentiates back perfectly
   (measured gap $0.00$).
   **Remedy: it is a checklist item, not a check.** Before you submit an indefinite integral,
   look at the answer and confirm the letter $C$ is physically present.
   The control that proves this arm is measuring something: a **non-constant** perturbation of the
   same size, $F + 10^{-3}x$, **is** detected.

2. **The domain.** $F' = f$ is a *pointwise* identity. It holds at every point where both sides
   are defined and says nothing about where that is.
   Part (a) is valid only for $x \ge 0$ and part (e) only for $x \ge 1$, and differentiating back
   confirms the identity at every in-domain point (gaps $6.66\times10^{-16}$ and
   $5.13\times10^{-16}$) while reporting nothing about the restriction.
   **Remedy: read the domain off the original integrand, not off your answer.** Every even root
   and every denominator in the *integrand* is a restriction.

3. **Swapped limits.** On a definite integral the antiderivative can be flawless while the
   evaluation runs backwards. $F(2)-F(4)$ on part (e) is built on an antiderivative whose
   differentiate-back gap is $5.13\times10^{-16}$, i.e. perfect, and the answer is still wrong by
   a sign.
   **Remedy: a magnitude-and-sign sanity check.** On part (e) the integrand $x\sqrt{x-1}$ is
   positive throughout $[2,4]$, so the answer must be positive. It is: $8.63$. A negative answer
   would be caught instantly by that one observation.

### The cheap magnitude checks

| part | sanity check | passes? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| c | at $a=b$ the answer must be exactly $0$ | yes, verified to $10^{-15}$ |
| c | swapping $a$ and $b$ must negate | yes, verified to $10^{-13}$ |
| e | integrand is positive on $[2,4]$, so the answer is positive | yes, $8.63 > 0$ |
| e | crude bound: $2\sqrt1 = 2$ at the left, $4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93$ at the right, width $2$, so the answer lies between $4$ and $13.9$ | yes, $8.63$ is inside |
| a, d | every term's exponent must be one **higher** than the integrand's | yes |

That last row is the fastest structural check there is, and it catches an entire class of panic
error: if any exponent in your answer is *lower* than the corresponding one in the integrand, you
have differentiated instead of integrated.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both exams are Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a correct method with a bad keystroke
scores zero.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| `+ C` on every indefinite integral | omit it, or write `+c` if the system is case-sensitive |
| `(2/3)*(cos(x))^3` | `2/3cos(x)^3` - the implicit multiplication is not guaranteed |
| `x^(7/2)` with brackets round the exponent | `x^7/2`, which parses as $\dfrac{x^7}{2}$ |
| `sqrt(3)` or `3^(1/2)` | `sqrt3` |
| `28*sqrt(3)/5 - 16/15` | a rounded decimal, unless the question asks for one |
| `(cos(x))^3` | `cos^3(x)`, which most parsers reject |
| bracket the whole numerator and denominator | rely on precedence in a typed fraction |

**The single most expensive typo on this question is `x^7/2`.**
Part (a)'s answer contains two fractional exponents, and both need bracketing. Written without
brackets the answer is off by a factor of $x^{7/2}$ divided by $x^7/2$, which is not close to
right and earns nothing.

**Second most expensive: the missing `+ C`.**
Three of the five parts are indefinite, and section 6 proves that no check you can run will
remind you. It has to be a habit.

**Preview every answer before you submit it.** Möbius renders what it parsed - if the preview
shows $\dfrac{x^7}{2}$ where you meant $x^{7/2}$, you have found the error for free.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
Across all six families there are only **four distinct tools**: the power rule, the $1/a$ factor,
a $u$-substitution, and a trig identity. Every row below is one of those four, reached by a
different-looking route. If you can name the rung, you can do the row.

Every entry was verified twice: the claimed antiderivative was differentiated back by
complex-step at many in-domain points, and $F(b)-F(a)$ was compared against tanh-sinh quadrature
that never saw the closed form.

### Family 0 - already a sum of powers

**Trigger:** you look at it and it is a polynomial, or becomes one after one expansion.
**Tool:** the power rule, term by term.
**Worked shape:** expand, then add one to each exponent and divide by the new one.

| integrand | antiderivative | a definite check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(x^2-4)^2$ **[part d]** | $\dfrac{x^5}{5} - \dfrac{8x^3}{3} + 16x + C$ | $\int_0^2 = 17.0666666667$ |
| $3x^4 - 2x + 7$ | $\dfrac{3x^5}{5} - x^2 + 7x + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 6.6000000000$ |
| $(x^3+1)^2$ | $\dfrac{x^7}{7} + \dfrac{x^4}{2} + x + C$ | $\int_0^{1.5} = 6.4720982143$ |
| $(2x-5)^2$ | $\dfrac{4x^3}{3} - 5x^2 + 25x + C$ | $\int_0^3 = 21.0000000000$ |

The last row is deliberate: $(2x-5)^2$ can be done at rung 1 **or** rung 2, and both are correct.
Rung 2 gives $\dfrac{(2x-5)^3}{6} + C$. These differ by a constant ($-125/6$), which is exactly
what $+C$ absorbs.

### Family 1 - algebra first, then the power rule

**Trigger:** a product or a quotient involving roots or powers of $x$ only.
**Tool:** multiply out or split the fraction, then family 0.
**Worked shape:** turn every root into a fractional power *before* you do anything else.

| integrand | antiderivative | a definite check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(x^2-x)\sqrt{3x}$ **[part a]** | $\dfrac{2\sqrt3}{7}x^{7/2} - \dfrac{2\sqrt3}{5}x^{5/2} + C$ | $\int_0^4 = 41.1733220542$ |
| $(x+1)\sqrt x$ | $\dfrac{2}{5}x^{5/2} + \dfrac{2}{3}x^{3/2} + C$ | $\int_0^4 = 18.1333333333$ |
| $\dfrac{x^3 + \sqrt{5x} - 4}{x^2}$ **[Q16.2c]** | $\dfrac{x^2}{2} - 2\sqrt5\,x^{-1/2} + \dfrac{4}{x} + C$ | $\int_1^4 = 6.7360679775$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2(x-3)}{\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac{2}{7}x^{7/2} - \dfrac{6}{5}x^{5/2} + C$ | $\int_0^4 = -1.8285714286$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^2} - \dfrac{1}{x^3}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{x} + \dfrac{1}{2x^2} + C$ | $\int_1^4 = 0.2812500000$ |

**The third row is Q16.2 part (c) and it is the family's whole point.** It looks like it needs the
quotient rule. There is no quotient rule for integrals. Splitting the fraction over $x^2$ gives
$x + \sqrt5\,x^{-3/2} - 4x^{-2}$, which is family 0. Note how many students would reach for
substitution here and find nothing.

Notice the **signs** in this table are generated by exactly two things: a minus in the original
integrand, and the minus produced when a negative exponent is integrated ($x^{-2}\to -x^{-1}$).
There is no third source.

### Family 2 - a linear inner function, the $1/a$ factor

**Trigger:** the integrand is $f(ax+b)$ for an $f$ you can integrate.
**Tool:** integrate the outside, then divide by $a$.
**Worked shape:** $\displaystyle\int f(ax+b)\,dx = \frac{1}{a}F(ax+b) + C$.

| integrand | antiderivative | a definite check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x + \cos 2x$ **[part c]** | $\dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{\sin 2x}{2} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.9546487134$ |
| $\sin 5x$ | $-\dfrac{\cos 5x}{5} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.1432675629$ |
| $(2x-5)^7$ | $\dfrac{(2x-5)^8}{16} + C$ | $\int_0^2 = -24414.0000000000$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{(3x+1)^2}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{3(3x+1)} + C$ | $\int_0^2 = 0.2857142857$ |
| $\sqrt{4x+9}$ | $\dfrac{(4x+9)^{3/2}}{6} + C$ | $\int_0^4 = 16.3333333333$ |

Read the denominators as structure: $5$, $16 = 8\times2$, $3$, $6 = \tfrac32\times4$.
Each is **the outside integration's denominator multiplied by $a$**. There is only one rule here
wearing five costumes.

The $(2x-5)^7$ row is family 0's fourth row with the power raised. At power $2$ you expand; at
power $7$ you would be expanding eight terms, so rung 2 wins. **The rung that wins depends on the
exponent, not on the shape.**

### Family 3 - a genuine $u$-substitution

**Trigger:** an inner function whose derivative appears in the rest, up to a constant factor.
**Tool:** $u$ = the inner function; convert $dx$ **completely**.
**Worked shape:** substitute, integrate in $u$, then either change the limits or substitute back.

| integrand | antiderivative | a definite check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x\sqrt{x-1}$ **[part e]** | $\dfrac{2}{5}(x-1)^{5/2} + \dfrac{2}{3}(x-1)^{3/2} + C$ | $\int_2^4 = 8.6328178557$ |
| $x(x^2+1)^4$ | $\dfrac{(x^2+1)^5}{10} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 3.1000000000$ |
| $\tan x\sec^2 x$ **[Q16.2b]** | $\dfrac{\tan^2 x}{2} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 1.2127594104$ |
| $\dfrac{\cos\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt x}$ **[Q16.2a]** | $\sqrt2\,\sin\sqrt{2x} + C$ | $\int_{0.5}^4 = -0.7543404428$ |
| $x^2\sqrt{x^3+2}$ | $\dfrac{2}{9}(x^3+2)^{3/2} + C$ | $\int_0^2 = 6.3987443282$ |
| $\sec^3 x\tan x$ **[Q16.2d]** | $\dfrac{\sec^3 x}{3} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 1.7800013583$ |

**The first row is the only one in the family where the substitution does not clear the integrand
by itself.** In every other row, $u$ and $du$ consume everything. In part (e) a lone $x$ survives
and must be rewritten as $u+1$. That is the family's hard case and it is the one on the exam.

The constants $\tfrac{1}{10}$, $\tfrac12$, $\sqrt2$, $\tfrac29$, $\tfrac13$ all come from the same
place: **the reciprocal of the constant factor by which the available $du$ differs from the
required one**, multiplied by the power rule's own denominator. Nothing here is memorised.

### Family 4 - a trig identity first

**Trigger:** a trig product where two substitution candidates have both failed, or an even/odd
power of a single trig function.
**Tool:** rewrite with an identity, then re-enter the ladder at rung 1.

| integrand | antiderivative | a definite check |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin 2x\cos x$ **[part b]** | $-\dfrac{2}{3}\cos^3 x + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.5615142632$ |
| $\sin 2x\cos x$ (product-to-sum route) | $-\dfrac{\cos 3x}{6} - \dfrac{\cos x}{2} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.5615142632$ |
| $\sin^2 x$ | $\dfrac{x}{2} - \dfrac{\sin 2x}{4} + C$ | $\int_0^\pi = 1.5707963268$ |
| $\cos^2 3x$ | $\dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{\sin 6x}{12} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.4767153752$ |
| $\sin 3x\cos 3x$ | $\dfrac{\sin^2 3x}{6} + C$ | $\int_0^1 = 0.0033191428$ |
| $\sin^3 x$ | $\dfrac{\cos^3 x}{3} - \cos x + C$ | $\int_0^\pi = 1.3333333333$ |

Rows one and two are **the same integral by two routes**, and their definite values agree to every
printed digit, which is the numerical statement of the algebra in section 5.

The even-power rows ($\sin^2$, $\cos^2$) and the odd-power row ($\sin^3$) need *different*
identities, and that is the family's one real decision:

- **even power** - use the half-angle identity, which converts it to a sum. The answer contains a
  bare $x$ term.
- **odd power** - peel one factor off and convert the rest with $\sin^2 = 1 - \cos^2$, which turns
  it into a rung-3 substitution. The answer contains no bare $x$ term.

That presence or absence of a bare $x$ is a complete check on which identity you used.

### Family 5 - definite integrals, where the limits are the trap

**Trigger:** limits on the integral sign.
**Tool:** everything above, plus $F(b)-F(a)$, and **no $+C$**.

| integral | value | the trap it carries |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\int_2^4 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx$ **[part e]** | $8.6328178557$ | forgetting to convert the limits with $u$ |
| $\int_a^b (x+\cos 2x)\,dx$ **[part c]** | $\dfrac{b^2-a^2}{2} + \dfrac{\sin 2b-\sin 2a}{2}$ | writing $+C$ on a definite integral |
| $\int_1^5 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx$ | $18.1333333333$ | identical to $\int_0^4 (x+1)\sqrt x\,dx$ - the same integral in disguise |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\sin 3x\cos 3x\,dx$ | $0.0000000000$ | an odd integrand over a symmetric interval; check the symmetry before computing anything |
| $\int_{2.7}^{2.7}(x+\cos 2x)\,dx$ | $0.0000000000$ | the degenerate case is legal and the answer is exactly zero |

**On the symbolic-limits form in part (c):** it was verified on six $(a,b)$ pairs including
$a=b$ and $b<a$. Neither was excluded as degenerate, and both behave exactly as the Fundamental
Theorem requires.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks on a question of this shape.

1. **The missing $+C$.** Three of five parts need it, nothing you can check will remind you, and
   it is the cheapest mark on the page to lose. Section 6 proves the blindness with seven seeded
   cases, all of which hide.
2. **A stray $x$ after substitution.** Part (e)'s defining error. Writing $\int x\,u^{1/2}du$
   instead of $\int(u+1)u^{1/2}du$ mixes two variables and nothing that follows can be right.
   Differentiating back **does** catch this (gap $1.38$), so the eight-second check saves it.
3. **The unbracketed fractional exponent, `x^7/2`.** A method-perfect part (a) scoring zero.
4. **Running the chain rule backwards.** Writing $\dfrac{(x^2-4)^3}{3}$ for part (d). There is no
   backwards chain rule; substitution only works when $du$ is actually present. Caught by
   differentiating back (gap $1.12\times10^1$).
5. **Dropping the $1/a$.** Part (c)'s $\cos 2x \to \sin 2x$ instead of $\tfrac12\sin 2x$. Caught
   by differentiating back (gap $1.00$).
6. **Forgetting to convert the limits.** Substituting $u = x-1$ and then evaluating from $2$ to
   $4$ in $u$-space rather than $1$ to $3$. Nothing in the algebra looks wrong.
   **Remedy: write the new limits down at the moment you write the substitution, not later.**
7. **Reaching for a technique before trying algebra.** Parts (a) and (d) both punish this, and
   Q16.2c punishes it harder. Rung 1 is first for a reason.
8. **Writing $+C$ on a definite integral.** The mirror of trap 1 and it looks careless, which on
   an answer-only exam is indistinguishable from being wrong.
9. **Missing the domain restriction.** Part (a) needs $x\ge0$ and part (e) needs $x\ge1$.
   Structurally invisible to every check - read it off the integrand.
10. **Swapping the limits.** Produces exactly the negative of the right answer, and the
    antiderivative behind it is flawless. Only a sign-and-magnitude sanity check finds it.
