## 0. The 30-second version

```
For each part, ask ONE question:  if I put a number in, what is the LAST
arithmetic operation I would perform?  That operation names the rule.

   last op = + or -   ->  differentiate term by term        (a)
   last op = /        ->  QUOTIENT RULE  (u'v - uv')/v^2    (b)

```

## 2. The five things you must have cold

These are the recall prerequisites.
If any of them needs a lookup, the procedure in section 3 will stall at exactly the point where the clock matters.

### 2.1 The five differentiation rules, with the pieces named

| rule | statement | the piece people drop |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| power | $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$, for **every** real $n$ | that it holds for negative and fractional $n$ too |
| constant multiple | $\dfrac{d}{dx}\,cf = c\,f'$ | nothing; this one is safe |
| sum | $\dfrac{d}{dx}(f\pm g) = f'\pm g'$ | nothing; this one is safe |
| product | $\dfrac{d}{dx}(fg) = f'g + fg'$ | nothing; it is symmetric, so order does not matter |
| quotient | $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{u}{v} = \dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ | **the order**; it is *not* symmetric |
| chain | $\dfrac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ | **the $g'(x)$**; this is the single most-dropped factor in the course |

**The two rules that lose marks are the two that are not symmetric.**
The product rule reads the same forwards and backwards, so there is nothing to get wrong.
The quotient rule does not: $u'v - uv'$ and $uv' - u'v$ differ by a sign, and both look plausible on the page.
The chain rule is not symmetric either, in the sense that the inner derivative is a factor you must *remember to add* rather than a term you must order correctly, which is why it is dropped rather than reversed.

The mnemonic that actually sticks for the quotient rule is that the **numerator's derivative comes first**, matching the order the fraction is written in.
"Low d-high minus high d-low" is the other common one and it encodes the same thing.

### 2.2 The trigonometric derivatives, all six

| $f$ | $f'$ | | $f$ | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | | $\csc x$ | $-\csc x\cot x$ |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | | $\sec x$ | $\sec x\tan x$ |
| $\tan x$ | $\sec^2 x$ | | $\cot x$ | $-\csc^2 x$ |

Read this as **three pairs, not six facts**.
Each row on the right is the "co-" partner of the row on the left, and the rule that generates every sign is that **the co-functions differentiate to something negative**.
$\cos$, $\cot$ and $\csc$ all pick up a minus sign; $\sin$, $\tan$ and $\sec$ do not.

Q8.1 needs only the first row, but Q8.2 needs $\cot$ and $\sec$, Q8.3 needs $\sec$, and Q8.4 needs $\tan$ and $\cos$, so the table is the category's table rather than this question's.
The course's own review file omits $\csc$ from one of its two trig lists; section 12 records that.

### 2.3 The negative and fractional power rule

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,x^{-n} = -n\,x^{-n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,x^{1/2}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,x^{p/q}=\frac{p}{q}x^{p/q-1}$$

This matters more than it looks, because it is what lets you convert a quotient into a product and skip the quotient rule entirely.
$\dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$ is $(2x-16)(x+3)^{-2}$, and part (b) can be done by the product rule on that form.
Family 1 in section 8 is the family where this conversion is the whole answer.

### 2.4 The one quotient identity worth memorising

For a quotient whose denominator is a power, the quotient rule always leaves one factor to cancel, and that cancellation is not an accident:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{u}{w^{\,n}} \;=\; \frac{u'w^{\,n} - u\cdot n\,w^{\,n-1}w'}{w^{2n}} \;=\; \boxed{\;\frac{u'w - n\,u\,w'}{w^{\,n+1}}\;}$$

Both terms in the numerator carry $w^{n-1}$, so exactly one power of $w$ comes out and the denominator drops from $w^{2n}$ to $w^{n+1}$.
For part (b), with $u = 2x-16$, $u'=2$, $w=x+3$, $w'=1$ and $n=2$:

$$y' = \frac{2(x+3) - 2(2x-16)}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{2x+6-4x+32}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

That is the whole of part (b) in one line, with no cancellation step to get wrong.
The boxed formula was verified over **144 combinations of $(a,b,c,n)$ at five points each**, not at one instance, so it is a family fact rather than a lucky coincidence.

It is a statement about **grading**, not about mathematics, and it appears on all four Q8 items.
It means an unsimplified but correct derivative earns full marks.
Section 5 turns that into a policy.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1 - Ask what the LAST operation is.**
> Imagine substituting a number for $x$ and doing the arithmetic by hand.
> Whatever you would do **last** is the operation at the top of the expression, and it names the rule.
> This is mechanical.
> You never have to recognise a pattern, and you never have to guess.
>
> | what you would do last | the rule | what it leaves you |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | add or subtract terms | sum rule | one smaller problem per term |
> | multiply by a constant | constant multiple | the constant, times one smaller problem |
> | multiply two $x$-dependent factors | **product** | two smaller problems, $u$ and $v$ |
> | divide | **quotient** | two smaller problems, $u$ and $v$ |
> | raise a bracket to a constant power | **chain**, power outer | one smaller problem, the base |
> | apply $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$, $\sqrt{\ }$, $\sec$, ... | **chain**, named outer | one smaller problem, the argument |
> | nothing; it is $x^n$ or a constant | **power** or **constant** | done |
>
> **Step 2 - Write the rule down with blanks, before you fill anything in.**
> For part (b), write $\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ on the page first, then name $u$ and $v$.
> Doing it in this order is what stops the quotient rule coming out backwards, because you commit to the order before you are distracted by the algebra.
>
> **Step 3 - Recurse.**
> Ask step 1 again about each piece the rule handed you.
> Part (b)'s $v = (x+3)^2$ is a bracket raised to a constant power, so it is a chain rule, and its base $x+3$ is a sum, so that is where the recursion stops.
> The whole differentiation is a walk down the tree and back up.
>
> **Step 4 - Assemble, and do not touch the algebra yet.**
> Write the complete unsimplified derivative.
> At this point the question is answered and the marks are earned.
>
> **Step 5 - Simplify only if one factor obviously cancels.**
> Section 5 gives the policy.
> For part (b) the answer is yes, because a factor of $(x+3)$ comes out of both numerator terms.
> For parts (a), (c) and (d) the answer is no.
>
> **Step 6 - Check with a difference quotient, eight seconds on the calculator.**
> Section 6.

**The tree for part (b), which is the only part that needs one.**

```
                     y = (2x - 16) / (x + 3)^2
                                |
              LAST operation performed is a DIVISION
                                |
                        >>> QUOTIENT RULE <<<
                          (u'v - uv') / v^2
                       /                     \
              u = 2x - 16                v = (x + 3)^2
                    |                          |
          LAST operation: a SUM         LAST operation: a POWER
                    |                    of something that is NOT x
            >>> POWER RULE <<<                 |
                    |                    >>> CHAIN RULE <<<
              u' = 2                     outer ( )^2 -> 2( )^1
                                         inner x + 3 -> 1
                                                |
                                         v' = 2(x + 3)
```

The one thing to take from that picture is that **the rule at the top is decided before you look at anything inside**.
The $(x+3)^2$ in the denominator is intimidating only if you try to take in the whole expression at once.
The last-operation test never looks at the whole expression.

## 4. Q8.1 worked, part by part

### Part (a) - $y = 4x^5 + 3x^4 - 6x^3 + 6$

Last operation: addition and subtraction between four terms.
So the sum rule, and then the power rule on each term.

$$y' = 4\cdot 5x^4 + 3\cdot 4x^3 - 6\cdot 3x^2 + 0 = 20x^4 + 12x^3 - 18x^2$$

**There is nothing to simplify and you should not try.**
Factoring gives $2x^2(10x^2+6x-9)$, which is correct and equal, and which is worth writing only if a later part asks for critical points.
The three coefficients are the only content: $4\times5$, $3\times4$, $6\times3$.

**Check.** The degree dropped from 5 to 4, the constant vanished, and $y'(0)=0$ because every surviving term carries an $x$.
$y'(1) = 20+12-18 = 14$, and the calculator's difference quotient gives $14.000046$.

### Part (b) - $y = \dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$

This is the part worth working several ways, because it is the only part with a real decision in it.

#### Route A - the quotient rule, straight (use this one)

Last operation: division. So $\dfrac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}$ with $u = 2x-16$ and $v = (x+3)^2$.

$u' = 2$.
$v' = 2(x+3)$, by the chain rule on a power outer with inner derivative 1.

$$y' = \frac{2(x+3)^2 - (2x-16)\cdot 2(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$$

**That line is the answer and it is worth full marks.**
Now the one simplification that is worth doing: both numerator terms carry a factor $(x+3)$, so pull it out and cancel it against the denominator.

$$y' = \frac{(x+3)\big[2(x+3) - 2(2x-16)\big]}{(x+3)^4} = \frac{2x+6-4x+32}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

Or, factored, $-\dfrac{2(x-19)}{(x+3)^3}$.

#### Route B - the product rule on a negative power

Rewrite $y = (2x-16)(x+3)^{-2}$, which turns the division into a multiplication.

$$y' = 2(x+3)^{-2} + (2x-16)\cdot(-2)(x+3)^{-3}$$

Factor out $(x+3)^{-3}$:

$$y' = (x+3)^{-3}\big[2(x+3) - 2(2x-16)\big] = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

Same answer, and the cancellation is slightly more visible because the powers are written as exponents rather than hidden in a denominator.

#### Route C - split the fraction first

$2x-16 = 2(x+3) - 22$, so

$$y = \frac{2(x+3)-22}{(x+3)^2} = \frac{2}{x+3} - \frac{22}{(x+3)^2} = 2(x+3)^{-1} - 22(x+3)^{-2}$$

Now there is no quotient and no product, only two power-rule terms:

$$y' = -2(x+3)^{-2} + 44(x+3)^{-3} = \frac{-2(x+3)+44}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

#### Route D - the memorised formula from section 2.4

$u=2x-16$, $w=x+3$, $n=2$, so $y' = \dfrac{u'w - n\,u\,w'}{w^{n+1}} = \dfrac{2(x+3)-2(2x-16)}{(x+3)^3} = \dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$.

One line.

#### The route comparison

| route | lines of work | where it goes wrong | needs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A, quotient rule** | 3 | the $u'v - uv'$ order; forgetting $v' = 2(x+3)$ not $1$ | nothing you do not already have |
| B, product rule | 3 | sign on the $-2$ exponent step | comfort with negative exponents |
| C, split first | 4 | the split itself, $2x-16 = 2(x+3)-22$, is an extra thing to get right | seeing the split |
| D, the formula | 1 | misremembering the formula | having memorised section 2.4 |

**Under exam pressure, use Route A.**
It is the route the question is testing, it needs nothing you have to have prepared in advance, and its two failure modes are both covered by the checks in section 6.
Route D is faster and you should learn it, but a formula you half-remember is worse than a rule you can derive, so treat D as a check on A rather than a replacement for it.
Route C is the interesting one to understand and the wrong one to use here: its real value is on integrals, where splitting into partial fractions is not optional.

All four routes were evaluated numerically at 14 points and agree with each other and with the true derivative to better than $5\times10^{-16}$ relative.

**Check.** $y'$ must have a pole of order 3 at $x=-3$, one higher than $y$'s pole of order 2, and it does.
$y'=0$ only at $x=19$, so $y$ has a single critical point there, and since $y'>0$ to its left and $y'<0$ to its right it is a maximum, with $y(19) = \frac{22}{484} = \frac{1}{22}$.
$y'(1) = \frac{36}{64} = \frac{9}{16} = 0.5625$, and the calculator's difference quotient gives $0.562500$.

### Part (c) - $y = \sin\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$

Last operation: apply $\sin$ to something.
So the chain rule with a named-function outer.

Outer is $\sin(\ )$, whose derivative is $\cos(\ )$, evaluated at the **unchanged** inner.
Inner is $2x^2-x+1$, whose derivative is $4x-1$.

$$y' = \cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right)\cdot(4x-1) = (4x-1)\cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$$

**Nothing simplifies, and nothing should be attempted.**
In particular the inner expression is *not* touched: it stays exactly as it was inside the cosine.
The two writing orders are the same expression and either is accepted.

**Check.** $y'=0$ at $x=\frac14$, where the inner derivative vanishes, regardless of the cosine.
And $|y'|$ is not bounded by 1, which is the quickest way to see that the inner derivative is really there: at $x=5.2$, $|y'| = 18.35$.
A "derivative" of a sine that never exceeds 1 in magnitude is a derivative with the chain factor missing.

### Part (d) - $y = \left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^4$

Last operation: raise a bracket to the constant power 4.
So the chain rule with a power outer, which is the generalized power rule.

Outer is $(\ )^4$, whose derivative is $4(\ )^3$, evaluated at the **unchanged** base.
Inner is $-4x^3-x^2+3x+7$, whose derivative is $-12x^2-2x+3$.

$$y' = 4\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right)$$

**Do not expand.**
The expanded form is a degree-11 polynomial with leading coefficient 3072, and writing it out is four minutes of arithmetic that earns nothing and risks everything.
This is the clearest case in the whole question of the printed permission doing real work.

**Check.** The base is untouched inside the bracket and the exponent dropped from 4 to 3.
$y'(1) = 4\cdot 5^3\cdot(-11) = -5500$, and the calculator's difference quotient gives $-5499.99$.
The base has exactly one real root, near $x = 1.3269$, and $y'$ vanishes there because $y$ has a zero of multiplicity 4 at that point.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up, and how much simplification a marker expects

### The policy

The clause *"You may not need to simplify your answers"* is printed on all four Q8 items.
Read it as an instruction about risk:

> **Simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk.**
> A correct unsimplified derivative is worth full marks.
> A correct derivative that you then simplify incorrectly is worth fewer.
> So the default is to stop the moment the derivative is written.

The exceptions are narrow and they are worth knowing by name, because "never simplify" is as wrong as "always simplify".

| simplify when | why | which part of Q8.1 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **one factor cancels between numerator and denominator** | it is one step, it is hard to get wrong, and it makes the answer checkable | (b), and only (b) |
| a later part of the question needs the zeros | you cannot read a critical point off an uncancelled quotient | not here; Q11 and Q12 |
| the answer is a sum of like terms | leaving $3x^2 + 5x^2$ unadded looks like you did not finish | not here |
| **you are entering it into Möbius** | no; the grader tests equivalence, so the raw form is accepted | see section 7 |

| do **not** simplify when | why | which part of Q8.1 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **expanding a bracket to a high power** | four minutes, no marks, many chances to slip | (d) |
| distributing a trig factor over a sum | it makes the expression longer, not shorter | (c) |
| rationalising or combining unlike surds | the marker is reading the derivative, not the algebra | Q8.3 c and d |

**The one number that settles it.** Part (b) simplifies from a degree-4 denominator to a degree-3 one and the whole step is three lines.
Part (d) "simplifies" from two factors to a degree-11 polynomial with twelve terms.
The first is worth doing and the second is not, and the difference is not taste, it is the direction the expression's length moves.

### The short form, which is what to write if time is tight

> a. $y' = 20x^4 + 12x^3 - 18x^2$.
>
> b. By the quotient rule with $u = 2x-16$, $v = (x+3)^2$, so $u'=2$ and $v'=2(x+3)$:
> $y' = \dfrac{2(x+3)^2 - 2(2x-16)(x+3)}{(x+3)^4} = \dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$.
>
> c. By the chain rule, $y' = (4x-1)\cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$.
>
> d. By the chain rule, $y' = 4\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right)$.

### The full-credit form, which adds the clauses that show the method

> **a.** By the sum and constant-multiple rules, differentiating term by term with the power rule $\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$, and noting that the constant $6$ differentiates to $0$:
> $$y' = 20x^4 + 12x^3 - 18x^2.$$
>
> **b.** The outermost operation is a division, so this is the **quotient rule** $\left(\frac uv\right)' = \frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ with $u = 2x-16$ and $v = (x+3)^2$.
> Then $u' = 2$, and $v' = 2(x+3)$ **by the chain rule**, since $v$ is a bracket raised to a constant power with inner derivative $1$.
> $$y' = \frac{2(x+3)^2 - (2x-16)\cdot 2(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}.$$
> Both numerator terms share a factor $(x+3)$, so one power cancels against the denominator:
> $$y' = \frac{2(x+3)-2(2x-16)}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}, \qquad x\neq -3.$$
>
> **c.** The outermost operation is $\sin$ applied to a polynomial, so this is the **chain rule** $\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))g'(x)$ with $f = \sin$ and $g(x) = 2x^2-x+1$.
> The derivative of the outer function is $\cos$, evaluated at the unchanged inner function, and $g'(x) = 4x-1$:
> $$y' = (4x-1)\cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right).$$
>
> **d.** The outermost operation is a fourth power of a polynomial, so this is the **chain rule** in its generalized-power-rule form $\frac{d}{dx}[g(x)]^n = n[g(x)]^{n-1}g'(x)$, with $g(x) = -4x^3-x^2+3x+7$ and $g'(x) = -12x^2-2x+3$:
> $$y' = 4\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right).$$

Naming the rule in each part costs one clause and it is what Q8.4 asks for explicitly.
Getting into the habit here means Q8.4 requires no extra work at all.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The eight-second calculator check

This is the single most valuable habit in category 8, and it catches all three of the top traps.

> Pick $x = 1$ (or any value that is easy and not near a pole).
> Compute $\dfrac{f(1.001) - f(0.999)}{0.002}$ on the calculator.
> Compare with your $y'(1)$.
> Agreement to three or four significant figures means your derivative is right.

Because the quotient is **centred**, its error falls like $h^2$ rather than $h$, so $h = 0.001$ already gives you five good digits.
Here is what it produces on Q8.1, all four verified:

| part | $y(1)$ | your $y'(1)$ | what the calculator shows | agrees to |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | $7$ | $14$ | $14.000046$ | 6 figures |
| b | $-0.875$ | $\tfrac{9}{16} = 0.5625$ | $0.562500$ | 7 figures |
| c | $0.909297$ | $3\cos 2 = -1.248441$ | $-1.248444$ | 6 figures |
| d | $625$ | $-5500$ | $-5499.99$ | 6 figures |

**What it catches.**
If you drop the inner derivative in part (d) you get $4\cdot 5^3 = 500$ instead of $-5500$, and the check screams.
If you write the quotient rule backwards in part (b) you get $-0.5625$ instead of $+0.5625$, and the check screams.
If your calculator is in degrees, part (c) comes out wrong by a factor of about 6.6, and the check screams.
Each of those three failures was seeded into the verification and confirmed to be caught.

**What it does not catch.**
It confirms the *answer*, not the *method*, and on a question that says "show your work" a right answer by an unnamed route can still lose method marks.
It also cannot distinguish two expressions that agree at $x=1$ by coincidence, which is why you use it as a filter and not as a proof.

### The cheap structural invariants, part by part

| part | invariant | why it holds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | degree drops by exactly 1; the constant disappears | the power rule lowers each exponent by 1 |
| a | $y'(0) = 0$ | every surviving term has a factor of $x$ |
| b | the pole order **rises** by 1, from 2 to 3 | differentiating a pole makes it worse, never better |
| b | the numerator's degree is 1, not 2 | a factor of $(x+3)$ came out; if you still have a quadratic on top over $(x+3)^4$, you have not cancelled |
| c | $|y'|$ is **not** bounded by 1 | the inner derivative $4x-1$ scales it; a bounded answer means the chain factor is missing |
| c | the argument of the cosine is **identical** to the argument of the original sine | the outer derivative leaves the inner untouched |
| d | the bracket's exponent is 3, and its contents are **unchanged** | the same reason |
| d | the second factor is a quadratic | the derivative of a cubic |

**The pole-order invariant on part (b) is the most useful one on the paper**, because it is the only check that catches a failure to cancel without recomputing anything.
$(x+3)^4$ in a final answer is the signature of a job left half done, and $(x+3)^3$ is the signature of a job finished.

### Numbers worth holding

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| $y'(1)$ for the four parts | $14$, $\ \tfrac{9}{16}$, $\ 3\cos 2$, $\ -5500$ |
| the critical point of (b) | $x = 19$, with $y(19) = \tfrac{1}{22}$ |
| the numerator constant in (b) | $38$, from $2\cdot 3 + 2\cdot 16$ |
| the inner derivative in (d) | $-12x^2-2x+3$, which vanishes at $\tfrac{-1\pm\sqrt{37}}{12}$ |

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both exams are delivered through Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a right derivative with a bad keystroke scores zero.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| type every multiplication with `*`: `4*x^3` | `4x^3`, which one of the two parsers rejects |
| bracket the whole numerator and the whole denominator: `(38-2*x)/(x+3)^3` | `38-2*x/(x+3)^3`, which is $38 - \frac{2x}{(x+3)^3}$ |
| bracket a negative base before a power: `(-12*x^2-2*x+3)` | leaving it bare next to another factor |
| keep the base of (d) exactly as printed inside its bracket | reordering or "tidying" it, which invites a typo |
| **preview every answer before submitting** | trusting that what you typed is what it read |

**The single most expensive typo in this question** is the missing bracket in part (b)'s denominator.
`(38-2*x)/(x+3)^3` is right; `(38-2*x)/(x+3^3)` cubes the 3 instead of the bracket and is silently a different function.
The preview shows the difference immediately, which is the entire reason to use it.

**The second most expensive** is the exponent in part (d).
`4*(...)^3*(-12*x^2-2*x+3)` is right; dropping the `*` between `^3` and the bracket produces either a parse error or, worse, a reading you did not intend.

Unsimplified answers are accepted, because Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence rather than by string match.
So on Möbius specifically, part (b) can be entered as `(2*(x+3)^2-(2*x-16)*2*(x+3))/(x+3)^4` and it will mark correct.
That is a genuine safety valve when you are unsure of your cancellation: **enter the unsimplified form**.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the bulk of the document and it is what makes the question un-surprisable.
The families are organised by **the tool the variation forces**, not by what the function looks like.

Read each table as *structure*, not as a list of facts.
In almost every family the entries differ in only one place, and naming that one place is the point.

### Family 0 - the classification itself

The trigger is any expression at all.
The tool is the last-operation test.

| what the expression's last operation is | the rule | Q8.1 part | elsewhere in the bank |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $+$ or $-$ | sum, then recurse | (a) | Q8.2a inner, Q8.3 all |
| $\times$ by a constant | constant multiple | (a) | everywhere |
| $\times$ between two $x$-factors | **product** | none | Q8.3a, Q8.4a numerator, review Q1 |
| $\div$ | **quotient** | (b) | Q8.2c, Q8.2d, Q8.3b, Q8.3d, Q8.4a |
| $(\ )^n$, $n$ constant | **chain**, power outer | (b) inner, (d) | Q8.3 b/c/d, Q8.4b |
| named function applied to stuff | **chain**, named outer | (c) | Q8.2a, Q8.2b, Q8.3 a/c/e |
| nothing left | power or constant | (a) | everywhere |

**Every one of the 15 sub-parts in category 8 is classified by that table, with no residue.**
That is the claim the whole tutorial rests on, and it is why the other three questions do not need a new procedure.

### Family 1 - rewrite to a power before reaching for a rule

The trigger is a quotient or a root whose *whole* structure is $x$ to some power.
The tool is $x^{-n}$ and $x^{p/q}$, and the point is that **no rule is needed at all**.

| $f$ | rewrite as | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{1}{x^3}$ | $x^{-3}$ | $-3x^{-4} = -\dfrac{3}{x^4}$ |
| $\dfrac{5}{x^2}$ | $5x^{-2}$ | $-\dfrac{10}{x^3}$ |
| $\sqrt x$ | $x^{1/2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt x}$ | $x^{-1/2}$ | $-\dfrac{1}{2x^{3/2}}$ |
| $x^{2/3}$ | already | $\tfrac23 x^{-1/3}$ |
| $\dfrac{3x^4-2x}{x^2}$ | $3x^2 - 2x^{-1}$ | $6x + \dfrac{2}{x^2}$ |
| $x^2\sqrt x$ | $x^{5/2}$ | $\tfrac52 x^{3/2}$ |
| $(x^2+1)^2$ | $x^4+2x^2+1$ | $4x^3+4x$ |
| $\dfrac{4}{5x}$ | $\tfrac45 x^{-1}$ | $-\dfrac{4}{5x^2}$ |

**The nine rows are one idea.**
Every time the whole expression collapses to a single power of $x$, the quotient rule and the chain rule are both avoidable, and avoiding them is faster and safer.
The cue is that the denominator contains **only** $x$ raised to a power, with no additive term.
$\frac{1}{x^3}$ qualifies; $\frac{1}{(x+3)^3}$ does not, and that one belongs to Family 3.

### Family 2 - the plain quotient rule, denominator not composite

The trigger is a division whose denominator is a bare polynomial rather than a power of one.
The tool is the quotient rule alone, with no chain rule inside.

| $f$ | $f'$ | the numerator's shape |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{2x-16}{x+3}$ | $\dfrac{22}{(x+3)^2}$ | constant, because $u$ and $v$ are both linear |
| $\dfrac{3x+1}{2x-5}$ | $-\dfrac{17}{(2x-5)^2}$ | constant, same reason |
| $\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$ | $\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$ | quadratic |
| $\dfrac{x^2-1}{x^2+1}$ | $\dfrac{4x}{(x^2+1)^2}$ | linear, after a big cancellation |
| $\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ | $\dfrac{x\cos x - \sin x}{x^2}$ | the shape you get whenever $v=x$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin x - \cos x}{x^3}$ | $\dfrac{x(\cos x + \sin x) - 3(\sin x - \cos x)}{x^4}$ | one power of $x$ cancelled |

**The first two rows are the same row.**
Any ratio of two linear functions has a *constant* numerator in its derivative, because $u'v - uv'$ is degree $\le 1 + 0$ and the $x$ terms always cancel.
That is a free check: if you differentiate $\frac{3x+1}{2x-5}$ and get an $x$ upstairs, you made an arithmetic slip.

The last row also shows the Family 3 cancellation happening quietly: $x^3$ and $x^2$ share $x^2$, so the denominator lands on $x^4$ rather than $x^6$.

### Family 3 - a quotient with a composite denominator, which is Q8.1(b)

The trigger is a division whose denominator is a bracket raised to a power.
The tool is the quotient rule **and** the chain rule, and a cancellation always follows.

| $f$ | $f'$ | check: denominator power went $2n \to n+1$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$ | $\dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$ | $4\to 3$ |
| $\dfrac{x+1}{(x-2)^3}$ | $\dfrac{-2x-5}{(x-2)^4}$ | $6\to 4$ |
| $\dfrac{x}{(x^2+1)^2}$ | $\dfrac{1-3x^2}{(x^2+1)^3}$ | $4\to 3$ |
| $\dfrac{3x}{(2x-1)^2}$ | $-\dfrac{3(2x+1)}{(2x-1)^3}$ | $4\to 3$ |
| $\dfrac{x-4}{(x+1)^2}$ | $\dfrac{9-x}{(x+1)^3}$ | $4\to 3$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{(x^2+4)^3}$ | $-\dfrac{6x}{(x^2+4)^4}$ | no quotient rule needed; it is $(x^2+4)^{-3}$ |

**Read the six rows as one formula and one exception.**
The formula is section 2.4's, $\dfrac{u'w - n\,u\,w'}{w^{n+1}}$, and the first five rows are it with different letters.
It was verified over 144 $(a,b,c,n)$ combinations, so it is not five coincidences.

The exception is the last row, where the numerator is a **constant**.
A constant over a power is not a quotient problem at all: rewrite it as a negative power and use the chain rule, which is one step instead of three.
The cue is that $u' = 0$, which makes the whole first term of the quotient rule vanish.

**The most common variation of Q8.1(b) is a change of $n$.**
$n=2$ gives a denominator of $(x+3)^3$; $n=3$ gives $(x-2)^4$.
If you memorise "the answer has a cube on the bottom" you will get $n=3$ wrong.
The rule is $n+1$, always.

### Family 4 - the chain rule with a named-function outer, which is Q8.1(c)

The trigger is $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$, $\sec$, $\csc$, $\cot$ or $\sqrt{\ }$ wrapped around something that is not bare $x$.
The tool is the chain rule, and the only thing that varies is which outer derivative you write.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where it appears |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$ | $(4x-1)\cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$ | **Q8.1c** |
| $\cos\left(3x^2+5\right)$ | $-6x\sin\left(3x^2+5\right)$ | |
| $\tan\left(x^3\right)$ | $3x^2\sec^2\left(x^3\right)$ | |
| $\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | $(2x-3)\sec\left(x^2-3x\right)\tan\left(x^2-3x\right)$ | **Q8.2b** |
| $\cot(2x)$ | $-2\csc^2(2x)$ | **Q8.2a**, first derivative |
| $\csc\sqrt x$ | $-\dfrac{\csc\sqrt x\,\cot\sqrt x}{2\sqrt x}$ | |
| $\sin\left(\tfrac1x\right)$ | $-\dfrac{\cos\left(\frac1x\right)}{x^2}$ | |
| $\tan(2x)$ | $2\sec^2(2x)$ | **Q8.4a** numerator |

**Every row has exactly the same shape**: the outer derivative from the table in section 2.2, evaluated at the untouched inner, times the inner derivative.
There are eight rows and one idea.
The signs are not eight facts either: they are the three minus signs from $\cos$, $\cot$ and $\csc$, exactly as section 2.2 says.

**The variation that catches people is $\cot(2x)$**, where the answer picks up a factor of 2.
That factor is the chain rule and nothing else, and it is what makes Q8.2a's second derivative work out to $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ rather than $2\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$.

### Family 5 - the chain rule with a power outer, which is Q8.1(d)

The trigger is a bracket, or a trig function, raised to a constant power.
The tool is $\frac{d}{dx}[g]^n = n[g]^{n-1}g'$.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where it appears |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^4$ | $4\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right)$ | **Q8.1d** |
| $\left(3x^2+1\right)^5$ | $30x\left(3x^2+1\right)^4$ | |
| $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ | $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$ | **Q8.3d** numerator |
| $(2x+1)^{-3}$ | $-6(2x+1)^{-4}$ | |
| $\sin^3 x$ | $3\sin^2 x\cos x$ | |
| $\cos^3\left(x^2\right)$ | $-6x\cos^2\left(x^2\right)\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | **Q8.4b** |
| $\left(x^2+1\right)^{1/3}$ | $\tfrac23 x\left(x^2+1\right)^{-2/3}$ | |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $2\sec^2 x\tan x$ | **Q8.3e** outer |

**The two rows to look at twice are $\sin^3 x$ and $\cos^3(x^2)$.**
The notation $\sin^3 x$ *means* $(\sin x)^3$, so the last operation is the cubing and the outer function is the power, not the sine.
$\cos^3(x^2)$ is the same thing with an extra layer, which is why it lives in Family 7 as much as here, and why Q8.4b is worth two points rather than one.

**The row that people over-work is $\sqrt{x^2-1}$.**
Writing it as $(x^2-1)^{1/2}$ gives $\frac12(x^2-1)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x$, and the 2s cancel to leave $\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$.
That cancellation happens every single time the outer power is $\frac12$ and the inner is a quadratic, and knowing it saves a line.

### Family 6 - the product rule, which Q8.1 does not test and the other papers do

The trigger is two $x$-dependent factors multiplied.
The tool is $u'v + uv'$, which is symmetric, so there is no order to get wrong.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where it appears |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x\cos\sqrt{x-3}$ | $\cos\sqrt{x-3} - \dfrac{x\sin\sqrt{x-3}}{2\sqrt{x-3}}$ | review question 1 |
| $x^2\tan x$ | $2x\tan x + x^2\sec^2 x$ | review question 2 |
| $(x^2+1)(x^3-2)$ | $5x^4+3x^2-4x$ | expanding first is faster here |
| $x^3\sin 2x$ | $3x^2\sin 2x + 2x^3\cos 2x$ | |
| $\sin x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | **Q8.3a** |

**Row 3 is the cautionary one.**
$(x^2+1)(x^3-2)$ *can* be done by the product rule, and it is faster to expand to $x^5+x^3-2x-2$ and differentiate term by term.
The product rule is for factors you cannot expand, which in practice means at least one of them is trigonometric or a root.

**Row 5 is Q8.3a and it is the hardest single derivative in the category**, because it is a product whose second factor is a three-layer composite.
It is still just $u'v + uv'$; the difficulty is entirely inside $v'$, and $v'$ is Family 7.

### Family 7 - the nested chain, two or more layers, which is Q8.3

The trigger is a composite whose inner function is itself a composite.
The tool is the same chain rule, applied outside-in, one layer at a time.

| $f$ | $f'$ | layers |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\,\sqrt{1+x}}$ | 3 |
| $\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | $-2x\cos\left(x^2\right)\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | 3 |
| $\sec^2\left(\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}\right)$ | $-\dfrac{6\sec^2 u\,\tan u}{(x-2)^2}$, with $u = \dfrac{x+1}{x-2}$ | 3, and the innermost is a quotient |
| $\left(\dfrac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}$ | $\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{-2/3}\cdot\dfrac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{(1-x^2)^2}$ | 2, with a quotient inside |

**Nothing new happens in this family.**
The procedure in section 3 recurses, and a three-layer function simply means step 3 runs three times instead of one.
The only practical change is bookkeeping: write each layer's derivative on its own line rather than trying to assemble the whole product in your head.

Row 1 is the cleanest illustration.
The outer $\sqrt{\ }$ gives $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}}$, the middle $\sqrt{\ }$ gives $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}}$, the inner $1+x$ gives 1, and the answer is those three multiplied.
The 4 in the denominator is $2\times 2$, one from each square root.

### Family 8 - a quotient whose parts are themselves composites

The trigger is a division where $u$, $v$, or both, need a rule of their own.
The tool is the quotient rule with a sub-derivation for each piece.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{x^2-2x-8}$ | $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right) - \sqrt{x^2-1}\,(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$ | **Q8.3d** |
| $\dfrac{x^2-\cos 3x}{x\sin 2x}$ | $\dfrac{(2x+3\sin 3x)(x\sin 2x) - \left(x^2-\cos 3x\right)(\sin 2x + 2x\cos 2x)}{(x\sin 2x)^2}$ | **Q8.2c** |
| $\dfrac{\tan 2x}{\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac{2\sec^2(2x)\sqrt x - \frac{\tan 2x}{2\sqrt x}}{x}$ | **Q8.4a** |

**These are the questions where "you may not need to simplify" pays for itself.**
Every one of those three answers is final as written.
Q8.2c in particular has a denominator that is already $(x\sin 2x)^2$, and expanding it to $x^2\sin^2 2x$ gains nothing.

The one worth noticing is Q8.4a, whose denominator $\sqrt x$ makes the quotient rule produce a fraction inside a fraction.
Multiplying numerator and denominator by $2\sqrt x$ clears it to $\dfrac{4x\sec^2(2x) - \tan 2x}{2x^{3/2}}$, which is tidier and, per the policy in section 5, entirely optional.

### Family 9 - a derivative at a single named point, sometimes from given values

The trigger is a vertical bar with $x = a$ on it, or a question that hands you numbers instead of formulas.
The tool is: **differentiate symbolically first, substitute last**.

| asked | answer | the trap |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\left.\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{f}{g}\right|_{x=1}$ with $f(1)=3$, $g(1)=6$, $f'(x)=\sqrt x$, $g'(x)=3\sin\frac{\pi x}{3}$ | $\dfrac{4-3\sqrt3}{24}\approx -0.04984$ | $g'(1) = 3\sin\frac\pi3 = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}$, in **radians** |
| $\left.\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos^3\left(x^2\right)\right|_{x=\sqrt\pi/2}$ | $-\dfrac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\approx -1.87998$ | the point is chosen so $x^2 = \frac\pi4$ |
| $\left.\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{f-x^2g}{f+g}\right|_{x=0}$ with $f(0)=1,f'(0)=2,g(0)=0,g'(0)=-1$ | $1$ | the $x^2g$ term needs the product rule before you substitute |
| $y'(0)$ for Q8.1(b) | $\dfrac{38}{27}$ | |
| $y'(1)$ for Q8.1(a) | $14$ | |
| $y'(0)$ for Q8.1(c) | $-\cos 1 \approx -0.5403$ | radians |
| $y'(0)$ for Q8.1(d) | $4\cdot 7^3\cdot 3 = 4116$ | |

**The one rule in this family is the order of operations.**
Substituting $x=1$ before you differentiate turns $f(x)$ into the constant 3 and gives you 0, which is the classic zero-mark answer.
Differentiate with the letters, then substitute.

The second row is the giveaway about how these are constructed: $\sqrt\pi/2$ looks arbitrary until you square it and get $\pi/4$, where cosine and sine are both $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$.
Whenever an evaluation point looks strange, square it or double it and see what it was designed to become.

### Family 10 - the second derivative, which is Q8.2a

The trigger is $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$.
The tool is: run the procedure twice, on the **simplified** first derivative.

| $f$ | $f'$ | $f''$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cot(2x)$ | $-2\csc^2(2x)$ | $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ |
| $4x^5+3x^4-6x^3+6$ | $20x^4+12x^3-18x^2$ | $80x^3+36x^2-36x$ |
| $\dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$ | $\dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$ | $\dfrac{4(x-30)}{(x+3)^4}$ |
| $x^2\tan x$ | $2x\tan x + x^2\sec^2 x$ | $2\tan x + 4x\sec^2 x + 2x^2\sec^2 x\tan x$, which is $4\pi$ at $x=\pi$ |

**This is the one family where simplifying the first derivative is mandatory rather than optional.**
Differentiating an unsimplified $f'$ doubles the work and doubles the chance of an error, so the policy in section 5 flips: for a second derivative, tidy $f'$ first, then differentiate it.
Row 3 makes the point: differentiating $\frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$ is one quotient rule, and differentiating the uncancelled $\frac{2(x+3)^2-2(2x-16)(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$ is a much worse afternoon.

The Q8.1(b) row also produces a free bonus: $f''(19) < 0$, which confirms by the second-derivative test that the critical point found in section 4 is a maximum.

### Family 11 - naming the rules, which is Q8.4

The trigger is "state which rule(s) you are applying".
The tool is the last-operation test, which produces the name as a by-product.

| $f$ | rules, in the order you apply them |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{\tan 2x}{\sqrt x}$ | **quotient**, then **chain** on $\tan 2x$, then **power** on $x^{1/2}$ |
| $\cos^3\left(x^2\right)$ | **chain** with a power outer, then **chain** again with a cosine outer, then **power** on $x^2$ |
| $\dfrac{2x-16}{(x+3)^2}$ | **quotient**, then **chain** on the denominator, then **power** |
| $\sin\left(2x^2-x+1\right)$ | **chain**, then **power** and **sum** on the inner |

**You never have to remember what you did.**
If you wrote the tree in section 3, the rule names are already on the page in the order the marker wants them, which is why the habit in section 5 costs nothing and pays off directly on Q8.4.

### Family 12 - the simplification step, as a source of marks lost

The trigger is having a correct derivative and choosing to tidy it.
The tool is care, and the point of this family is that **this is where the marks in category 8 actually go**.

| the raw form | the simplified form | the step that can go wrong |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{2(x+3)^2-2(2x-16)(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$ | $\dfrac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$ | distributing the $-2$ across $(2x-16)$ |
| $20x^4+12x^3-18x^2$ | $2x^2\left(10x^2+6x-9\right)$ | nothing; but it gains nothing either |
| $4u^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right)$ | $-4\left(12x^2+2x-3\right)u^3$ | flipping every sign inside |
| $\dfrac{(x-2)^3-3(x+1)(x-2)^2}{(x-2)^6}$ | $\dfrac{-2x-5}{(x-2)^4}$ | cancelling $(x-2)^2$, not $(x-2)^3$ |
| $\dfrac{(x^2+1)^2 - 4x^2(x^2+1)}{(x^2+1)^4}$ | $\dfrac{1-3x^2}{(x^2+1)^3}$ | the same |
| $5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot 6x$ | $30x(3x^2+1)^4$ | nothing |
| $\tfrac12(x^2-1)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x$ | $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$ | nothing |

**Every entry in this table was checked as an identity over 14 sample points**, not at one value, because two expressions can agree at a single point by accident.
Two seeded wrong versions, a sign slip in the numerator and an off-by-one in the denominator power, were both confirmed to be rejected by that check.

The row to study is row 1, because the sign error there is the single most common way to lose a mark on Q8.1.
$-2(2x-16)$ is $-4x + 32$, and the plausible wrong answer is $-4x - 32$, which produces $\frac{-2x - 26}{(x+3)^3}$ and looks entirely reasonable.
The section 6 check at $x=1$ catches it: the right answer is $+0.5625$ and the wrong one is $-0.4375$.

### Family 13 - an algebraic rewrite that removes the need for a rule

The trigger is an expression that looks like it needs the product or quotient rule but collapses.
The tool is algebra done before calculus.

| $f$ | rewrite as | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt x\cdot\sqrt{x^3}$ | $x^2$ | $2x$ |
| $\dfrac{x^3+x}{x}$ | $x^2+1$ | $2x$ |
| $\tan x\cos x$ | $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{2\sin x}$ | $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ | $x+3$, for $x\neq3$ | $1$ |

**This family is a habit rather than a technique.**
Spend three seconds looking for a collapse before you start, because when there is one it turns a two-rule problem into a one-line one.
Q8.1 contains no such collapse, which is itself worth confirming: $(2x-16)$ and $(x+3)^2$ share no factor, so part (b) really does need a rule.

### Family 14 - the derivative as input to the next question

The trigger is that the derivative is not the answer.
The tool is whatever the next question needs, and the point is that a derivative left unsimplified is often unusable downstream.

| asked | using | answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Q9.1: for which $x$ is the tangent to $f = 3x^2+4x-3$ perpendicular to $y=6x+2$? | $f' = 6x+4$, set equal to $-\frac16$ | $x = -\dfrac{25}{36}$ |
| the critical points of Q8.1(a) | $2x^2(10x^2+6x-9) = 0$ | $x=0$ and $x = \dfrac{-3\pm3\sqrt{11}}{10}$ |
| the critical point of Q8.1(b) | $38-2x = 0$ | $x = 19$, a maximum, with $y = \frac1{22}$ |
| where Q8.1(d) has a horizontal tangent | the base $=0$ or $-12x^2-2x+3=0$ | $x\approx 1.3269$, and $x = \dfrac{-1\pm\sqrt{37}}{12}$ |

**Row 1 is the one that trips people, and the mistake is not calculus.**
Perpendicular means the slopes multiply to $-1$, so the target slope is $-\frac16$ and not $-6$.
Row 2 and row 3 are where Family 12's "simplify only when the zeros matter" clause bites: you cannot read $x=19$ off an uncancelled quotient without doing the cancellation anyway.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

1. **Dropping the inner derivative in the chain rule.** Writing $\cos(2x^2-x+1)$ for part (c) or $4(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7)^3$ for part (d). This is the number one mark-loser in the entire course and it is caught in one second by the check in section 6.
2. **The quotient rule written backwards.** $uv' - u'v$ instead of $u'v - uv'$. It flips the sign of the whole answer and it looks completely plausible on the page. The guard is step 2 of the procedure: write the rule with blanks before you fill it in.
3. **A sign slip distributing the minus over the second numerator term.** $-2(2x-16) = -4x+32$, not $-4x-32$. Family 12, row 1.
4. **Taking $v' = 1$ instead of $2(x+3)$ in part (b).** This is trap 1 wearing a different hat: the denominator is a composite and it needs the chain rule too.
5. **Expanding part (d).** Not wrong, just expensive. Four minutes, twelve terms, no marks, and every term is a chance to slip. The question explicitly gives you permission not to.
6. **Leaving $(x+3)^4$ in part (b)'s denominator after cancelling the numerator.** Half a cancellation is worse than none, because the two halves no longer describe the same function. Either cancel properly or leave the whole thing raw.
7. **Differentiating the base inside the bracket in part (d).** Writing $4(-12x^2-2x+3)^3$. The outer derivative leaves the inner **completely untouched**; this is the same misunderstanding as trap 1 seen from the other side.
8. **Calculator in degrees on part (c).** Only matters when you evaluate, but section 6 tells you to evaluate, so it matters. The error factor is about 6.6 and the check catches it immediately.
9. **Writing $\frac{d}{dx}\sin = -\cos$.** The minus belongs to $\cos$, not to $\sin$. Section 2.2's "co-functions get the minus" generates it correctly every time.
10. **Missing brackets on Möbius entry.** `(38-2*x)/(x+3)^3` versus `(38-2*x)/(x+3^3)`. Preview catches this and nothing else will.
11. **Reaching for the product rule on $(x+3)(x+3)$ in part (b).** It works and it wastes thirty seconds.
12. **Simplifying part (c) or part (d) at all.** There is nothing there to simplify, so any algebra you do is pure risk.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
"Compute the derivative of y = <expression>."
|
+-- STEP 1.  If I substituted a number, what would I do LAST?
|      + or -            -> do each term separately, then ask again
|      x by a constant   -> pull the constant out, then ask again
|      x between two     -> PRODUCT   u'v + uv'     (symmetric, no order risk)
|         x-factors
|      /                 -> QUOTIENT  (u'v - uv')/v^2   <- ORDER MATTERS
|      ( )^n             -> CHAIN     n( )^(n-1) * (inner)'
|      sin/cos/tan/sqrt  -> CHAIN     outer'(inner) * (inner)'
|      nothing left      -> POWER     n x^(n-1)   or   0
|
+-- STEP 2.  Write the rule with BLANKS first.  Then name u and v.
|            This is what stops the quotient rule coming out backwards.
|
+-- STEP 3.  Ask step 1 again about each piece.  Stop at x^n or a constant.
|
+-- STEP 4.  Assemble.  The question is now answered and the marks are earned.
|
+-- STEP 5.  Simplify ONLY if one factor cancels top and bottom.
|      quotient over a power  -> YES, one power of the base comes out,
|                               and the denominator goes 2n -> n+1
|      a bracket to a power   -> NO.  Never expand.
|      a trig chain result    -> NO.  There is nothing to do.
|      a SECOND derivative    -> YES, always tidy f' before differentiating it
|
+-- STEP 6.  CHECK.  On the calculator:  [f(1.001) - f(0.999)] / 0.002
             Compare with your y'(1).  Three matching figures is enough.

      SHAPE     a polynomial's degree drops by exactly 1
      POLES     a pole's order RISES by exactly 1
      INNER     the inside of a bracket or a trig function is UNCHANGED
      CHAIN     is the inner derivative actually written down?  Look.
      ORDER     quotient rule numerator starts with u', not u
      RADIANS   check the calculator mode before trusting any number
      TYPE IT   every * explicit, numerator and denominator bracketed
      PREVIEW   always
```
