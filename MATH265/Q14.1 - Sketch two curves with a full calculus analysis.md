## 0. The 30-second version

```
        DOMAIN  ->  INTERCEPTS  ->  SYMMETRY  ->  ASYMPTOTES
                                                      |
                                                      v
            f'  ->  critical pts (f'=0 AND f' undefined)  ->  sign chart  ->  inc/dec
                                                      |
                                                      v
            f'' ->  candidates (f''=0 AND f'' undefined) ->  SIGN CHANGE?  ->  concavity
                                                      |
                                                      v
                          ASSEMBLE, then CROSS-CHECK, then draw
```

Two lines to hold on to while you do it.

- **Every later step is filtered by the domain**, so the domain goes first and never gets revisited.
- **A feature is only a feature at a point of the domain**, so a sign change of $f''$ across an excluded point is not an inflection.

## 2. The seven things you must have cold

### 2.1 The four shapes

The signs of $f'$ and $f''$ together fix the shape of the arc, and there are exactly four.

| $f'$ | $f''$ | shape | reads as |
| :---: | :---: | :--- | :--- |
| $+$ | $+$ | increasing, concave up | rising and steepening |
| $+$ | $-$ | increasing, concave down | rising and flattening |
| $-$ | $+$ | decreasing, concave up | falling and flattening |
| $-$ | $-$ | decreasing, concave down | falling and steepening |

Taught in the Q5.1 / Q5.2 / Q5.3 family, which is where the sign-to-shape translation is drilled.
Here it is used as a *lookup*: once the two sign charts exist, every arc of the sketch is determined.

### 2.2 Three kinds of non-differentiable point, and only one of them is a cusp

| name | one-sided slopes | example | is it a cusp? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :---: |
| corner | finite, unequal | $\lvert x^2-4\rvert$ at $x=\pm 2$ | no |
| cusp | infinite, **opposite** signs | $x^{2/3}(x-5)$ at $x=0$ | **yes** |
| vertical tangent | infinite, **same** sign | $x^{1/3}$ at $x=0$ | no |

Both parts of Q14.1 have none of the three, and the stem asks about cusps anyway.
That is the point: the examiner wants the discrimination demonstrated, and the way to demonstrate it in one line is to say *why* there are none.

### 2.3 An inflection needs a sign change, at a point of the domain

$f''=0$ is neither necessary nor sufficient.

- $x^4$ has $f''(0)=0$ and no inflection, because $f''=12x^2$ does not change sign.
- $x^{1/3}$ has $f''$ **undefined** at $0$ and $x=0$ **is** an inflection, because the concavity flips.
- Part (a) has $f''$ changing sign across $x=\pm 1$ and **no** inflection, because $\pm 1$ are not in the domain.

All three are verified in `q141d` family 7 and `q141` block I.

### 2.4 A critical point is $f'=0$ **or** $f'$ undefined

A cusp is a critical point.
This matters for the order of the checklist and section 3 returns to it.

### 2.5 $f'=0$ does not mean extremum

It means a horizontal tangent.
Part (b) has infinitely many horizontal tangents and no extrema at all.

### 2.6 Asymptote rules

| kind | test |
| :--- | :--- |
| vertical | **cancel common factors first**, then the surviving denominator's zeros |
| horizontal | $\deg N < \deg D \Rightarrow y=0$; $\deg N = \deg D \Rightarrow y = \tfrac{\text{lead }N}{\text{lead }D}$; $\deg N > \deg D \Rightarrow$ none |
| slant | exactly when $\deg N = \deg D + 1$; divide, and the quotient is the line |

Q4.1 is the tutorial for this, and section 12 records a defect in the course notes' version of the vertical rule that part (a) cannot detect.

### 2.7 A curve may cross a horizontal asymptote and may never cross a vertical one

An asymptote is a statement about a limit, not a barrier.
$x/(x^2+1)$ crosses $y=0$ at the origin.
Part (a) happens never to cross its $y=1$, and that is a fact about this curve, proved in section 6, not a general rule.

## 3. The universal procedure, and why the order is the order

> 1. **Domain.** Where is $f$ defined? Write the excluded points down as a set. Everything after this step is filtered by it.
> 2. **Intercepts.** $f(0)$ for the $y$-intercept; solve $f(x)=0$ for the $x$-intercepts.
> 3. **Symmetry.** Test $f(-x)$ against $f(x)$ and $-f(x)$. Even or odd halves the remaining work.
> 4. **Asymptotes.** Vertical (after cancelling), horizontal (by degree), slant (only if $\deg N = \deg D + 1$).
> 5. **$f'$.** Compute it. Find **both** kinds of critical point: $f'=0$, and $f'$ undefined while $f$ is defined. Classify any non-differentiable point as corner, cusp or vertical tangent **now**.
> 6. **Sign chart of $f'$.** Break the line at every excluded point *and* every critical point. Read off increasing and decreasing, then classify each critical point by the sign change.
> 7. **$f''$.** Compute it from $f'$. Find candidates: $f''=0$ and $f''$ undefined.
> 8. **Sign chart of $f''$.** Break at the same excluded points plus the candidates. An inflection is a candidate **in the domain** where the sign actually changes.
> 9. **Assemble and cross-check.** Section 6. Only then draw.

### Why this order and not another

**Domain first, because it is the filter.**
Every later step produces candidate $x$-values, and every one of them has to be tested for membership in the domain.
Doing it first means the test is a lookup; doing it last means re-auditing four lists.
Part (a) is the clean demonstration: $x=\pm 1$ turn up as denominators in $f'$ and $f''$ and as the places the concavity flips, and each time the answer is "not in the domain, so not a feature".

**Symmetry before the derivatives, because it halves them.**
Part (a) is even, so the sign chart on $x<0$ is the mirror of $x>0$ and the asymptote behaviour at $x=-1$ is forced by the behaviour at $x=1$.
Part (b) is odd, so the whole picture on $x<0$ is a rotation of $x>0$.
Cheap, and it is a free cross-check on the derivative work later.

**Asymptotes before $f'$, because they tell you the shape of the ends.**
Knowing part (a) tends to $1$ at both infinities means the monotonicity you derive on $(1,\infty)$ must be *decreasing towards 1*, and if your sign chart says otherwise, one of the two is wrong.

**Cusps in step 5, not at the end.**
This is the ordering the course notes get wrong, and it matters.
A cusp is a critical point, so it must appear in the sign chart of $f'$ in step 6.
`MATH265_Review.md`'s checklist puts "Cusps" at step 9, after both sign charts and immediately before the sketch, which means steps 6 and 8 were built without a break the function actually has.
On a question with a cusp, that costs the monotonicity table.

**$f''$ after the $f'$ sign chart, not interleaved with it.**
Q8.2 records that the differentiation *rule* can change between the first and second pass.
Part (a) is exactly that: $f$ needs the quotient rule, and $f' = -2x(x^2-1)^{-2}$ is fastest by the product rule with a chain factor.
Finishing one pass before starting the next keeps the two from contaminating each other.

**Assemble last, and cross-check before drawing.**
A sketch is the only deliverable that can silently contradict the lists above it.
Section 6 gives the three checks that catch it.

### Does the same order serve both parts?

Yes, and this is worth saying because the two functions could not look less alike.

| | part (a) $x^2/(x^2-1)$ | part (b) $x+\sin x$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| kind | rational | line plus a bounded oscillation |
| domain | $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-1,1\}$ | all of $\mathbb{R}$ |
| symmetry | **even** | **odd** |
| asymptotes | two vertical, one horizontal | **none** |
| feature count | finite: one critical point | **infinite**, one family per period |
| critical points | one, and it **is** an extremum | infinitely many, and **none** is an extremum |
| inflections | **none** | infinitely many |
| what step 4 returns | three asymptotes | nothing |
| what step 8 returns | nothing | everything |
| how the answer is written | as points | as a **formula in $k$** |

The order serves both.
What differs is which steps return something, and the two parts are near-exact complements: **(a) has asymptotes and no inflections, (b) has inflections and no asymptotes.**
That is not a coincidence, it is the design of the question, and noticing it is the fastest way to check you have not mixed up which answer belongs to which part.

Only two things change in practice.

- **Step 3 gains weight on (b).** Because the features repeat, you solve one period and then state the answer with an integer parameter $k$. Writing out five instances instead of a formula is where marks leak.
- **Step 6's classification changes tool on (b).** $f'=1+\cos x$ touches zero without crossing it, so the second-derivative test is silent ($f''=0$ too) and the sign chart shows no change. You need the first-derivative test, and its verdict is "neither".

## 4. Both parts worked, in checklist order

### 4a. $f(x) = \dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}$

**Step 1, domain.**
$x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)=0$ at $x=\pm 1$.
Numerator $x^2$ and denominator $x^2-1$ share no factor, so nothing cancels.
Domain $(-\infty,-1)\cup(-1,1)\cup(1,\infty)$.

**Step 2, intercepts.**
$f(0)=\dfrac{0}{-1}=0$.
$f(x)=0$ needs $x^2=0$, so $x=0$ only, and it is a **double** root, so the curve touches the axis rather than crossing it.
The single point $(0,0)$ is both intercepts.

**Step 3, symmetry.**
$f(-x)=\dfrac{(-x)^2}{(-x)^2-1}=\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}=f(x)$.
**Even.** Symmetric about the $y$-axis.
Checked exactly, in rational arithmetic, at 121 sample points, and checked *not* to be odd at all 34 valid points of a second sample.

**Step 4, asymptotes.**

- *Vertical.* Nothing cancelled, so both zeros of the denominator survive: $x=-1$ and $x=1$.
  Signs: on $(1,\infty)$ the denominator is positive so $f\to+\infty$ as $x\to1^+$; on $(-1,1)$ it is negative so $f\to-\infty$ as $x\to 1^-$.
  Evenness gives the other pole for free: $f\to+\infty$ as $x\to-1^-$ and $f\to-\infty$ as $x\to-1^+$.
- *Horizontal.* Degrees are equal ($2$ and $2$) and both leading coefficients are $1$, so $y=1$, on both sides.
- *Slant.* Needs $\deg N=\deg D+1$, and $2\neq 3$. None.

**Step 5, $f'$.**
Quotient rule:
$$f'(x)=\frac{2x(x^2-1)-x^2(2x)}{(x^2-1)^2}=\frac{2x^3-2x-2x^3}{(x^2-1)^2}=\frac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}.$$
$f'=0$ needs $-2x=0$, so $x=0$.
$f'$ is undefined only at $x=\pm 1$, which are **not in the domain**, so they are not critical points.
$f$ is a rational function and is differentiable at every point of its domain, so there are **no cusps, no corners and no vertical tangents**.

Critical points: $x=0$ only.

**Step 6, sign chart of $f'$.**
$(x^2-1)^2>0$ for every $x$ in the domain, so the sign of $f'$ is the sign of $-2x$.

| interval | $(-\infty,-1)$ | $(-1,0)$ | $(0,1)$ | $(1,\infty)$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| sign of $f'$ | $+$ | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ |
| $f$ is | increasing | increasing | decreasing | decreasing |

$f'$ goes $+$ then $-$ across $x=0$, so $(0,0)$ is a **local maximum**.
Each interval was sampled at 400001 points and the sign was uniform in every one.

**Step 7, $f''$.**
Differentiate $f'=-2x(x^2-1)^{-2}$ by the product rule with a chain factor.
Note the rule has changed from the quotient rule used for $f'$, exactly as Q8.2 warns.
$$f''(x)=-2(x^2-1)^{-2}+(-2x)(-2)(x^2-1)^{-3}(2x)=\frac{-2(x^2-1)+8x^2}{(x^2-1)^3}=\frac{6x^2+2}{(x^2-1)^3}=\frac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}.$$
$f''=0$ needs $3x^2+1=0$, so $x^2=-\tfrac13$: **no real solution**.
The discriminant of $6x^2+2$ is $-48$.
$f''$ is undefined only at $x=\pm 1$, again outside the domain.

**Step 8, sign chart of $f''$.**
The numerator $2(3x^2+1)\geq 2>0$ always, so the sign of $f''$ is the sign of $(x^2-1)^3$, which is the sign of $x^2-1$.

| interval | $(-\infty,-1)$ | $(-1,1)$ | $(1,\infty)$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| sign of $f''$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $f$ is | concave up | concave down | concave up |

The concavity **does** change across $x=-1$ and $x=1$.
Neither is an inflection point, because neither is in the domain, so there is nothing there to be a point of the graph.
There are **no points of inflection**.

$f''(0)=\dfrac{2}{(-1)^3}=-2<0$, which confirms the local maximum a second way.

**Step 9, assemble.**

| branch | goes from | to | shape |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(-\infty,-1)$ | $y\to 1^+$ at $x\to-\infty$ | $y\to+\infty$ at $x\to-1^-$ | increasing, concave up |
| $(-1,1)$ | $y\to-\infty$ at $x\to-1^+$ | $y\to-\infty$ at $x\to1^-$ | up to $(0,0)$ then down, concave down throughout |
| $(1,\infty)$ | $y\to+\infty$ at $x\to1^+$ | $y\to 1^+$ at $x\to\infty$ | decreasing, concave up |

Values to plot, each computed: $f(\pm 2)=\tfrac43\approx 1.3333$, $f(\pm 3)=1.125$, $f(\pm 1.5)=1.8$, $f(\pm 0.5)=-\tfrac13\approx -0.3333$.

Range $(-\infty,0]\cup(1,\infty)$.
Note the local maximum value $0$ sits **below** the horizontal asymptote $y=1$, so the local maximum is emphatically not a global one, and the outer branches never come near it.

### 4b. $f(x)=x+\sin x$

**Step 1, domain.** All of $\mathbb{R}$.

**Step 2, intercepts.**
$f(0)=0$.
For $0<x\leq 1$ both $x>0$ and $\sin x>0$, so $f>0$; for $x>1$, $f\geq x-1>0$.
So there is no positive root, and by oddness no negative one.
$x=0$ is the only intercept.

**Step 3, symmetry.**
$f(-x)=-x+\sin(-x)=-x-\sin x=-f(x)$.
**Odd.** Symmetric about the origin, verified to $10^{-12}$ at 9000 points.

**Step 4, asymptotes.**
$f$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$, so no vertical asymptote.
$f$ is unbounded in both directions, so no horizontal asymptote.

The trap here has a name.
$f(x)-x=\sin x$, which **does not tend to zero**; it oscillates in $[-1,1]$ for ever, hitting $+1$ and $-1$ at every $(2k+\tfrac12)\pi$.
So **$y=x$ is not a slant asymptote.**
It is a *centre line*: the graph meets it at every multiple of $\pi$ and never strays more than $1$ from it.
Say "centre line", not "asymptote", and say why.

**Step 5, $f'$.**
$f'(x)=1+\cos x$.
$f'=0$ needs $\cos x=-1$, so $x=(2k+1)\pi$ for every integer $k$.
$f'$ is finite everywhere ($0\leq f'\leq 2$), so there are **no cusps, no corners and no vertical tangents**.

Critical points $\big((2k+1)\pi,\,(2k+1)\pi\big)$, because $\sin\big((2k+1)\pi\big)=0$ so $f$ takes the value $x$ there.
They lie on the centre line.

**Step 6, sign chart of $f'$.**
$\cos x\geq -1$, so $f'=1+\cos x\geq 0$ **everywhere**, with equality only at the isolated points $(2k+1)\pi$.
There is **no sign change anywhere**, so $f$ is increasing on the whole real line, and strictly so.
Verified at 2000001 samples on $[-4\pi,4\pi]$: the minimum sampled value of $f'$ is exactly $0$ and no sample is negative.

Classification of the critical points.
The second-derivative test is **inconclusive**, because $f''\big((2k+1)\pi\big)=-\sin\big((2k+1)\pi\big)=0$.
Fall back to the first-derivative test: $f'>0$ on both sides, so no sign change, so **neither a maximum nor a minimum**.
They are horizontal tangents at which the curve keeps rising.
$f$ has **no local extrema at all**.

**Step 7, $f''$.**
$f''(x)=-\sin x$.
$f''=0$ needs $\sin x=0$, so $x=k\pi$ for every integer $k$.
$f''$ is defined everywhere, so there are no other candidates.

**Step 8, sign chart of $f''$.**
$f''=-\sin x$ changes sign at every multiple of $\pi$, because $\sin$ does.
Each candidate is therefore a genuine inflection, verified by an actual sign change (not by $f''=0$) at all seven multiples of $\pi$ in $[-3\pi,3\pi]$.

| interval | $(-\pi,0)$ | $(0,\pi)$ | $(\pi,2\pi)$ | $(2\pi,3\pi)$ |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $\sin x$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ |
| $f''=-\sin x$ | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ | $-$ |
| $f$ is | concave up | concave down | concave up | concave down |

Points of inflection $\big(k\pi,\,k\pi\big)$ for every integer $k$.
They lie on the centre line too.

**Step 9, assemble, and the structural fact that makes the sketch easy.**

The odd multiples of $\pi$ are a **subset** of all the multiples of $\pi$.
So every critical point is also an inflection point, and the picture per period is:

```
  x:      0 ........ pi ........ 2pi ........ 3pi
  f'':    -  (down)   0   +  (up)  0   -  (down) 0   +
  f' :    +           0(!)         +           0(!)   +
          |           |            |           |
          |      flat inflection   |      flat inflection
      plain inflection        plain inflection
          (f'=2, steepest)        (f'=2, steepest)
```

The graph is a staircase that never turns back: it rises steeply through each even multiple of $\pi$ (where $f'=2$, its maximum slope), flattens to a horizontal tangent at each odd multiple, and rises again.
Every one of those four marked points sits exactly on $y=x$.

Values to plot, each computed: $f(\pi/2)=\pi/2+1\approx 2.5708$, $f(3\pi/2)=3\pi/2-1\approx 3.7124$, $f(\pi)=\pi\approx 3.1416$, and by oddness $f(-\pi/2)\approx -2.5708$.

**Part (a), the whole answer in eight lines.**

> Domain: $x\neq\pm1$. Numerator and denominator share no factor, so nothing cancels.
> Even: $f(-x)=f(x)$. Intercept $(0,0)$ only.
> Vertical asymptotes: $x=-1$, $x=1$. Horizontal asymptote $y=1$ (equal degrees, leading ratio $1$). No slant.
> $f'(x)=\dfrac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}$. Since $(x^2-1)^2>0$, $\operatorname{sign}f'=\operatorname{sign}(-2x)$: increasing on $(-\infty,-1)$ and $(-1,0)$, decreasing on $(0,1)$ and $(1,\infty)$.
> **Critical points: $(0,0)$, a local maximum** ($f'$ goes $+$ to $-$; also $f''(0)=-2<0$).
> $f''(x)=\dfrac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}$. Numerator $>0$ always, so $\operatorname{sign}f''=\operatorname{sign}(x^2-1)$: concave up on $\lvert x\rvert>1$, concave down on $\lvert x\rvert<1$.
> **Points of inflection: none.** $f''$ is never zero, and the concavity change at $x=\pm1$ is at excluded points, not points of the graph.
> **Cusps: none.** $f$ is rational and differentiable at every point of its domain.

**Part (b), the whole answer in seven lines.**

> Domain: all reals. Odd: $f(-x)=-f(x)$. Intercept $(0,0)$ only.
> **Vertical asymptotes: none** ($f$ is continuous). No horizontal asymptote ($f$ is unbounded). $y=x$ is **not** an asymptote, because $f(x)-x=\sin x$ does not tend to $0$; it is a centre line.
> $f'(x)=1+\cos x\geq 0$, so $f$ is increasing on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
> **Critical points: $\big((2k+1)\pi,(2k+1)\pi\big)$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.** These are horizontal tangents. The second-derivative test is inconclusive there ($f''=0$); by the first-derivative test $f'>0$ on both sides, so **none is a maximum or a minimum**. $f$ has no local extrema.
> $f''(x)=-\sin x$, which changes sign at every multiple of $\pi$.
> **Points of inflection: $\big(k\pi,k\pi\big)$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.** Concave up on $\big((2k-1)\pi,2k\pi\big)$, concave down on $\big(2k\pi,(2k+1)\pi\big)$.
> **Cusps: none.** $f$ is differentiable everywhere ($0\leq f'\leq 2$).

- "The concavity change at $x=\pm1$ is at excluded points, not points of the graph." Without it, "no inflection points" looks like you never computed $f''$.
- "The second-derivative test is inconclusive there." Without it, "not extrema" looks like a guess.
- "$y=x$ is not an asymptote." Without it, a sketch that hugs $y=x$ is indistinguishable from one that claims the wrong thing.

## 6. Verify before you commit

Three cross-checks, each of which catches a specific way the pieces can disagree.
Run all three before drawing, because a sketch made from inconsistent lists takes longer to repair than to redo.

### Check 1: does every claimed inflection actually change sign?

Take each candidate and evaluate $f''$ just to the left and just to the right.
The product must be **negative**.

- Part (b), at $x=k\pi$: $f''(k\pi-0.05)\cdot f''(k\pi+0.05)<0$ at all seven multiples in $[-3\pi,3\pi]$. Passes.
- The same test at $x=\pi/2$, where $f''=-1\neq 0$: product positive. Correctly **rejected**.
- Part (a): there is nothing to test, because $f''$ is never zero. The check that matters instead is that the sign change at $x=\pm1$ is at a point *outside the domain*.

This is the check that catches the most common wrong answer in the whole topic: listing $f''=0$ as an inflection.

### Check 2: is the monotonicity table consistent with the critical points?

Count the sign changes in the $f'$ row and count the extrema you claimed.
They must be equal.

- Part (a): the $f'$ row is $+\,+\,-\,-$, which is **one** change, and there is **one** claimed extremum. Consistent.
- Part (b): the $f'$ row is $+\,+\,+\,\dots$, which is **zero** changes, and **zero** claimed extrema. Consistent.

If you had classified part (b)'s $x=\pi$ as a maximum, this check fails immediately: a maximum requires a $+$ to $-$ change and there is none.

A second, cheaper form of the same check: **the intervals must tile the domain**, breaking only at excluded points and critical points.
On part (a) that is four intervals from two excluded points and one critical point, which is $2+1+1=4$. Correct.

### Check 3: is the drawing consistent with the asymptotes?

Two separate questions, and they have different answers.

- **A vertical asymptote can never be crossed.** $x=1$ is not in the domain, so no point of the graph has $x=1$. A polyline drawn straight through it is simply wrong, and it is the single most common drawing error on rational functions.
- **A horizontal asymptote may or may not be crossable, and you have to check.** Solve $f(x)=L$. For part (a): $\dfrac{x^2}{x^2-1}=1$ gives $x^2=x^2-1$, that is $0=-1$, impossible. **So this curve never crosses $y=1$**, and a sketch showing it dipping below $1$ on $(1,\infty)$ contradicts the algebra. Verified numerically at 60000 points right of $x=1$: $f-1$ is strictly positive throughout, and it gets below $10^{-4}$ without ever reaching zero.
- The contrast that stops this becoming a false rule: $x/(x^2+1)$ **does** cross its horizontal asymptote $y=0$, at the origin. So "curves never cross horizontal asymptotes" is false in general and true for part (a) for a reason you can write in one line.

### Two magnitude checks that cost nothing

- Part (a) near a pole behaves like $\dfrac{1}{2\varepsilon}$: $f(1-10^{-4})\approx -4999.25$, and each further factor of $100$ in $\varepsilon$ multiplies the value by $100$. If your mental picture has the branch levelling off near the pole, it is wrong.
- Part (b) at its steepest has $f'=2$, so no arc of the sketch should ever be steeper than slope $2$, and none should ever have negative slope.

### The self-check that does not work, on either part

This is the sharpest single finding of this question, and it is the same shape as Q13.1's fixed-point blind spot.

**Checking your derivative at the answer cannot detect an error, on either part.**

- **Part (a).** Five plausible slips in $f'$ were tested over a 401-point grid: forgetting the minus sign, omitting the chain factor on the denominator, writing the quotient rule upside down, failing to square the denominator, and cubing it. **Every one of the five agrees with the truth at exactly one point, and that point is $x=0$** - the critical point, the one $x$-value the question forces you to write down. The mechanism: each slip differs from the truth only by a factor multiplying the numerator $2x$, and $2x$ vanishes at $0$.
- **Part (b).** The sign slip $f''=+\sin x$ instead of $-\sin x$ agrees with the truth at **every multiple of $\pi$** - which is exactly the set of inflection points the question asks for.

So on part (a) the trap sits on the critical point, and on part (b) it sits on the inflection set.
**Check your derivatives somewhere that is not a feature.**
$x=2$ is safe for part (a): all five slips are caught there.
$x=\pi/3$ is safe for part (b): it is not a multiple of $\pi$, not a half-odd multiple, and not $\pi/4+k\pi$, so none of the four slips survives.

## 7. Writing the answer down

Both course exams are delivered through Möbius, so the entry format matters as much as the mathematics.
A 20-point sketch cannot be fully machine-marked, so the likeliest shape is the four lists entered as text or selected from options, with the graph either uploaded or hand-marked.
That is inference from the format, flagged as such, and the safe strategy does not depend on which it is.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| write **all four** headings per part, including the empty ones | leave a heading out because the answer is "none" |
| write "none" as a word | leave a blank, a dash, or $\emptyset$ where a marker expects a statement |
| give part (b)'s answers as a formula in $k$ with $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ | list five instances and stop |
| give critical points and inflections as **ordered pairs** | give only the $x$-values when the stem says "points" |
| write asymptotes as **equations**: $x=-1$, $x=1$, $y=1$ | write them as numbers: $-1$, $1$ |
| say $\pi$, not $3.1416$ | round a symbolic answer |
| classify each critical point (max, min, or neither) | list a critical point without saying which |

**The single most expensive omission** is the word "none" against cusps and against part (a)'s inflections.
Three of the eight cells are empty, and an empty cell reads as an unattempted cell.

**The single most expensive typo** is writing part (b)'s critical points as $k\pi$ instead of $(2k+1)\pi$.
That answer is the inflection set, so it is not merely wrong; it collides with the *other* deliverable on the same line, and it is exactly what the $f''=+\sin x$ sign slip would also produce.

Two entry details specific to this question.

- $(2k+1)\pi$ and $k\pi$ must be distinguishable at a glance in your own writing, because they are the two answers most easily swapped.
- If a box asks for critical points and you have infinitely many, the answer is the formula, and it needs the quantifier: "for all integers $k$".

## 8. The variation catalog

Read the tables below as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
Eleven families cover every reworded version of this question the papers could carry, and they are organised by **the tool the variation forces**, not by what the formula looks like.
The whole catalog is generated by three independent switches.

1. **Is the domain punctured?** Rational with surviving poles, rational with a cancelling factor, or entire.
2. **What does the end behaviour do?** Horizontal, slant, none, or oscillating.
3. **Is $f$ differentiable everywhere on its domain?** Yes, corner, cusp, or vertical tangent.

Every family below is one setting of those three switches, and each entry's numbers were computed and asserted, not carried over.
Scope note, measured: **no exponential and no logarithm appears anywhere on any of the four papers**, so this catalog is deliberately algebraic and trigonometric.

### Family 0 - rational, equal degrees, nothing cancels

*Trigger:* a quotient of two polynomials of the same degree with no common factor.
*Forces:* the whole checklist, with a horizontal asymptote from the leading ratio and poles that survive.
*Worked shape:* the exam's own part (a).

| $f$ | poles | HA | critical | inflections |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^2/(x^2-1)$ | $x=\pm1$ | $y=1$ | $(0,0)$ max | none |
| $x^2/(x^2-4)$ | $x=\pm2$ | $y=1$ | $(0,0)$ max | none |
| $x^2/(x^2-9)$ | $x=\pm3$ | $y=1$ | $(0,0)$ max | none |

Only **one** structure appears in this whole family; the pole locations move and nothing else does.
The general form $x^2/(x^2-a^2)$ has $f'=-2a^2x/(x^2-a^2)^2$ and $f''=2a^2(3x^2+a^2)/(x^2-a^2)^3$, whose numerator is positive for every real $x$, so **no member of this family has an inflection point**.
That single sentence discharges the family.

### Family 1 - a common factor, so a hole and a pole

*Trigger:* numerator and denominator share a root.
*Forces:* the pole-versus-hole discrimination from Q4.1, before anything else.
*Worked shape:* cancel first, then run the checklist on the simplified function, and put the hole back at the end as an open circle.

| $f$ | simplifies to | hole | pole | HA | critical | inflections |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(x^2-x)/(x^2-1)$ | $x/(x+1)$ | $x=1$, $y=\tfrac12$ | $x=-1$ | $y=1$ | none | none |
| $(x^2-4)/(x^2-x-2)$ | $(x+2)/(x+1)$ | $x=2$, $y=\tfrac43$ | $x=-1$ | $y=1$ | none | none |
| $(x^2-1)/(x^2-3x+2)$ | $(x+1)/(x-2)$ | $x=1$, $y=-2$ | $x=2$ | $y=1$ | none | none |

Note what these have in common: after cancelling, each is a **Möbius function** $(ax+b)/(cx+d)$, whose derivative never vanishes and whose second derivative never vanishes.
So the entire family has **no critical points and no inflections**, and the whole question is the hole.
The hole's $y$-value is the limit, and it must be computed from the *cancelled* form.

### Family 2 - one degree higher on top, so a slant asymptote

*Trigger:* $\deg N=\deg D+1$ exactly.
*Forces:* polynomial division, and the recognition that horizontal and slant are mutually exclusive.

| $f$ | division | slant | pole | critical | inflections |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^2/(x-1)$ | $x+1+\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ | $y=x+1$ | $x=1$ | $(0,0)$ max, $(2,4)$ min | none |
| $x^2/(x+1)$ | $x-1+\dfrac{1}{x+1}$ | $y=x-1$ | $x=-1$ | $(-2,-4)$ max, $(0,0)$ min | none |

The counter-intuitive feature, and it is asked: the local **minimum** value ($4$) is *greater* than the local **maximum** value ($0$).
That is legal because the two lie on different branches, separated by the pole.
A student who checks "max above min" as a sanity rule will think they have made an error.

### Family 3 - lower degree on top, so a horizontal asymptote the curve crosses

*Trigger:* $\deg N<\deg D$, and typically no real poles at all.
*Forces:* the fact that a horizontal asymptote is not a barrier, plus three inflections instead of none.

| $f$ | HA | crosses HA at | critical | inflections |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x/(x^2+1)$ | $y=0$ | $x=0$ | $(1,\tfrac12)$ max, $(-1,-\tfrac12)$ min | $0$, $\pm\sqrt3$; $f(\sqrt3)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\approx0.4330$ |
| $x/(x^2+4)$ | $y=0$ | $x=0$ | $(2,\tfrac14)$ max, $(-2,-\tfrac14)$ min | $0$, $\pm2\sqrt3$; $f(2\sqrt3)=\tfrac{\sqrt3}{8}\approx0.2165$ |

This is the family that refutes "a curve never crosses its horizontal asymptote".
The general form $x/(x^2+a^2)$ has extrema at $x=\pm a$ with values $\pm\tfrac{1}{2a}$, and inflections at $0$ and $\pm a\sqrt3$.
Three magnitudes, and the signs are generated by oddness.

### Family 4 - a line plus a bounded oscillation, and the amplitude bifurcation

*Trigger:* $f(x)=x+A\sin x$ or $x+A\cos x$.
*Forces:* infinitely many features stated as a formula in $k$, and the touch-versus-crossing distinction.

This is the family with real structure, because **the amplitude decides the answer qualitatively**, and the exam sits exactly on the boundary.

| $\lvert A\rvert$ | $\min f'$ | critical points | extrema? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $<1$ (e.g. $0.5$, $0.9$, $0.99$) | $1-\lvert A\rvert>0$ | **none at all** | none |
| $=1$ (**the exam**) | exactly $0$ | infinitely many, touching zero | **none** |
| $>1$ (e.g. $1.01$, $1.5$, $2$) | $1-\lvert A\rvert<0$ | infinitely many, crossing zero | genuine max/min pairs |

The mechanism is one line: $f'=1+A\cos x=0$ needs $\cos x=-1/A$, which is solvable exactly when $\lvert A\rvert\geq1$, and gives a *repeated* root exactly when $\lvert A\rvert=1$.
So part (b) is the borderline case, and that is why its critical points are not extrema.
Measured across seven amplitudes: $\lvert A\rvert<1$ gives zero sign changes on $(-3\pi,3\pi)$, $\lvert A\rvert>1$ gives six.

| $f$ | critical points | extrema? | inflections |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x+\sin x$ | $(2k+1)\pi$ | no | $k\pi$ |
| $x-\sin x$ | $2k\pi$ | no | $k\pi$ |
| $x+\cos x$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{2}+2k\pi$ | no | $\tfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi$ |
| $x+2\cos x$ | $\tfrac{\pi}{6}+2k\pi$ max, $\tfrac{5\pi}{6}+2k\pi$ min | **yes** | $\tfrac{\pi}{2}+k\pi$ |
| $x+2\sin x$ | $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}+2k\pi$ max, $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}+2k\pi$ min | **yes** | $k\pi$ |

$x+\cos x$ is worth singling out: it is **Q15.1 part (b)** of this same paper's item 5.
Two consecutive items on Final Exam 1 use the same shape of function, which makes this family the highest-value drill in the topic.
Note also that $x-\sin x$ has its critical points at the *even* multiples of $\pi$ and its concavity **reversed** from $x+\sin x$: concave up on $(0,\pi)$ where $x+\sin x$ is concave down.
Same inflection set, opposite shape.

For $x+2\cos x$: $f'=1-2\sin x=0$ at $\sin x=\tfrac12$, so $x=\tfrac{\pi}{6}$ and $\tfrac{5\pi}{6}$, with $f(\tfrac{\pi}{6})=\tfrac{\pi}{6}+\sqrt3\approx2.2556$ and $f(\tfrac{5\pi}{6})=\tfrac{5\pi}{6}-\sqrt3\approx0.8859$.

### Family 5 - a genuine cusp

*Trigger:* a fractional power with an **even** numerator and odd denominator, like $x^{2/3}$.
*Forces:* one-sided slopes, and the recognition that a cusp is a critical point and belongs in step 5.

| $f$ | cusp at | one-sided slopes | other critical | value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^{2/3}$ | $x=0$ | $+\infty$ right, $-\infty$ left | none | $f(0)=0$, a min |
| $x^{2/3}(x-5)$ | $x=0$ | $-\infty$ right, $+\infty$ left | $x=2$, a min | $f(2)=-3\cdot2^{2/3}\approx-4.7622$ |

The test is mechanical: compute $\lim_{h\to0^+}\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}$ and the left twin.
Both infinite with **opposite** signs is a cusp.
For $x^{2/3}(x-5)$ the measured slopes multiply by exactly $10$ for each factor of $1000$ in $h$, which is the $h^{-1/3}$ signature.
Note $x=0$ is a local **maximum** for $x^{2/3}(x-5)$ even though it is a downward cusp point, because $f'>0$ to the left and $f'<0$ to the right.
This is Q15.5's function, so the family is examined elsewhere too.

### Family 6 - a corner, which is not a cusp

*Trigger:* an absolute value around something that changes sign.
*Forces:* finite unequal one-sided slopes, and the centred-difference trap.

| $f$ | corners at | one-sided slopes | other features |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lvert x^2-1\rvert$ | $x=\pm1$ | $\pm2$ | max at $(0,1)$, minima $0$ at the corners |
| $\lvert x^2-4\rvert$ | $x=\pm2$ | $\pm4$ | max at $(0,4)$, minima $0$ at the corners |

The trap, which the Q5.x family records and which this run sharpened: **a centred difference quotient at a corner returns the average of the two one-sided slopes.**

- At $x=2$ on $\lvert x^2-4\rvert$ the slopes are $+4$ and $-4$, so the centred quotient reads $0$, and a numerical check certifies a non-differentiable point as a smooth stationary one.
- The general statement is the average, not zero. On the asymmetric corner of $f(x)=2x$ for $x\geq0$, $x$ for $x<0$, the one-sided slopes are $2$ and $1$ and the centred quotient reads exactly $1.5$ - a perfectly plausible number that is **neither** slope. That is arguably worse than reading $0$, because $0$ at least looks suspicious.

**Test one-sided slopes separately.** Always.

### Family 7 - a vertical tangent, which is also not a cusp

*Trigger:* a fractional power with an **odd** numerator and odd denominator, like $x^{1/3}$.

$f(x)=x^{1/3}$ has $f'=\tfrac13x^{-2/3}\to+\infty$ from **both** sides, so it is a vertical tangent and not a cusp.
And $f''=-\tfrac29x^{-5/3}$ flips sign across $0$, so **$x=0$ is an inflection point** even though $f''$ is undefined there.
One function, three teaching points: a critical point of the $f'$-undefined kind, not a cusp, and an inflection where $f''$ does not exist.

### Family 8 - $f''=0$ without an inflection, and the converse

*Trigger:* an even-multiplicity root of $f''$.

| $f$ | $f''$ | at $x=0$ | inflection? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^4$ | $12x^2$ | zero, no sign change | **no** |
| $x^3$ | $6x$ | zero, sign change | yes |
| $x^{1/3}$ | $-\tfrac29x^{-5/3}$ | undefined, sign change | **yes** |
| $x^2/(x^2-1)$ | $\tfrac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}$ | never zero | **no**, at $\pm1$ for a different reason |

Four rows, four different reasons, and the exam's part (a) is the fourth: the sign change happens at a point **not in the domain**.
This is the family that generates the most common wrong answer in the topic.

### Family 9 - the odd rational twin of part (a)

*Trigger:* the same denominator with an odd numerator.

$f(x)=x/(x^2-1)$ has the same poles $x=\pm1$ as part (a) and is otherwise its opposite in every respect.

| | $x^2/(x^2-1)$ | $x/(x^2-1)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| symmetry | even | **odd** |
| HA | $y=1$ | $y=0$ |
| crosses HA? | never | **yes, at $x=0$** |
| $f'$ | $\dfrac{-2x}{(x^2-1)^2}$ | $\dfrac{-(x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^2}$ |
| critical points | one, at $x=0$ | **none** ($-(x^2+1)<0$ always) |
| $f''$ | $\dfrac{2(3x^2+1)}{(x^2-1)^3}$ | $\dfrac{2x(x^2+3)}{(x^2-1)^3}$ |
| inflections | **none** | **one, at $x=0$** |

Change the numerator's exponent by one and every answer flips.
This is the sharpest available drill on "the answers come from the algebra, not from the shape of the formula".
Also useful: $x/(x^2-1)$ is decreasing on all three of its branches with no turning point anywhere, which surprises people who expect a pole pair to force a turn.

### Family 10 - a polynomial with part (b)'s structure

*Trigger:* a polynomial whose $f'$ has a repeated root.

$f(x)=x^4-4x^3$ has $f'=4x^2(x-3)$ and $f''=12x(x-2)$.

- Critical points $x=0$ and $x=3$. At $x=0$ the root of $f'$ is **double**, so $f'$ does not change sign ($f'<0$ on both sides) and $x=0$ is a critical point that is **not** an extremum. $x=3$ is a genuine local minimum, $f(3)=-27$.
- Inflections at $x=0$ and $x=2$, both with genuine sign changes; $f(2)=-16$.
- So $x=0$ is **both a critical point and an inflection point**, and the second-derivative test there is inconclusive because $f''(0)=0$.

That is exactly part (b)'s structure in a polynomial you can factor by hand, which makes it the best bridge between the two halves of the exam question.
If part (b)'s "critical point that is also an inflection and is not an extremum" feels exotic, this is the same thing in four lines of algebra.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks on this question.

1. **Leaving a "none" blank.** Four of the eight demanded cells are empty: cusps on both parts, vertical asymptotes on (b), and inflections on (a). A blank reads as unattempted, and this is the cheapest and largest loss available on a 20-point question.
2. **Calling $f''=0$ an inflection without testing the sign change.** Sufficient for nothing. Part (b) has seven candidates in $[-3\pi,3\pi]$ and all seven happen to pass, which is precisely why the habit never gets punished until it meets $x^4$.
3. **Calling a concavity change at an excluded point an inflection.** Part (a)'s concavity flips across $x=\pm1$ and there is no inflection there, because there is no point there. This is the single most likely wrong answer on part (a).
4. **Calling $f'=0$ an extremum.** Part (b) is built to punish it. Every one of its infinitely many critical points has a horizontal tangent and none is an extremum, and the second-derivative test is silent because $f''=0$ there too.
5. **Writing $y=x$ as an asymptote for part (b).** $f(x)-x=\sin x$ does not tend to zero. Call it a centre line.
6. **Swapping $(2k+1)\pi$ and $k\pi$.** The two deliverables on part (b) are nested sets, and the wrong one is the other correct answer.
7. **Checking your derivative at the answer.** All five plausible $f'$ slips on part (a) agree with the truth at exactly $x=0$, and part (b)'s $f''$ sign slip agrees at every $k\pi$. The one point you are certain to re-check is the one that cannot fail. Check at $x=2$ and at $x=\pi/3$ instead.
8. **Drawing the curve through a vertical asymptote.** $x=1$ is not in the domain and no point of the graph has that abscissa.
9. **Drawing part (a) dipping below $y=1$ on $(1,\infty)$.** It cannot: $f(x)=1$ has no solution. The drawing would contradict the algebra you have already written.
10. **Finding cusps at step 9.** A cusp is a critical point and it breaks the $f'$ sign chart, so it must be found in step 5. The course notes' checklist has this in the wrong place.
11. **Confusing a corner with a cusp**, or a vertical tangent with a cusp. Three distinct things, distinguished only by the two one-sided slopes.
12. **Trusting a centred difference quotient at a non-differentiable point.** It returns the average of the one-sided slopes, which is $0$ at a symmetric corner and a plausible wrong number otherwise.
13. **Listing five instances of a periodic family instead of a formula in $k$.**
14. **Sampling for part (b)'s critical points.** They cannot be found by sampling at any resolution: $f'$ touches zero without crossing, so a grid at step $1.0$, $0.5$, $0.25$ or $0.1$ reports "$f'>0$ everywhere, no critical points". Solve $\cos x=-1$ instead.
15. **Assuming the local maximum is the highest point.** Part (a)'s local max value is $0$, and the outer branches are all above $1$.
16. **Assuming a local max value exceeds a local min value.** False across a pole, as family 2 shows: $0$ and $4$.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
START: you are given f(x). Twenty points. Four lists per part.
|
+-- 1. DOMAIN. Any denominator? any even root? any log?
|      RATIONAL -> factor top AND bottom. CANCEL common factors NOW.
|                  surviving denominator zeros  -> VERTICAL ASYMPTOTES
|                  cancelled zeros              -> HOLES (open circles, not VAs)
|      ELSE     -> domain is R. Write "no vertical asymptotes".
|
+-- 2. INTERCEPTS. f(0)=? and solve f(x)=0.
|
+-- 3. SYMMETRY. f(-x) = f(x)? EVEN, mirror the y-axis.
|                f(-x) = -f(x)? ODD, rotate about the origin.
|                Halves everything below. Do it before differentiating.
|
+-- 4. END BEHAVIOUR.
|      deg N < deg D  -> HA y = 0        (and CHECK whether it is crossed)
|      deg N = deg D  -> HA y = ratio    (and CHECK whether it is crossed)
|      deg N = deg D+1 -> divide; SLANT y = quotient
```
