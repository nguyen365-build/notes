Volume of a cone as a function of its radius

Consider a cone whose height is double the size of its radius.

  • a.Express the volume $V$ of the cone as a function of radius $r$.
  • b.Find the domain of the function in a.
  • c.Use the function in a. to compute the volume of the cone for $r = 3$.

00The 30-second version

If you only remember one thing, remember this chain.

"height is double the radius"
  -> write the SENTENCE as an equation             h = 2r
  -> put it into the hint formula                  V = pi*r^2*(2r)/3
  -> collect                                       V = 2*pi*r^3/3
  -> domain is PHYSICAL, not algebraic             r > 0,  so (0, infinity)
  -> evaluate                                      V(3) = 2*pi*27/3 = 18*pi

Five lines, about thirty seconds, no calculator. The whole question is one substitution.

What makes people lose the marks is line 1, not line 3.

01What this question is really testing

It looks like arithmetic. It is a reading test with an arithmetic tail.

Four reflexes, and where each one comes back
ReflexWhere it comes back
Turning an English constraint into an equationrelated rates, optimization, every applied word problem
Eliminating a variable so the formula has exactly one inputoptimization (you cannot differentiate a two-variable expression), related rates
Separating the algebraic domain from the contextual domainQ1.2 composite domains, Q4.1 asymptotes, all curve sketching
Keeping the answer exact ($18\pi$, not $56.55$)every "compute" instruction on a Maple-graded paper
The reason to study this one properly

Midterm 1 question 7 is the same cone. It says "a pile in shape of a cone whose base diameter and height are always equal", which is $d = h$, and since $d = 2r$ that is $h = 2r$ - character for character the constraint in this question. MT1 Q7 then asks for $dh/dt$ instead of $V$.

So the setup you are learning here is reused later with calculus bolted onto it. That is why Family 8 exists.

Where the 4 points sit. Part a is the substitution and carries most of the credit. Part b is one interval and is the part most often thrown away. Part c is arithmetic you cannot afford to fumble.

02The three things you must have cold

The one relationship worth internalising

A cone is exactly one third of the cylinder that wraps it. That is where the $/3$ comes from, and Section 06 turns it into a free check.

This is the actual content of the question. Read the sentence, put the subject on the left of the equals sign, and translate.

The direction test · two seconds

Put in a number. If "the height is double the radius" and $r = 1$, is $h = 2$ or $h = 0.5$? A cone twice as tall as its radius is tall and narrow, so $h = 2$.

Written the wrong way round you would get $h = r/2$ and the answer $\pi r^3/6$, which is four times too small.

Diameter is the second-most-expensive word in these questions. Any sentence containing "diameter" gets a $d = 2r$ substitution before you translate anything else.

2.3  The two domains, which are different things

What it isHow you find it
Algebraic domainevery input the formula can acceptdenominators nonzero, even roots non-negative, logs positive
Contextual domainevery input the situation can acceptevery physical length strictly positive, plus any bound the geometry forces

The answer to a modelling question is the intersection of the two.

For this question the algebraic domain of $\frac{2\pi r^3}{3}$ is all of $\mathbb{R}$, because a polynomial accepts anything. The algebra contributes nothing at all, and the context does one hundred percent of the work. That is exactly why part b is worth asking, and exactly why people answer $\mathbb{R}$ and lose it.

03The universal procedure

Every "express $Q$ as a function of $x$" question on this course is this procedure.

  1. Write the formula that contains the quantity you are asked for. If the paper gives a hint formula, use its letters exactly, so your work lines up with the marker's.
  2. Count the variables. The target is one. The formula here has two, $r$ and $h$.
  3. Write every constraint sentence as an equation. One constraint kills one variable.
  4. Solve each constraint for the variable you want to remove, not for the one you are keeping.
  5. Substitute and simplify to one clean expression in the requested variable.
  6. Domain: start from the algebraic domain, then intersect every physical requirement. Each length must be strictly greater than zero, and that includes lengths that are not the variable you kept.
  7. Evaluate if asked, and keep it exact.
  8. Check with Section 06 before you commit.

Classify before you start

Step 2 versus step 3 tells you which kind of problem you are holding.

04Q1.4 worked three ways

Route A  ·  substitute the constraint straight into the hint

The constraint sentence is "height is double the radius", so $h = 2r$. Put that into the given formula, changing nothing else.

$$V = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3} = \frac{\pi r^2 (2r)}{3} = \frac{2\pi r^3}{3}$$

Domain: $r$ is a length, so $r > 0$; and $h = 2r$ is then automatically positive, so it adds no new restriction. The domain is $(0,\infty)$.

Evaluate at $r = 3$, where $h = 6$:

$$V(3) = \frac{2\pi (3)^3}{3} = \frac{2\pi \cdot 27}{3} = 18\pi \approx 56.549$$

Three lines of algebra and no opportunity to go wrong after line one.

Route B  ·  go through the height first, then convert back

Solve the constraint the other way, $r = \dfrac{h}{2}$, and eliminate $r$ instead:

$$V = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \frac{\pi h^3}{12}$$

That is a function of $h$, not of $r$, so it does not answer part a as written. Put $h = 2r$ back in to finish:

$$V = \frac{\pi (2r)^3}{12} = \frac{8\pi r^3}{12} = \frac{2\pi r^3}{3} \quad\checkmark$$

Longer, and it squares a fraction, which is one extra place to drop a factor. Learn it anyway, because $V = \dfrac{\pi h^3}{12}$ is the exact form Midterm 1 question 7 needs, and it is the form you want whenever the height is the thing changing.

Route C  ·  use the fact that the shape is fixed

"Height is double the radius" pins the shape completely; only the size varies. Every length therefore scales together, so the volume must scale as the cube of any one length: $V = k\,r^3$.

Find $k$ from a single easy instance. At $r = 1$ the height is $2$, so $V = \pi(1)^2(2)/3 = 2\pi/3$, giving $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ and $V(r) = \dfrac{2\pi r^3}{3}$.

Fastest if you already trust the scaling argument. A poor route to write down where method is marked, because "the shape is fixed" needs a sentence of justification that the substitution does not.

Use Route A. Fewest steps, uses the letters the paper gave you, and its only failure mode is the one you have already checked with the direction test.

Route C is worth thirty seconds afterwards as independent confirmation, because it reaches the same answer without repeating Route A's algebra - and a check that repeats the work it is checking is not a check.

05The exam-ready write-up

If the paper wants method, these are the sentences that earn it. If it is Möbius, you still want them in your head, because they are the order of operations.

a. The height is double the radius, so $h = 2r$. Substituting into $V = \dfrac{\pi r^2 h}{3}$ gives $V = \dfrac{\pi r^2(2r)}{3} = \dfrac{2\pi r^3}{3}$.

b. $r$ is the radius of a physical cone, so $r > 0$; the expression itself is a polynomial and places no further restriction, and $h = 2r > 0$ follows automatically. The domain is $(0, \infty)$.

c. $V(3) = \dfrac{2\pi (3)^3}{3} = 18\pi$ cubic units.

Three things that pick up marks in part b specifically. Say why $r>0$ (it is a length), say that the polynomial adds nothing, and say that $h$ needed checking too even though it turned out free. That last clause is the one that shows you would have caught the $h = r - 2$ version.

06Verify before you commit

Eight seconds, and it catches every error this question can produce.

The cylinder check · the good one

A cone is one third of the cylinder around it. At $r = 3$ the height is $6$, so the cylinder has volume $\pi r^2 h = \pi \cdot 9 \cdot 6 = 54\pi$. A third of $54\pi$ is $18\pi$. $\checkmark$

This reaches the answer without touching your formula at all, which is what makes it a real check.

The degree check

$V$ must come out as degree 3 in $r$, always, whenever the constraint is $h = cr$. Two powers come from $r^2$ and one from the $h$. If you have written $r^2$, you substituted a constant for $h$ instead of something proportional to $r$; if you have $r^4$, you squared something you should not have.

The coefficient check

$h = 2r$ doubles the height, so the answer must be exactly twice $\dfrac{\pi r^3}{3}$, the $h = r$ cone. $\dfrac{2\pi r^3}{3}$ is. $\dfrac{\pi r^3}{6}$ is not - that is the constraint read backwards.

The scaling check

Volume goes as $r^3$, so doubling the radius multiplies the volume by $8$ and tripling it by $27$. $V(3)/V(1) = 18\pi \div \frac{2\pi}{3} = 27$. $\checkmark$

If your part c is not $18\pi$, you have not found a new cone. You have made an arithmetic error.

07Answer-entry rules

Möbius grades by mathematical equivalence, so a correct answer is never rejected for its form. It is rejected for being parsed differently from how you meant it - silently, with no error message.

Powersr^3r**3, r3
Fractions2/3*Pi*r^3 or 2*Pi*r^3/32*Pi*r^(3/3)

The recommended form is 2/3*Pi*r^3. Putting the fraction at the front means there is no /3 left dangling at the end for the parser to attach to the wrong thing, and no exponent adjacent to a slash.

The single most expensive typo

2*Pi*r^(3/3) is $2\pi r$, and is not close to anything. The runner-up is answering part b with R or (-infinity,infinity) because the polynomial accepts it.

A flag · the one trap preview does not catch

Möbius sits on top of Maple, and Maple treats Pi as the constant $3.14159\ldots$ while plain pi is just an unassigned Greek letter - a variable. Standard Möbius questions accept pi; Maple-graded ones do not, and nothing in the interface tells you which engine you are in.

Worse, the preview renders both as the same $\pi$ glyph, so previewing does not distinguish them. Type Pi, which is the constant under Maple and is accepted by Möbius, and confirm the behaviour on a practice question rather than during the exam.

Always click the preview icon anyway. An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics. It catches every bracket error in the table above, which is most of them.

This is the part that makes the question repeatable. Read each table as structure, not as a list of facts.

How to read Family 0

There are only four distinct coefficients in play - $\tfrac{\pi}{6}, \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \pi$ and their multiples - and every one of them is just $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ multiplied by whatever number the sentence attaches to $r$.

You are not memorising ten answers. You are memorising one substitution and reading ten sentences correctly.

The square root looks like it is protecting you, and it is not. $\sqrt{100 - r^2}$ is perfectly happy at $r = -0.5$, because $r$ is squared before the root sees it.

The algebra supplies only the upper edge $r < 10$. The lower edge $r > 0$ is still pure context.

09The trap list

Ranked by how often they actually cost marks.

  1. Reading the constraint backwards. $r = 2h$ instead of $h = 2r$ gives $\frac{\pi r^3}{6}$, four times too small, and it destroys all three parts at once. Use the direction test in 2.2.
  2. Answering part b with $\mathbb{R}$. The polynomial genuinely accepts every real number. The cone does not. This is the most commonly dropped single mark on the question.
  3. Including the endpoint, writing $[0,\infty)$. A cone of radius zero is a point, not a cone with volume zero. The interval is open.
  4. Leaving $h$ in the answer. "As a function of $r$" means no $h$ may appear. $\frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}$ with a note that $h=2r$ is not an answer to part a.
  5. Missing the second domain edge in the Family 1 and Family 2 variations. Every length must be positive, not just the one you kept.
  6. Diameter read as radius. Any sentence containing "diameter" needs $d=2r$ applied before anything else.
  7. Giving a decimal for part c. $18\pi$ is the answer; $56.55$ is a rounding of it.
  8. Typing pi where the engine wants Pi, which the preview cannot show you.
  9. Using the cylinder formula - that is, forgetting the $/3$. The cylinder check in 06 catches this instantly.
  10. Squaring only $r$ and not the whole of $h/2$ on Route B, giving $\frac{\pi h^3}{6}$ instead of $\frac{\pi h^3}{12}$.

10Drill set

No answers beside the questions. Do all twelve on paper in under fifteen minutes, then check.

  1. A cone's height is three times its radius. Express $V$ as a function of $r$.
  2. A cone's base diameter equals its height. Express $V$ as a function of $h$.
  3. A cone's height is 2 less than its radius. Express $V$ as a function of $r$, and give the domain.
  4. A cone has slant height 6. Express $V$ as a function of $r$, and give the domain.
  5. A cylinder has volume 500. Express its total surface area as a function of $r$.
  6. Express the volume of a sphere as a function of its surface area $A$.
  7. Two positive numbers sum to 30. Express their product as a function of one of them.
  8. State the domain of the cone model $V(r)=\dfrac{\pi r^2 (12-r)}{3}$.
  9. For the Q1.4 cone, find $r$ when $V = 486\pi$.

How to mark yourself

Score translation separately from algebra. If the substitutions are clean but the constraints keep coming out reversed or off by a factor of two, the problem is 2.2, and drilling more algebra will not fix it.

If the constraints are right but the domains keep coming out as $\mathbb{R}$, the problem is 2.3 - and that is worth more marks per minute than anything else on this list.
