Six Conditions, One Sketch

Three of them pin single points. Three of them govern whole intervals, and those three are the ones that decide the picture: a branch that falls all the way onto a horizontal asymptote can only sit above it.

Two transcription conflicts, found before anything was drawn

Condition (c) is one-sided on the paper and two-sided in the question bank. Both copies of the exam write \(\lim_{x\to 3^-}\), with a minus superscript. notes\MATH265.md and MATH265_Review.md both drop it. The difference is real: route F below satisfies the paper exactly as printed, has a finite right-hand limit at 3, and fails the two-sided version.

Condition (d)'s closed bracket is unsatisfiable. \([3,\infty)\) includes a point where \(f\) is undefined by (c), so \(f'(3)\) cannot exist for any candidate at all. Read it as \((3,\infty)\), say so in one clause, and move on.

The one sentence that decides this question. Conditions (b) and (d) are not two facts about the right-hand tail, they are one: a branch that falls all the way onto a horizontal asymptote must be above it, so the side of approach is forced rather than chosen. Q5.2's tail had no \(f'\) condition and could arrive from either side. Every interval condition here works like that, by removing a freedom a limit condition alone would have left you.

1An interval condition is about shape, never about height

This is the single idea behind (d), (e) and (f), and it is where most of the lost marks are.

The confusion that actually happens

\(f'<0\) gets read as \(f<0\), and the branch is drawn below the \(x\)-axis. The shipped answer's right branch is above the axis from \(x=3\) to \(x=6\) and below it after that, and both halves satisfy (d) equally, because (d) never mentions height.

The four shapes, and which two can settle onto a level

f′ < 0   f″ > 0
Falling, flattening. Settles onto \(y=L\) from above. This is Q5.3's right branch.
f′ > 0   f″ < 0
Rising, flattening. Settles onto \(y=L\) from below. This is what Q5.2's tail was free to do.
f′ < 0   f″ < 0

f′ > 0   f″ > 0
Rising and steepening. Cannot settle: it runs away to \(+\infty\).

Why "falling onto \(L\)" forces "above \(L\)"

Suppose \(f\) is decreasing on \((3,\infty)\) and \(f(x_0)\le L\) for some \(x_0>3\). Then \(f(x)<f(x_0)\le L\) for every \(x>x_0\), so the limit is at most \(f(x_1)\) for any \(x_1>x_0\), which is strictly below \(L\). That contradicts \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L\). So \(f(x)>L\) everywhere on \((3,\infty)\). Checked on all six legal routes below, at \(x\) up to \(10^{6}\).

Why the bottom two cannot settle

If \(f'<0\) and \(f''\le0\) on the whole of \([c,\infty)\) then \(f'\) is decreasing, so \(f'(x)\le f'(c)<0\), and therefore \(f(x)\le f(c)+f'(c)(x-c)\), which runs to \(-\infty\). The consequence for Q5.3: given (b) and (d), it is impossible for \(f''\) to be negative on the whole of \([4,\infty)\). Condition (f) states the strongest version of something the other conditions had already half-forced.

The qualifier that keeps this honest

"Concave down" alone is not banned near a horizontal asymptote. \(f(x)=-2-\tfrac1x\) is increasing, concave down, and settles onto \(y=-2\) from below: that is the second glyph. What is banned is the pair \(f'<0\) and \(f''<0\) on an entire tail. Q5.2's tutorial compresses this to "a tail must be concave the opposite way from its direction of travel", which is right in spirit and slightly too strong as written: a tail may be concave the same way on part of itself, just never on all of it.

Something else in the list does most of the right side's work anyway. \(f\) falls on \((3,\infty)\) and tends to \(-2\), so as \(x\) comes down towards 3 the function rises: \(\lim_{x\to3^+}f\) is either \(+\infty\) or some finite number above \(-2\). It cannot be \(-\infty\) and it cannot be at or below \(-2\).

What to draw

Draw both branches going up. That satisfies the one-sided reading, the two-sided reading, and any marker's expectation, and it costs nothing.

Why \([3,\infty)\) has to be read open

\(f'(3)\) requires \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(3+h)-f(3)}{h}\) to exist as a single finite number. Condition (c) makes \(f\) unbounded just left of 3, so whatever value you assign to \(f(3)\), the numerator runs to \(+\infty\) while \(h\) is negative and the quotient runs to \(-\infty\). No finite slope exists, so \(f'(3)<0\) is false for every function satisfying (c).

How route C's coefficients are pinned

Write \(f(x)=-2+\dfrac{ax+b}{(x-3)^{2}}\). The \(-2\) comes from (b), and the numerator's degree is at most 1 or the limit would not be \(-2\). Then \(f(0)=-2+\tfrac b9=-3\) gives \(b=-9\). The squared denominator makes the pole \(+\infty\) on both sides provided \(3a-9>0\), so \(a>3\); working out \(f'\) shows (d) needs \(a>3\) again and (e) needs \(a<18\). Any \(a\) strictly between 3 and 18 works, and 6 was chosen.

And then the function, with the justification a ten-point item expects

Take \(f(x)=-3+\dfrac{x^{2}}{3-x}\) for \(x<3\), and \(f(x)=-2+\dfrac{6}{x-3}\) for \(x>3\).

\(f(0)=-3\), which is (a). As \(x\to\infty\), \(f(x)+2=\dfrac{6}{x-3}\to0^{+}\), so \(f\to-2\) from above, which is (b). As \(x\to3^-\) the numerator tends to 9 and \(3-x\to0^{+}\), so \(f\to+\infty\); the same happens on the other branch as \(x\to3^{+}\), so \(x=3\) is a vertical asymptote with both branches rising, which is (c) and more.

On \((3,\infty)\), \(f'(x)=-\dfrac{6}{(x-3)^{2}}<0\), which is (d). On \(x<3\), \(f'(x)=\dfrac{x(6-x)}{(3-x)^{2}}\), negative for every \(x<0\) and so in particular on \((-\infty,-2)\), which is (e). On \((3,\infty)\), \(f''(x)=\dfrac{12}{(x-3)^{3}}>0\), which gives (f) on \([4,\infty)\) and in fact on the whole branch.

Four things students routinely skip: answering the first sentence at all; naming the feature rather than describing the drawing; saying which side the tail is approached from; and saying that the bracket in (d) has to be read open.

Three traps in the checking itself

A centred difference cannot see a corner

\(\frac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}\) at route B's corner returns about \(1.7\times10^{-7}\): a stable, plausible zero, which is just the average of \(-1\) and \(+1\). The residue is truncation error and scales with the second derivative, not with roundoff, which is why a tolerance calibrated on a straight-armed corner is the wrong tolerance for a curved one. Test one side at a time.

A sampling grid coarser than the defect reports the defect as clean

While building this page a deliberately broken function was constructed with a climb \(0.6\) units wide inside \((3,4)\), and the checking grid, stepping \(0.90\) units at a time, walked straight over it and called the function decreasing. On paper the same mistake is checking three points and concluding a shape. Drag your finger along the whole branch.

A sign change is not a crossing

Looking for the graph to pass from below \(y=-2\) to above it catches a genuine crossing and also catches a jump that steps over the line without ever taking the value. If you want to claim the graph crosses, point at where it is actually on the line.

The four most expensive answers, in order

1. A right branch approaching \(y=-2\) from below. It looks like a textbook hyperbola and it silently contradicts (d), because a branch below \(-2\) that keeps falling can never come back up to it.

2. Drawing the left of \(x=3\) going down. \(\frac1{x-3}\) dives on the left of its pole; use an even power or two pieces, never a bare \(\frac1{x-a}\).

3. Answering [3,infinity) to "where is \(f\) decreasing". An interval of decrease cannot contain a point outside the domain.

4. Reading \(f'<0\) as \(f<0\).

If the item is multiple choice, eliminate rather than select, and not in the printed order: the tail's side of approach first, then the left of \(x=3\), then the far left, then the bend on \([4,\infty)\), and only last the height at \(x=0\).

9The trap list

  1. Drawing the right branch arriving at \(y=-2\) from below. Conditions (b) and (d) forbid it: a decreasing branch that ever dips to or below \(-2\) can never come back up. The most heavily signalled fact on the item, and the one a hyperbola drawn from memory gets wrong.
  2. Skipping the interpretation half. The question opens with "Give the interpretation in terms of the graph". A perfect sketch and no sentences is at best half of ten points.
  3. Reading \(f'<0\) as \(f<0\). The shipped right branch is above the axis on \((3,6)\) and below it after \(x=6\), and (d) holds on all of it.
  4. Drawing the left of \(x=3\) going down. Condition (c) names \(+\infty\). That is route W, which breaks (c) and nothing else.
  5. Copying the bracket \([3,\infty)\) into the answer. \(f\) is undefined at 3, so no interval of decrease can contain it.
  6. Believing concave up means increasing. The shipped right branch is concave up and decreasing at the same time, everywhere.
  7. Believing the graph cannot cross \(y=-2\). It must, on \((0,3)\), and it does so twice. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers.
  8. Forcing the left tail onto \(y=-2\) as well. Only \(x\to+\infty\) was constrained.
  9. Ignoring the superscript minus in (c). Draw both branches up anyway, but know which version the paper printed.
  10. Assuming \(f'(0)=0\). Nothing says the marked point is a turning point; route D puts the minimum at \(3-\sqrt{15}\).
  11. Leaving \((-2,3)\) blank or arbitrary. It is not mentioned and it is not free: an unbroken branch must turn around and must cross \(y=-2\).
  12. Checking a slope with a centred difference, or a monotonicity claim at three points. Both report a defect as clean.
  13. Leaving it unlabelled. Dash both asymptotes and write their equations, mark the dot, write "decreasing" on each constrained interval and "concave up" on \([4,\infty)\).
  14. Failing the vertical line test. At most one hit everywhere, and no hit at \(x=3\).

|

|              f' < 0 , f'' > 0  -> falling, flattening -> settles FROM ABOVE
