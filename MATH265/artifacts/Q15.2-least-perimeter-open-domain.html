The Argument Is The Answer

Q15.2 wants the rectangle of area 220 with the smallest perimeter. The number is almost impossible to get wrong: it is a square, and three separate wrong objectives all produce the same square. What can go wrong is the sentence underneath it. The feasible domain is \(w > 0\), open at both ends, so there are no endpoints to compare and the Extreme Value Theorem that licensed Q15.1's whole method does not apply. The justification is the question.

02 What this question inherits, and what it adds

Q15.1 established the closed-interval method: list the critical points, add both endpoints, evaluate everything, and compare. The Extreme Value Theorem is what turns that comparison into a proof, and it needs a closed and bounded interval.

Here the interval is not given. You have to derive it, and what you derive is \(w > 0\): open on the left, because a width of zero has no matching length; unbounded on the right, because nothing caps how long a thin rectangle may be. So the theorem is unavailable, the endpoint rows of the candidate table are empty, and the comparison cannot be run at all.

Inherited

FROM Q15.1, UNCHANGED

What a critical point is. Local against absolute. The value against the point. And that an absence is a deliverable, which is why "there is no maximum" is a written answer rather than a blank.

Added

THE MODELLING LAYER

Turning prose into a constraint and an objective, naming the variables, eliminating one of them, and stating the feasible domain yourself.

The real difference

AN OPEN DOMAIN

No endpoints, no Extreme Value Theorem, no comparison. A minimum still exists, but you have to prove it with convexity, a sign change, or a limit at the ends.

03 The objective, and why the answer is a square

The first panel draws \(P(w) = 2w + 440/w\) with its two component terms. Two things in it are the whole question. There are no endpoint markers anywhere, because there are no endpoints: the curve simply leaves the top of the frame on the left and keeps climbing on the right. And the rising line \(2w\) crosses the falling hyperbola \(440/w\) directly beneath the minimum, which is the cheapest self-check on this question, drawn rather than asserted.

The minimum level is drawn all the way across so you can see that no part of the curve goes below it, and the curve leaves the top of the frame on the left while still climbing on the right, so neither end supplies a candidate. Compare this with Q15.1's figure, where the level line existed to show that the curve only reached it at an endpoint. Here there is no endpoint for it to reach.

The second panel is the same fact in the shape of the actual objects. Five rectangles, all of area \(220\), drawn to the same scale on one baseline. The perimeters run \(118\), \(71\), \(59.3296\), \(71\), \(118\), and the list is symmetric about the square.

That symmetry is exact and it is the deepest reason the answer is a square: \(P(220/w) = 440/w + 2w = P(w)\), so swapping the width and the length leaves the perimeter alone. A minimum of a function symmetric under \(w \mapsto 220/w\) must sit at that map's fixed point, and the fixed point is \(w = \sqrt{220}\).

And that is exactly why the square is not a check. Three of the ten plausible mutilations tested in this run report the correct dimensions, because minimising the semi-perimeter \(w + l\), the diagonal \(\sqrt{w^2 + l^2}\), or \(w^2 + l^2\) are all symmetric in \(w\) and \(l\) and are therefore stationary where the two are equal. "I got a square, so I must be right" is worth nothing.

04 The procedure, and where the fork is

  1. Name the variables and draw the thing. Two unknowns, \(w\) and \(l\).
  2. Write the constraint from the given number. \(wl = 220\). It is an equation, and it is never differentiated.
  3. Write the objective from what the stem asks to optimise. \(P = 2w + 2l\). It is a function, and it always is.
  4. Check the units. Every term of \(P\) must be a length, which rules out \(2w + 440\) before any arithmetic.
  5. Eliminate using the constraint. \(l = 220/w\), so \(P(w) = 2w + 440/w\).
  6. State the domain. \(w > 0\), open at both ends. This is the fork, and it decides everything after it.
  7. Differentiate and solve. \(P'(w) = 2 - 440/w^2 = 0\), so \(w^2 = 220\).
  8. Discard the infeasible root, saying why. \(-\sqrt{220}\) solves the same equation and is a local maximum; a width cannot be negative.
  9. Justify, because there are no endpoints. \(P''(w) = 880/w^3 > 0\) for all \(w > 0\).
  10. Recover the other variable. \(l = 220/\sqrt{220} = \sqrt{220}\), so it is a square.
  11. Check the constraint on your own answer. \(\sqrt{220}\cdot\sqrt{220} = 220\).
  12. Answer the question asked. Dimensions first, then the perimeter, separately.

Step 6 comes before step 7 and not after, and the reason is practical. Written first, step 8 becomes automatic and step 9 becomes obviously necessary. Written afterwards, the temptation is to invent an interval to compare on, and inventing one gets the right answer here by luck. See trap 4.

05 Four routes, and which one to use

A · eliminate, then \(P''\)

6 LINES · USE THIS ONE

\(P' = 2 - 440/w^2 = 0\) gives \(w^2 = 220\); \(P'' = 880/w^3 > 0\) for all \(w > 0\), so the local minimum is the absolute one. The words "for all \(w > 0\)" are what upgrade local to global, and they cost three words.

B · eliminate, then a sign chart

8 LINES · NEVER FAILS

\(P'(14) = -0.2449\) and \(P'(20) = +0.9\): one sign change, minus to plus. Slower, but it is the only route that survives \(P''\) being zero or undefined, which is what happened on both parts of Q15.1.

C · AM-GM

2 LINES · NO CALCULUS

\(2w + 440/w \ge 2\sqrt{2\cdot 440}\), which is \(2\sqrt{880} = 8\sqrt{55}\), with equality exactly when \(2w = 440/w\). A complete proof of a global minimum in two lines. Keep it as a check, not as the answer.

D · the symmetry route

4 LINES · EXPLAINS WHY

\(w\) and \(l\) are the roots of \(t^2 - st + 220 = 0\), real only when \(s^2 \ge 880\). The least semi-perimeter is \(\sqrt{880}\), and there the discriminant is exactly \(0\), so the two roots coincide.

Convexity is strictly stronger than the one-point test, and the distinction is the whole of route A. \(P''(c) > 0\) at the critical point proves a local minimum. \(P'' > 0\) on the whole domain proves that local minimum is the global one, with no comparison and no endpoints. That is precisely the gap an open domain opens up, and one phrase closes it.

06 The write-up, and how to check it in eight seconds

Let the rectangle be \(w\) by \(l\) cm. Constraint: \(wl = 220\), so \(l = 220/w\), with \(w > 0\).
Objective: \(P = 2w + 2l = 2w + 440/w\).
\(P'(w) = 2 - 440/w^2 = 0\), so \(w^2 = 220\), so \(w = \sqrt{220}\) (positive, since \(w\) is a width).
\(P''(w) = 880/w^3 > 0\) for all \(w > 0\), so \(P\) is convex and this is the absolute minimum.
Then \(l = 220/\sqrt{220} = \sqrt{220}\), so the rectangle is a square.
Dimensions \(2\sqrt{55} \approx 14.8324\) cm by \(2\sqrt{55}\) cm; perimeter \(8\sqrt{55} \approx 59.3296\) cm.

Do not use the perimeter to check the dimensions. The objective is flat to second order at its minimum, so a width of \(15\) instead of \(14.8324\) is \(1.13\) percent wrong and gives a perimeter of \(59.333333\) against the true \(59.329588\), which is \(0.0063\) percent wrong and agrees to four significant figures. And \(15\) by \(220/15\) satisfies the constraint exactly, so the constraint check does not catch it either. Only the derivative does.

The two self-checks fail on different wrong answers, which is why both are worth doing. The constraint check catches a rounded \(14.83\) by \(14.83\) square, whose area is \(219.9289\) and whose perimeter \(59.32\) is below the true minimum, an impossibility for a real rectangle. The AM-GM check catches a perimeter that is simply wrong. Neither catches a nearly-right width.

Magnitude sanity, if you have five seconds and no calculator: \(220\) sits between \(196 = 14^2\) and \(225 = 15^2\), so the side is between \(14\) and \(15\) and nearer \(15\), and the perimeter is a shade under \(60\).

09 The trap list

  1. Skipping the justification, because the number came out clean. The domain is open, so there is nothing to compare against and the derivative test or the sign chart is the only evidence there is. This is the trap specific to this question and it is the most expensive.
  2. Treating the square as a check. Three different wrong objectives report it, because all three are symmetric in \(w\) and \(l\). The shape confirms nothing.
  3. Forgetting to double one side. \(2w + l\) gives \(\sqrt{110}\) by \(\sqrt{440}\), which is the correct answer to the wall problem and therefore looks real.
  4. Inventing a closed interval so Q15.1's method can be used. On \([1,100]\) it returns the right minimum, so the wrong rule is rewarded; on \([16,100]\) it returns \(59.5\), a quarter of a percent out.
  5. Reporting the perimeter where the dimensions were asked, or the reverse. \(59.3296\) is exactly four times \(14.8324\), and both are lengths in cm.
  6. Rounding the dimensions and breaking the constraint. \(14.83\) by \(14.83\) has area \(219.9289\) and a perimeter below the true minimum. Round one side and recompute the other: \(14.83\) by \(220/14.83 = 14.8348\) keeps the area at exactly \(220\).
  7. Using the perimeter to check the dimensions. The objective is flat at the minimum, so a \(1.13\) percent error in the width shows up as \(0.0063\) percent in the perimeter.
  8. A sign slip in the derivative. \(2 + 440/w^2\) is positive everywhere, so it reports no critical point at all. The tell is the absence of a solution, not a wrong one.
  9. Solving \(w^2 = 440\). \(\sqrt{440} \approx 20.976177\), a \(41.42\) percent overshoot, perimeter \(62.928531\), \(6.07\) percent high. This one is visible in the value, so AM-GM catches it.
  10. Differentiating the constraint instead of the objective. \(\frac{d}{dw}(220) = 0\) says nothing. Label which equation is which before differentiating anything.
  11. Reading \(220\) as a length. It is printed as \(220\ \text{cm}^2\) and the exponent is the whole of the information. The units gate catches this for free.
  12. Simplifying \(\sqrt{220}\) to \(2\sqrt{110}\). \(220 = 4\cdot 55\) with \(55\) squarefree, so \(2\sqrt{55}\) is the fully simplified form. And note \(2\sqrt{110}\) is the same \(20.976177\) that turns up in traps 3 and 9.

\(20.976177\) appears three separate times in this question's neighbourhood: as the square's diagonal, as the long side of the three-sided fence, and as the answer to solving \(w^2 = 440\) by mistake. Seeing it come out is not evidence you solved the right problem.

11 Answer entry

The dimensions

TWO ANSWERS, BOTH THE SAME

Type 2*sqrt(55) or sqrt(220), with the explicit *. Say "a square of side" in words too, so the marker does not have to infer the shape.

The perimeter

A THIRD, SEPARATE ANSWER

Type 8*sqrt(55). Keep the radical exact; rounding to 14.83 breaks the constraint and puts the perimeter below its own minimum.

The expensive typo

AN UNBRACKETED EXPONENT

220^(1/2) is \(14.8324\) and 220^1/2 is \(220/2 = 110\), a factor of about seven, and it is marked wrong without comment. Bracket every fractional exponent.

The second most expensive

THE VALUE FOR THE POINT

Answering \(59.3296\) where the dimensions are wanted is exactly four times the true side. Answering \(14.8324\) for the perimeter is \(75\) percent low.

Always preview. The exam is answer-only and machine-marked, so a right method with a bad keystroke scores zero.
