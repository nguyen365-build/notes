MATH 265 · FINAL EXAM 2 · ITEM 8 · 4 POINTS · Q19.2

The Split That Fires

Q19.1 said the absolute value in $A=\int \lvert f-g\rvert$ is a rule you are told and never see fire. This is the question where it fires. The curves genuinely cross inside the interval, so the region is three pieces, and integrating straight through gives a negative number that becomes a confident wrong answer the moment somebody makes it positive.

THE QUESTION

Area between $\cos x$ and $y=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ on $[-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}]$

THE ANSWER

$2\sqrt2-2 \approx 0.8284271247$

TYPE INTO MÖBIUS

2*sqrt(2)-2

01 Four decisions, then one antiderivative

The integration in this question is $\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x$ and $\int c\,dx=cx$. Everything that can go wrong goes wrong before that.

  1. Solve for the crossings, as a family. $\cos x = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ gives $x=\pm\tfrac{\pi}{4}+2k\pi$. Write the whole family down, then keep the members inside the interval. Do not solve for one value.
  2. Notice both are strictly interior. $-\tfrac{\pi}{2} < -\tfrac{\pi}{4} < \tfrac{\pi}{4} < \tfrac{\pi}{2}$. That is what forces the split, and it is the only thing separating this question from Q19.1.
  3. Decide the orientation on each piece separately, at a point strictly between two knots where $\lvert\cos x-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\rvert$ is not small. Never at a knot: there the difference is zero and the comparison decides nothing.
  4. Use the symmetry on purpose. $\cos x-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ is even, so $\lvert D\rvert$ is even, so do $[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}]$ and double. Write the 2 down at the moment you use it.

Three errors share the value 0.2214414691, and one of them is the negative one. The moment a student writes “an area is positive” and drops the minus sign, the second row becomes the first row exactly.

The consequence is the opposite of Q19.1's, and that is the point of this page. On Q19.1 three errors all gave $-4.5$, the magnitude was right, and “make it positive” accidentally rescued you. Here the magnitude is wrong - 0.221 against 0.828 - so the same repair converts a visibly broken answer into a confident, plausible, wrong one. Settle the orientation before the integration, never after it.

A second collision: two errors share 0.3034928278, because the outer lobes are equal and a sign flip on one cancels the other exactly.

None of these is caught by arithmetic. A sketch with the three pieces marked and an explicit sentence saying which curve is on top in each catches six of the twelve. Writing the general solution before filtering catches two more. Writing the factor of 2 down at the moment you use the symmetry catches one.

03 Why $1/\sqrt2$, and why the $\pi$ cancels

Replace the line height with a general $c$ in $[0,1]$ and let $a=\arccos c$. The same three-piece calculation gives one formula for the whole family, checked against the catalog at 51 values of $c$:

$$A(c) = 4\sqrt{1-c^2} - 4c\arccos(c) + \pi c - 2$$

Differentiate, and the two $\arccos'$ terms cancel because $\cos a = c$:

$$\frac{dA}{dc} = \pi - 4\arccos(c), \qquad \frac{d^2A}{dc^2} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{1-c^2}} > 0$$

That vanishes exactly at $\arccos c=\pi/4$, which is $c=1/\sqrt2$. The question's own line height is precisely the one that minimises the area, and the minimum value is $2\sqrt2-2$.

And the $\pi$ appears in $A(c)$ only inside $\pi c - 4c\arccos c = c\,(\pi - 4\arccos c)$, which is $c$ times the derivative. At the stationary height that bracket is zero, so no $\pi$ survives. The cancellation has a reason.

Two things to carry from this. “There is no $\pi$ in my answer” is not evidence of a dropped term on this particular $c$ - at $c=1/2$ the answer is $2\sqrt3-2-\pi/6$ and the $\pi$ is genuinely there. And a “find $c$ so the area is $A$” question has two answers, not one, because $A(c)$ falls and rises again.

Archimedes is the dangerous one. Q19.1's best shortcut, $A=\tfrac23\times\text{span}\times\text{largest gap}$, is exact for a parabola against a chord. Applied to this question's middle lobe it gives 0.3067170615 against the true 0.3034928278 - 1.0624 percent high. A cosine arch resembles a parabola closely enough that a wrong shortcut lands within one percent, and no mental check will reject it. Carry Archimedes for parabolas only.

All four computational routes plus the closed form agree, with a total spread of $6.7\times10^{-16}$.

05 Two numerical warnings, because both look like health

A NEAR-MISS CROSSING IS ALMOST FREE

Moving the split from $\pi/4$ to $\pi/4+0.0025$ changes the area by 0.000534 percent. The measured order of the error in the offset is 2.0094, 2.0047, 2.0024, 2.0012 - second order, because the difference vanishes at a true crossing. A mis-identified split at $3\pi/8$ costs 14.70 percent.

So the answer cannot tell you that a nearly-right crossing was guessed rather than solved. Solve it.

A LUCKY GRID IS NOT A CORRECT METHOD

Integrating $\lvert D\rvert$ straight through both kinks with Simpson at $n=4000$ has error $8.9\times10^{-16}$, which looks exact. At $n=4002$ the same routine on the same function has error $7.3\times10^{-8}$. The kinks sit at one quarter and three quarters of the span, so any $n$ divisible by 8 puts them on panel boundaries.

Measured orders across the kink: 4.02, 4.01, 4.00 aligned; 1.78, 1.89, 1.94, 1.97, 1.99 mis-aligned.

Q19.1 could not measure a convergence order at all, because its integrand was a quadratic and Simpson is exact on every cubic. Here it is measurable, and it was measured rather than quoted.

Two of those four roots on $[-2\pi,2\pi]$, at $\pm 7\pi/4$, are not in the region and contribute nothing. A periodic boundary makes “how many intersections are there” a question with no answer until you say where. On Q19.1 you could search $[-6,6]$ and be safe; here that finds real roots the question never asked about.

The census has two honest failures, both measured on this page rather than assumed. At a tangency it finds nothing, because a curve that touches without crossing has no sign change - and whether it finds anything at all depends on grid parity: on $\cos x=1$ an even grid, which lands exactly on $x=0$, reports one root and an odd grid reports zero. And a grid point landing exactly on a genuine root makes the sign-change product zero rather than negative, so the root is lost silently. That last one cost a wrong drill answer before it was caught.

The structure worth seeing. The three F1 rows are one formula at three values of $c$, and the third has no algebraic part at all because $4\sqrt{1-c^2}=2$ exactly cancels the $-2$ there. F2's first row is numerically identical to F1's second, because $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ is $\cos x$ on $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ shifted. F4's first row equals the exam answer from an entirely different geometry, so meeting $2\sqrt2-2$ on a paper does not tell you which question you solved. The two F6 rows agree because both windows have length $2\pi$: any window of one full period gives the same total, wherever it starts. The one integration technique the whole category needs beyond the basics is $\int\arccos y\,dy = y\arccos y-\sqrt{1-y^2}$.

08 The trap list

  1. Not splitting at the crossings. Gives $-0.2214$, and then “make it positive” gives $+0.2214$. This is the whole question.
  2. Losing the $\pm$ in $x=\pm\arccos c$. Half the crossings, or a region in the wrong place.
  3. Forgetting to double after using the symmetry. Gives $\sqrt2-1=0.4142$, positive and plausible.
  4. Taking the wrong branch. $3\pi/4$ solves $\cos x=-1/\sqrt2$. Check the branch against the sign of $c$.
  5. Taking the wrong period. $7\pi/4$ is a real solution and is not in the interval.
  6. Deciding orientation at a crossing. The difference is zero there and decides nothing.
  7. Deciding orientation once for the whole interval when the curves cross. Three pieces, three decisions.
  8. Splitting somewhere that is not a crossing. Splitting at $x=0$ changes nothing, because the sign does not turn there.
  9. Degree mode. Gives 12.69, which at least announces itself.
  10. Reading the crossings off a sketch. A near-miss costs 0.0005 percent, so you will never be told you were guessing.
  11. Assuming a missing $\pi$ means a dropped term. On this specific $c$ the $\pi$ cancels for a reason.
