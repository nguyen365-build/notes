Naming the Rules

Two derivatives, and a second sentence that changes the job: state which rule(s) you are applying. It is the only question in the bank that grades the name of the rule rather than the result of using it, and it closes the four-question derivative block.

The answers
a.\(\dfrac{2\sec^2(2x)\sqrt{x}-\tan(2x)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}}{x}\), or cleared, \(\dfrac{4x\sec^2(2x)-\tan(2x)}{2x^{3/2}}\)
quotient rule, then chain rule, then power rule
b.\(-6x\cos^2(x^2)\sin(x^2)\), which at \(x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\) is \(-\dfrac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\approx-1.8799712060\)
chain rule twice, then power rule
What to type into Möbius
a.(4*x/cos(2*x)^2-tan(2*x))/(2*x^(3/2))
b.-3*sqrt(2*pi)/4
Part (b) is a single number, so it is the one that must be exactly right. The grader tests equivalence, so -(3/4)*sqrt(2)*sqrt(pi) and -3*sqrt(pi)/(2*sqrt(2)) are accepted too.

01What closes here

This is the last of four tutorials on the same block of exam questions, and the four are cumulative on purpose. Each one adds exactly one idea to a procedure that never changes.

Those point values were read out of the four papers rather than typed: they sum to 48 across 15 sub-parts, which makes this the largest computational block in the course.

What is new, and it is not mathematics

On the other three questions, rule selection stays private. You pick a rule in your head, you write the derivative, and the derivative is the whole answer. Here the paper prints “State which rule(s) you are applying”, and that one sentence turns a private habit into a graded artifact. So the new content is about writing, and it is three things.

A vocabulary

There is a small fixed set of names a marker recognises. You cannot be graded on producing a name you do not have, so section 03 counts the list out of the course's own files.

A granularity

The printed “(s)” is plural, so a list is expected. The live question is how fine that list must be: does one “chain rule” cover a three-layer composite?

A new way to lose marks

Everywhere else, a correct derivative is a correct answer. Here you can differentiate perfectly and still drop marks by labelling the work wrongly, by naming a rule you did not use, or by leaving the labels off entirely. None of those are calculus errors, which is exactly why they are easy to miss when you revise.

Four points on a paper worth 81 makes this the joint-lightest item on Final 2, at 4.94 percent. Read that as good news with a catch: it should be the fastest four points you collect, and the only thing that makes it slow is hesitating over the naming demand.

Does one “chain rule” cover three layers?

This is the only genuinely ambiguous thing about the question, so it is worth settling now rather than in the exam room. Part (b) has three layers, two of which are chain-rule steps.

GranularityWhat you would write for (b)The risk
coarse“chain rule”if the marker wants one name per step, this understates the work by two thirds
per step“chain rule (applied twice), and the power rule”none that I can see
per layer, named“chain with a power outer, then chain with a \(\cos\) outer, then the power rule”none, other than time
Write the middle one

The reason is a cost asymmetry, not a piece of knowledge. Naming too finely costs a few seconds of writing. Naming too coarsely costs marks if, and only if, the marker was counting steps. You cannot know which marker you have, and the extra precision is nearly free, so the fine-grained list wins either way.

Being honest about that advice

No marking scheme, rubric, or part-mark breakdown exists in any course file. That was checked: the words “rubric”, “marking scheme” and “part marks” appear nowhere in the bank, the papers, or the review notes. So every claim here about how much naming earns is inference from the printed instruction. What is not inference is the instruction itself, and the fact that its “(s)” is plural: a question expecting one name would not have written it.

The previous question in this block taught you to write a layer ledger so you would stop losing factors in deep composites. Add one column to it and it stops being a private scratchpad: the last column is the rule list this question asks for.

The factor count still works as a check, unchanged: three layers must give three factors. An answer with a 3 in front has lost the inner layer; an answer with a 2 in front has lost the outer one.

05The two parts, worked

They are a deliberate pair rather than two random derivatives. Part (a) is a breadth test and part (b) is a depth test, and between them they force you to name every rule the course teaches except the product rule.

Part (a): a quotient, three different names

The last operation is a division, so this is the quotient rule with \(u=\tan(2x)\) and \(v=\sqrt x=x^{1/2}\). Then \(u'=2\sec^2(2x)\) by the chain rule, and \(v'=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}\) by the power rule.

$$y'=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}=\frac{2\sec^2(2x)\sqrt x-\tan(2x)\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}}{x}$$

Stop here

That is a complete answer. The item prints “You may not need to simplify your answer”, so every line past this point is risk without reward. If you do want the single cleared fraction it is \(\frac{4x\sec^2(2x)-\tan(2x)}{2x^{3/2}}\), and the two forms were checked as an identity to \(10^{-16}\) relative.

The power rule on \(\sqrt x\) is the name almost everyone uses and almost nobody writes down, because \(\sqrt x\) does not look like \(x^n\) until you rewrite it. It is the single most-omitted name on this question.

Part (b): substitute last, and the point is designed

First read the expression correctly: \(\cos^3(x^2)\) means \(\left[\cos(x^2)\right]^3\). It does not mean \(\cos(x^6)\). Those are different functions, not just different derivatives: at \(x=1\) they are \(0.158\) and \(0.540\).

Run the ledger from section 04, then and only then substitute. At \(x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\) we have \(x^2=\frac\pi4\) exactly, so \(\cos^2\left(\frac\pi4\right)=\frac12\) and \(\sin\left(\frac\pi4\right)=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\):

$$y'\left(\tfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\right)=-6\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=-\frac{6\sqrt{2\pi}}{8}=-\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\approx-1.8799712060$$

The total-loss error

Substituting \(x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\) before differentiating means you are differentiating the constant \(\cos^3\left(\frac\pi4\right)\), whose derivative is zero. The vertical bar says where to evaluate, not when to substitute.

That \(\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\) looks awkward until you square it. An examiner who wanted an ugly number would not have picked a value whose square is the one angle where sine and cosine agree, so the moment you see it, expect \(\frac\pi4\) and expect the final arithmetic to be clean.

The route changes the rule list

This is the signature idea of the question and it exists nowhere else in the block. Every function below can be attacked more than one way, and each route is correct. What changes is the names, and the names are graded.

06The two points you must not check at

The check itself is inherited and unchanged: compute \(\frac{f(x+0.001)-f(x-0.001)}{0.002}\) and compare. At \(h=0.001\) both parts clear five significant figures, which is the step size measured as optimal on a ten-digit calculator.

What is specific to this question is where you are allowed to do it. An earlier question in this block established that a dropped chain factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1. Both of this question's most likely errors have such a point, and both of those points are numbers a student would naturally reach for.

PartThe likely errorThe factor that goes missingIt equals 1 at
aforgetting the power rule, writing \(v'=1\)\(\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}\)\(x=\frac14\)

The instruction

Never check at \(x=\frac14\) or \(x=\frac12\) on this question. Use \(x=1.1\), or use the exam's own point for part (b), which sits comfortably clear of both blind spots. More generally: choose the check point after looking at your chain factors, not before.

One thing the check cannot do here. It confirms the derivative and says nothing at all about the rule list, which is half the marks. There is no numerical check for naming, which is why the audit below is written as a procedure rather than left to care.

07The write-up, and the two-way audit

The simplification policy from the first question in this block still applies, and the naming demand runs against it. That opposition is the whole of this section, and nothing else in the block has a component where doing more is the safe direction.

Simplify as little as you can. Name as much as you can.

Algebra past the derivative is unpaid risk. Naming past the minimum is paid, or at worst free, because the question asked for it in writing.

What counts as naming a rule

This counts

“By the quotient rule, with \(u=\tan 2x\) and \(v=\sqrt x\).”  “By the chain rule, outer \(\tan\), inner \(2x\).”  “By the power rule on \(x^{1/2}\).”

This does not

Writing \(\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}\) with no name attached. A correct answer with no words at all. And worst of the three, naming a rule you did not use, which is a positive claim about your method that the marker can see is false.

The short form, if time is tight

a. By the quotient rule with \(u=\tan(2x)\), \(v=\sqrt x\); \(u'=2\sec^2(2x)\) by the chain rule, \(v'=\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}\) by the power rule: $$y'=\frac{2\sec^2(2x)\sqrt x-\tan(2x)\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}}{x}.$$

b. By the chain rule applied twice, with the power rule on the inner \(x^2\): $$y'=-6x\cos^2(x^2)\sin(x^2),$$ and at \(x=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\), where \(x^2=\frac\pi4\), this is \(-\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\).

That is roughly ninety seconds of writing and it answers both halves of the question. Notice how little of it is extra work: every clause is something you had to decide anyway, and the only difference is that the decision is now on the paper instead of in your head.

The audit, in both directions

Before you move on, read the answer and the rule list against each other. It takes about ten seconds and it catches the failure mode unique to this question.

The second direction is the one that catches real omissions, and on part (a) it catches the specific one people make.

09The traps, ranked

  1. Not naming the rules at all. The question asks in writing, and it is half of what makes this item different from the other three.
  2. Dropping the inner \(2x\) in part (b). You write \(-3\cos^2(x^2)\sin(x^2)\) and it looks completely reasonable. Catch it with the factor count: three layers, three factors, coefficient 6.
  3. Substituting the point before differentiating. Then you are differentiating a constant and the answer is 0. Total loss on part (b).
  4. Writing the quotient rule as \(uv'-u'v\). The sign flips and the answer is exactly wrong. Write \(\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}\) with blanks before naming \(u\) and \(v\), every time.
  5. Forgetting the power rule on \(\sqrt x\). Invisible at \(x=\frac14\), which is why section 06 forbids checking there.
  6. Squaring errors at \(\frac\pi4\). \(\cos^2\left(\frac\pi4\right)=\frac12\), not \(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\). Getting it wrong scales the answer by \(\sqrt2\), at the last step, when attention is lowest.
  7. Misreading \(\cos^3(x^2)\) as \(\cos(x^6)\). Different functions, not just different derivatives.
  8. Naming a rule you did not use. Worse than silence, because it is a false claim the marker can check.
  9. Bracketing errors in Möbius. x^3/2 is not x^(3/2), both parse, and neither raises an error.

11Closing the block: one procedure, four questions

This is the last of the four, so it is worth saying plainly what they add up to. Everything in the block is one loop, and each question stresses a different part of it.

1. If a point is given, IGNORE it until step 5.
2. Ask what the LAST operation is.  That names the rule.  Write the name down.
3. Write the rule with BLANKS, then fill in u and v, or outer and inner.
4. RECURSE on every piece, one ledger line and one name per turn,
   until every piece is x^n or a constant.
5. ASSEMBLE, then substitute the point, then run a second pass if one was asked.
6. COUNT: layers against factors.
7. CHECK with a centred difference at h = 0.001, at a point you chose
   AFTER looking at your chain factors.
8. Stop simplifying.  Name generously, simplify stingily.

Steps 1 to 4 come from Q8.1. Step 5's second clause is Q8.2's. Steps 4 and 6 in ledger form are Q8.3's. Step 2's “write the name down” and step 8's second clause are this question's. There is nothing else.
