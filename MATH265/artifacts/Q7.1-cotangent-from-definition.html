Cotangent From The Definition

The only question on the paper that asks where the differentiation rules come from. It is a proof, not a computation, so the answer is worth almost nothing and the six marks sit in six lines of algebra with a limit in front of them.

The answer
$$\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x$$

Valid for every \(x\) with \(\sin x\neq0\), that is for \(x\neq k\pi\). Stating that domain is part of a complete answer.

Read this before anything else

Writing \(-\csc^2 x\) on a blank page scores the same as writing nothing. The instruction is "use the definition of the derivative as a limit", and that is an imperative about method. Quoting the memorised table entry answers a question nobody asked, and differentiating \(\cos x/\sin x\) by the quotient rule assumes exactly the result you were told to establish.

The tell is one symbol: if \(\lim\) never appears in your work, you did not use the definition.

The identity on rung 4 was tested as an identity in two free variables, at 91 different \((x,h)\) pairs: thirteen values of \(x\) spread across four quadrants, crossed with seven values of \(h\) including negative ones. It holds at every single pair. So does every other step of all four routes below. Checking only that the chain ends at \(-\csc^2x\) would verify the one line a marker gives no credit for.

03The two things to have cold

The identity, and why it is the only one that matters

You will use the sine subtraction formula backwards, and that is the move students miss.

$$\sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B \qquad\xrightarrow{\ A=x,\ B=x+h\ }\qquad \cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)=-\sin h$$

Two things about it deserve a moment. The right-hand side has no \(x\) in it at all: an expression in two variables collapsed to one in a single variable, which is what a subtraction formula does and is why this question is six marks rather than sixteen.

And the sign is the entire difference between \(\cot\) and \(\tan\). Run the same move on \(\tan\) and the numerator is \(\sin(x+h)\cos x-\sin x\cos(x+h)=+\sin h\). Same identity, arguments swapped, opposite sign. That one sign is why \(\tan'=+\sec^2x\) and \(\cot'=-\csc^2x\). Both are verified.

The limits, and which one you actually need

$$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\qquad\qquad\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}=0$$
The structural fact worth more than any single answer

Which of the two standard limits you need is a fact about the function, not about you. For \(\cot\) and \(\tan\) you need only \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1\), because every \(\cos h\) term cancels identically before any limit is taken. For \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) you need both, because a \(\cos h\) survives to the very end. That is the whole shape of section 06, family 1.

05Four routes, and which one to write

All four are legitimate uses of the definition and all four give \(-\csc^2x\). Seeing the same answer arrive by four roads is the strongest check available that the answer is right.

Route B, expand both angles

The course's own review notes point here. It works, it is one line longer, and it has one more place to slip. Expand \(\cot(x+h)\) top and bottom, subtract \(\cot x\), and the numerator is

$$\sin x(\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h)-\cos x(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)=-\sin h(\sin^2x+\cos^2x)=-\sin h$$

The two \(\cos h\) terms cancel against each other and the Pythagorean identity collapses the rest. The denominator \(\sin x(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)\) is just \(\sin x\sin(x+h)\), so Routes A and B meet at the identical expression. The instructive part is what does not happen: the \(\cos h\) terms die before any limit, so this route never needs the second standard limit either.

Route C, the cotangent addition formula

Shorter than either, if you happen to know \(\cot(A+B)=\dfrac{\cot A\cot B-1}{\cot A+\cot B}\).

$$\cot(x+h)-\cot x=\frac{-(1+\cot^2x)}{\cot x+\cot h}=\frac{-\csc^2x}{\cot x+\cot h}$$

The answer has appeared before any limit is taken. Divide by \(h\), and since \(h\cot x\to0\) while \(h\cot h\to1\), the denominator tends to 1. Elegant, and a bad exam choice for one reason: almost nobody has that formula memorised, and rebuilding it under pressure costs more than Route A costs in total.

Route D, the \(x\to a\) form

$$\cot'(a)=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\cot x-\cot a}{x-a}=\lim_{x\to a}\left(-\frac{\sin(x-a)}{x-a}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin x\sin a}\right)=-\frac{1}{\sin^2a}$$

Same mathematics, different bookkeeping. Use it when the question hands you a specific point, or when a limit is disguised as something else, which is family 9 below.

07Why you cannot check a proof with a calculator

The temptation, once the algebra is done, is to punch \(h=10^{-6}\) into a calculator and see whether \(-1.4123\) comes out. That checks the answer. It does not touch the derivation, which is what is being marked. And it is a much weaker check than it looks, in three ways that are worth knowing on their own terms.

Halving \(h\) halves the forward error and quarters the centred one, so the forward quotient is first order and the centred one is second order. Both claims are made over a five-rung ladder chosen inside the asymptotic regime, because a single value proves nothing about a method; both were tested against seeded controls that swap the two and correctly fail.

Smaller h is not better past a point

The centred error at \(h=10^{-6}\) is \(2.77\times10^{-13}\). At \(h=10^{-12}\) it is \(3.18\times10^{-5}\), which is a hundred million times worse. Subtracting two nearly equal numbers and dividing by a tiny one loses more to floating-point rounding than it gains in truncation. A numerical check has a sweet spot, and driving \(h\) to zero walks straight past it.

And here is the number that matters for this question. You need \(h\) below \(4.9\times10^{-4}\) before the forward quotient gives even three correct significant figures. Someone "checking" their derivation at \(h=0.01\) sees \(-1.4033\) against a true \(-1.4123\), and could not tell a correct answer from one wrong in the third digit.

08The write-up, and typing it

What to write if the paper wants method

There is no published rubric for this paper, so the allocation below is proposed, inferred from where the mathematical content actually sits. Treat it as a guide to what must appear on the page, not as a quotation.

marksfor
1writing the definition with \(\cot\) substituted in
1rewriting \(\cot\) as \(\cos/\sin\) and combining over one denominator
2recognising the numerator as \(\sin(x-(x+h))=-\sin h\), and naming the identity
1separating \(\frac{\sin h}{h}\) and citing \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\)
1finishing to \(-\csc^2x\), using the continuity of \(\sin\)

By definition, \(\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}\). Using \(\cot t=\frac{\cos t}{\sin t}\) and combining over the common denominator \(\sin(x+h)\sin x\) gives \(\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)}{h\,\sin(x+h)\sin x}\).

By the sine subtraction formula \(\sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B\), taken with \(A=x\) and \(B=x+h\), the numerator is \(\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)=\sin(-h)=-\sin h\), using that sine is odd. So the quotient is \(\displaystyle-\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(x+h)\sin x}\).

Each factor has a limit: the first tends to 1 by \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\), and the second tends to \(\frac{1}{\sin^2x}\) since \(\sin\) is continuous. Because both limits exist, the limit of the product is the product of the limits, and \(\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x\), valid for all \(x\neq k\pi\).

One thing to be careful not to write: do not say "cancel \(h\)". Nothing cancels here. The \(h\) pairs up with \(\sin h\) and is disposed of by a limit, not by algebra, and that distinction is exactly what separates the trigonometric cases from the polynomial ones.

  1. Using the quotient rule. It gets the right answer and answers a question nobody asked. This is the number one way to score zero while writing correct mathematics.
  2. Quoting \(-\csc^2x\) with no working. The same failure, in a shorter form.
  3. The direction of the subtraction, which flips the sign. You need \(\sin(x-(x+h))\), not \(\sin((x+h)-x)\). Backwards gives \(+\csc^2x\), which is wrong by a sign and looks completely plausible. The guard is that \(\cot\) decreases, so \(\cot'\) is negative.
  4. Saying "cancel the \(h\)". Nothing cancels in a trigonometric case, and this is the trigonometric case. The course's own review notes give exactly this advice; see the footer.
  5. Never naming the identity. Letting \(-\sin h\) appear from nowhere throws away the two marks that step is worth.
  6. Never citing \(\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1\). It is the only non-algebraic fact in the whole proof.
  7. Losing the minus sign inside the power on entry. (-csc(x))^2 is positive.
  8. Dropping the domain. \(-\csc^2x\) holds only where \(\sin x\neq0\). One clause completes the answer.
  9. Splitting the limit without saying why it is allowed. A limit of a product is the product of the limits only when both exist.
  10. Reaching for \(\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0\) here. A correct fact, not needed on either route, and using it signals you ground through Route B without noticing the cancellation.
  11. Confusing the pole at \(x=k\pi\) with a corner. Different failures, different explanations.
  12. Trying to check the derivation with a calculator. At any plausible \(h\) it does not even check the answer to three digits.

11The one-page decision tree

"Use the DEFINITION to find the derivative of f."
|
+-- FIRST, before anything: write
|      f'(x) = lim (h->0) [ f(x+h) - f(x) ] / h
|    with your f substituted in.  That is a mark, and it is free.
|    If the symbol lim never appears in your work, you did not use
|    the definition, whatever else you wrote.
|
+-- What SHAPE is f?  This picks the opening move.
|     POLYNOMIAL    -> expand f(x+h), cancel h, substitute h = 0.
|     A QUOTIENT    -> put it over ONE denominator.
|     A ROOT        -> multiply top and bottom by the CONJUGATE.
|     TRIGONOMETRIC -> an IDENTITY.  h will NOT cancel.
|
+-- Trigonometric?  Which identity is decided by which function:
|     sin, cos   -> ADDITION formula.  You will need BOTH standard
|                   limits, because a cos h survives to the end.
|     tan, cot   -> ONE DENOMINATOR, then SINE SUBTRACTION.
|                   Numerator becomes +sin h (tan) or -sin h (cot).
|                   You need only sin(h)/h -> 1.
|     sec, csc   -> ONE DENOMINATOR, then SUM-TO-PRODUCT.
|                   Numerator carries sin(h/2).
|
+-- For cot, the four lines that matter:
|     1. cot(x+h) - cot x   over   sin(x+h) sin x
|     2. numerator = sin( x - (x+h) ) = sin(-h) = -sin h
|        (the SUBTRACTION runs x MINUS x+h.  Backwards flips the sign.)
|     3. split:  -(sin h / h) * 1/( sin(x+h) sin x )
|     4. limit:  -(1) * 1/sin^2 x  =  -csc^2 x,   for x != k*pi
|
+-- Before you stop, NAME three things.  Two marks live here.
|     the identity you used;  the standard limit you used;
|     why the limit splits (both factors have limits).
|
+-- CHECK, in this order:
      SIGN      cot DECREASES on every branch, so cot' < 0 everywhere.
      NUMERATOR after the identity it must contain NO x.
      SIZE      |cot'| >= 1 always; the largest value it takes is -1.
      POINT     cot'(pi/4) = -2.  A whole number, easy to hold.
      DOMAIN    say "for x != k*pi".  One clause, and it finishes it.
      TYPE IT   -1/(sin(x))^2, minus OUTSIDE the square.  Then PREVIEW.
