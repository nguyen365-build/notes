The Invisible Factor

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the hinge of this course: it is the one statement that says differentiation and integration are the same subject read in opposite directions. This question puts two variable limits on one integral, and the two of them behave completely differently. One carries a chain factor of 2, and dropping it is caught immediately. The other carries a chain factor of 1, and dropping it cannot be caught by any check at all.

THE QUESTION

Use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt\)

THE ANSWER

\(\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right)\)

TYPE INTO MÖBIUS

sin(x^2)-2*sin(4*x^2)

The whole method, in four lines

  int from 2x to x  of f(t) dt
        |
        |  1. SPLIT at any constant c:   int_{2x}^{c} + int_{c}^{x}
        |  2. FLIP the first one:        int_{c}^{x} - int_{c}^{2x}
        |  3. FTC1 + CHAIN on each:      f(x)*(x)'  -  f(2x)*(2x)'
        |  4. SUBSTITUTE into f:         sin(x^2)*1 - sin((2x)^2)*2
        v
  sin(x^2) - 2 sin(4x^2)

Read step 3 as one sentence: top limit minus bottom limit, each one plugged into \(f\) and each one multiplied by its own derivative. The constant \(c\) never survives to the answer, which is the sign that splitting there was legitimate. Splitting at \(0\) is the usual choice, and it works even when \(0\) lies outside the interval.

Why there is a factor at all

A chain factor is a speed. The upper limit of this integral sits at \(t = x\), so when \(x\) moves by one, that end of the interval moves by one. The lower limit sits at \(t = 2x\), so the same move drags that end twice as far. The derivative of the accumulated area picks up exactly that speed as a multiplier, which is all the chain rule is doing here.

Which half of the theorem is this question asking for

Both halves are called the Fundamental Theorem, and telling them apart takes one glance at where the \(\frac{d}{dx}\) is sitting.

The integrand is a deliberate choice. \(\sin\left(t^2\right)\) has no elementary antiderivative at all: it is the Fresnel sine integral, and no combination of powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions differentiates to it. So the Part 2 route is not merely slow here, it is impossible. When an examiner writes a nasty integrand and then puts a \(\frac{d}{dx}\) outside it, that pairing is telling you not to integrate anything.

Three moves, and one of them is invisible

MOVE 1 · SPLIT

Two variable limits cannot be handled at once, so cut the interval at a constant. \(\int_{2x}^{x} = \int_{0}^{x} - \int_{0}^{2x}\). The minus is the orientation convention \(\int_a^b = -\int_b^a\), and it is the only place the minus sign in the answer comes from.

MOVE 2 · CHAIN, TOP

\(\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \sin\left(x^2\right)\cdot 1\). The factor is \(1\) because the derivative of \(x\) is \(1\). Leave it out and the expression does not change.

MOVE 3 · CHAIN, BOTTOM

\(\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \sin\left(4x^2\right)\cdot 2\). Substitute the whole limit: \(f\) at \(2x\) is \(\sin\left((2x)^2\right)=\sin\left(4x^2\right)\), never \(\sin\left(2x^2\right)\).

The finding: one of the classic mistakes cannot be detected

The Q8.3 tutorial in this series established a rule about checking your own work: a dropped chain factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1. There it was a warning about one unlucky test point. Here it is the whole real line. The upper limit's factor is \(1\) at every value of \(x\), so a student who does not know that the chain rule applies to a limit of integration still writes the correct upper term. Fifteen distinct wrong answers were compared against the truth at 6001 points across \([-3,3]\): fourteen are caught almost everywhere, and one is not caught anywhere.

So validate a chain factor by counting, not by checking a number. One factor per variable limit, written down before anything is evaluated. That is the only method that sees the factor of 1.

And pick the check point on purpose

The point you test at matters more than the test. Every one of the fifteen candidate answers evaluates to zero at \(x=0\), because every term contains a sine of something that vanishes there. Two more slip through at \(x=1\), where \(4x^2\), \(4x\) and \(2\cdot 2\) all happen to coincide.

Two cheap checks catch things a spot value cannot. The answer must be an even function of \(x\), because \(F\) is odd and the derivative of an odd function is even. And there must be exactly one term per variable limit, with the lower limit's term negative before any sign inside \(f\) is considered. Worth carrying: \(F'(1) = 2.3550759754\), \(F'(0.5) = -1.4355380104\), and \(F'\left(\sqrt{\pi}\right)=0\) exactly, since \(x^2=\pi\) makes both sines vanish at once.

What continuity is doing in the hypothesis

Continuity of \(f\) at a point is exactly what buys you \(F'=f\) at that point. Take the step function that is \(0\) below zero and \(1\) from zero up, and let \(F(x)=\int_0^x f\). Then \(F(x)=x\) on the right and \(F(x)=0\) on the left, so \(F\) is continuous, and its left and right difference quotients at \(0\) are \(0\) and \(1\). The derivative simply does not exist there, and it exists everywhere else. The theorem is not wrong at a discontinuity; it says nothing there.

For this question the screen is clean. As \(x\) runs over \([-3,3]\) the limits sweep \(t\) over \([-6,6]\), and \(\sin\left(t^2\right)\) is a composition of two entire functions. Sampling it 200001 times across that range found no non-finite value and no jump. So the answer holds for every real \(x\), with no exclusions.
