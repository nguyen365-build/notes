MATH 265 · Midterm 2 · Question 5 · 10 points

Five Conditions, One Sketch

Two of these five conditions land on the same point, and they cooperate: together they force a limit that neither of them states. A third is written as an equality with infinity, which is a stronger claim than it looks. Everything else is bookkeeping.

The question, verbatim

Interpret in terms of the graph of the function \(f\) the conditions listed below. Sketch the graph of a single function \(f(x)\) which satisfies all of the conditions listed below.

a · level
\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = 3\)
Horizontal asymptoteThe line \(y=3\) is a horizontal asymptote at the right-hand end: as \(x\) grows without bound the curve settles onto it and the vertical gap tends to zero.
Does not constrain the left-hand end, and does not stop the graph crossing \(y=3\).
b · point
\(f(0) = 0\)
A point on the graphThe function is defined at \(0\) and its value there is \(0\), so \((0,0)\) lies on the graph.
On its own, says nothing at all about the curve on either side of \(0\).
c · approach
\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to-1} f(x) = 2\)
Both branches convergeAs \(x\) approaches \(-1\) from the left and from the right, the height of the curve approaches \(2\).
Does not constrain \(f(-1)\), which may be \(2\), may be another number, or may be undefined.
d · pole
\(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1} f(x) = \infty\)
Vertical asymptote, both sides upBecause this is an equality with \(+\infty\) and not the phrase “does not exist”, the curve rises without bound on both sides of \(x=1\). \(f\) is undefined there.
Rules out \(\frac{1}{x-1}\), whose two sides disagree. An even power in the denominator keeps the sign.
e · kink
\(f\) is continuous but not differentiable at \(x=0\)
Unbroken, but no tangent lineBoth one-sided limits equal the value, so \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=f(0)=0\); but the two one-sided slopes differ, so \(f'(0)\) does not exist. The cheapest picture is a corner.
The word continuous deletes the break option, leaving corner, cusp and vertical tangent.

Conditions (b) and (e) both live at \(x=0\). That is the one structural difference from the four-condition version of this question, where the conditions sat at three separate places and could not interact.

The one sentence that decides this question. (b) puts a dot at the origin and (e) welds both branches onto it, so together they force \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=0\), which neither says alone. The four-condition version did the opposite at the same point and forced the graph to break there. Same notation, opposite picture.

01The answer, and how to read it

There is no unique answer, and that is the point: any function with these five features scores full marks, and the marks are spread over the five conditions. Here is one, chosen so that every landmark is a whole number or a small fraction.

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}-2x, & x \le 0\\[4pt] \dfrac{x}{1-x}, & 0 < x < 1\\[4pt] 3 + \dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x > 1\end{cases}$$

Read the picture in the order the marks are placed. A straight line of slope \(-2\) comes down through \((-1,2)\) to the origin. There it meets a rising curve at a visible angle: same height, different slope, which is condition (e) in its entirety. That curve crosses \(y=3\) at \(x=\tfrac34\) and runs up the dashed line \(x=1\). On the far side of that line a second branch comes down from \(+\infty\) and flattens onto \(y=3\) from above.

Why the graph crosses its own asymptote

The middle branch starts at height \(0\) and runs to \(+\infty\). If it is drawn without lifting the pen, the Intermediate Value Theorem forces it through every height above \(0\), and \(3\) is one of them. A horizontal asymptote constrains the tail, not the whole curve. Only the vertical asymptote is a barrier, and only because \(f\) is undefined there.

The two cells students get wrong are both in the “ok” column

\(f(a)=0\) together with \(\lim_{x\to a}f=2\) is legal: it is the displaced dot, a hollow circle at \((a,2)\) and a solid dot at \((a,0)\). And “continuous and not differentiable” is legal: it is condition (e), and it is the whole reason this question exists. Both are outlined in the matrix.

This tutorial's own first draft had those two cells marked impossible. The exhaustive searcher rejected the draft, which is what a gate is for.

04“\(=\infty\)” is stronger than “does not exist”

This is the single most misread line on the paper, and it is condition (d). The rule underneath is one sentence: an even power in the denominator keeps the sign, an odd power flips it.

Only two pictures exist here: both branches the same way, or the two branches opposite. Every row is one of those two with labels changed.

The most attractive wrong answer on this question

Change one sign in the middle branch, to \(\dfrac{x}{x-1}\), and conditions (a), (b), (c) and (e) all still hold. But now the left branch runs to \(-\infty\) while the right runs to \(+\infty\), the two-sided limit does not exist, and “\(\lim_{x\to1}f=\infty\)” becomes false rather than approximate. Verified: that function breaks condition (d) and only condition (d).

A hole is also not an asymptote. \(\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}\) has a vanishing denominator at \(x=1\) and its limit there is \(2\). The rule is “denominator zero after simplification”, and the course review file drops that qualifier in one of the three places it states the rule.

05Routing: what is forced between the marks

Once the marks are placed, each gap between them is a small design problem with a forced answer. This is the step the four-condition version never needed.

On an interval where \(f\) is continuousWhat is forced

\(f\) runs from \(+\infty\) down to \(L\)\(f\) takes every value above \(L\), and none at or below it
vertical asymptote at \(p\), horizontal at \(L\) for \(x>p\)the branch must come back down; it cannot keep rising
\(f'>0\) throughout, \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L\)\(f<L\) on that tail, approaching from below, concave down
\(f'<0\) throughout, \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L\)\(f>L\) on that tail, approaching from above, concave up

In this question it bites twice. The middle branch must cross \(y=3\), and the right branch must descend on net. A right branch that climbs contradicts (a) and (d) together, and which one it contradicts depends only on where you stopped drawing.

The last two rows answer most of the six-condition version of this question in one line: a tail that settles onto a horizontal asymptote is concave the opposite way from its direction of travel.

Any numeric reword is one substitution

For the condition set “\(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=H\); \(f(0)=v\); \(\lim_{x\to c}f=m\) with \(c<0\); \(\lim_{x\to p}f=+\infty\) with \(p>0\); continuous but not differentiable at \(0\)”:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} v + \dfrac{m-v}{c}\,x, & x\le0\\[8pt] v + \dfrac{x}{p-x}, & 0<x<p\\[8pt] H + \dfrac{1}{x-p}, & x>p \end{cases}$$

The corner slopes are \(\frac{m-v}{c}\) and \(\frac1p\). Eight instances of this were audited against their own five conditions. There is exactly one way to get it wrong: if \(\frac{m-v}{c}=\frac1p\) the two slopes agree, the join is straight, and condition (e) fails silently. If your numbers land there, scale the middle branch.

A trap in the checking itself

If you check differentiability by sampling either side of \(0\) and averaging, you are computing the centred difference \(\frac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}\), and for this function it returns a stable, entirely plausible \(-0.5\). That number is just the average of \(-2\) and \(+1\).

A centred difference is blind to corners, and here it does something worse than fail: it reports a specific slope, so it looks like a successful measurement. Differentiability has to be tested one side at a time, which is what the definition says. The automated checker behind this page was rewritten to do exactly that.

10The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

  1. Drawing the left side of \(x=1\) going down. Condition (d) is an equality with \(+\infty\), so both branches rise. Reaching for \(\frac{1}{x-1}\) because it is the hyperbola you know loses the most heavily signalled condition on the item.
  2. Skipping the interpretation half. The question opens with “Interpret in terms of the graph”. A perfect sketch and no sentences is at best half of ten points.
  3. Drawing a smooth curve through the origin. (b) and (e) together do force the curve through \((0,0)\) unbroken, which makes the smooth version feel right. What it must not be is smooth: the pen has to change direction.
  4. Breaking the graph at the origin instead. The opposite error, from pattern-matching to the four-condition version, where the conditions at \(x=0\) really did force a break. The word “continuous” is the difference.
  5. Answering “no” to “is \(f\) continuous at \(x=0\)”. The condition says continuous. Three of the four non-differentiability mechanisms are continuous anyway.
  6. Writing \(f(-1)=2\) as though (c) said so. It says the limit is \(2\). Making \(f(-1)=2\) is a legal choice, not a requirement, and a question can ask which it is.
  7. Believing the graph cannot cross \(y=3\). It must, on \((0,1)\), if that branch is drawn unbroken. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers.
  8. Forcing the left tail onto \(y=3\) as well. Only \(x\to+\infty\) was constrained.
  9. Letting the right branch rise. (a) and (d) together force it back down.
  10. Using a cusp and calling it a corner, or the reverse. A corner has two different finite one-sided slopes; a cusp has two infinite ones of opposite sign; a vertical tangent has two of the same sign.
  11. Checking differentiability with a centred difference. It returns a stable \(-0.5\) here, which is just the average of the two true slopes.
  12. Putting the asymptote at \(x=-1\) and the finite limit at \(x=1\). The conditions are printed out of order along the axis: (c) is at \(-1\) and (d) is at \(1\).
  13. Leaving it unlabelled. On a ten-point picture the marker is looking for five features. Dash both asymptotes and write their equations.
  14. Failing the vertical line test. A leftover solid dot turns a correct answer into a non-function.

11Drill set

No answers beside the questions. Twelve problems in under thirty minutes, on paper. For each one give the interpretation sentences and a labelled sketch, and name the mechanism you used at any non-differentiable point.

  1. \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=-2\); \(f(0)=1\); \(\lim_{x\to-1}f=3\); \(\lim_{x\to2}f=\infty\); continuous but not differentiable at \(0\).
  2. All five conditions, but the non-differentiability at \(0\) must be a cusp.
  3. All five, but with \(\lim_{x\to1}f=-\infty\).
  4. All five except that \(\lim_{x\to1}f\) does not exist because the two sides disagree.
  5. All five, and in addition \(f(-1)\) is undefined.
  6. Explain why no function can satisfy \(\lim_{x\to1}f=\infty\) and “\(f\) is continuous at \(1\)”.
  7. Explain why no function can satisfy “\(f\) is differentiable at \(0\)” together with condition (e).
  8. All five, and in addition \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f=3\).
  9. All five, but the right branch must approach \(y=3\) from below.
  10. Given \(f(x)=\dfrac{2x-6}{(x-2)^2}\), state the horizontal asymptote, the behaviour at \(x=2\) on each side, \(f(0)\), and every \(x\)-intercept.
  11. Write a function for: \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f=-1\); \(f(0)=0\); \(\lim_{x\to-2}f=4\); \(\lim_{x\to2}f=\infty\); continuous but not differentiable at \(0\).

13How every number here was checked

A sketch question has no single numeric answer, so what is verified is that a proposed function satisfies the conditions. Each condition is tested the way its own definition is written, never by looking at one sample point: a finite limit must show an error shrinking by at least a factor of five along a ladder down to \(10^{-6}\); a limit at infinity must shrink along a ladder up to \(10^{8}\); a pole must show a magnitude increasing at every rung, exceeding \(10^{8}\), with a consistent sign on each side separately; a corner must show two one-sided difference quotients each stable across \(h\in[10^{-5},10^{-7}]\) and differing from each other.

Two verifier defects, and both looked exactly like wrong mathematics

One. Route B's corner slopes were reported wrong. They are \(\pm5\), and the checker's default tolerance of \(10^{-4}\) rejected them. The one-sided quotient is exactly \(\frac{3h+5}{(h-1)^2}\), which is \(5+13h+O(h^2)\); at the ladder's first rung \(h=10^{-5}\) that is \(5.00013\), missing a \(10^{-4}\) window by thirty per cent. The error is truncation, not roundoff, and it scales with the second derivative: a tolerance calibrated on a piecewise-linear corner is too tight for a curved one. The claim now ships at \(10^{-3}\), with the exact rational identity beside it, one control confirming the original tolerance still rejects it, and a second confirming \(10^{-3}\) still rejects a genuinely wrong pair of slopes.

Two. The same-\(x\) matrix disagreed with the exhaustive searcher on two cells. The author was wrong, not the searcher: a value together with a different finite limit is the displaced dot, and it is legal. That is the single idea this question is built on, and the gate caught the tutorial writing it down backwards.

Three label collisions in this figure were caught the same way, by looking at the rendered page rather than at the code: x = 1 and corner (0, 0) sat on top of the tick labels, and the dashed rules struck through the 3 and 1 ticks. The figure is generated from the function, not drawn. Each branch is sampled separately and stops exactly on its own breakpoint, because a uniformly sampled polyline steps across \(x=0\) and rounds the corner off, which is the one feature the question tests. The finished figure is then re-read from its own coordinates: the left polyline ends exactly at the origin and the middle one starts exactly there; the drawn screen slope changes across that junction; no point lands on the pole column \(x=1\); every right-branch point is strictly above \(y=3\) and its gap to that line shrinks monotonically without reaching zero.
