Estimate, Not Interval

Item 3 of Final Exam 2 asks two questions about the same function over the same interval. Part (a) finds its largest and smallest values. Part (b) is this question, and it needs exactly those two numbers, one multiplication, and a decision about what to write down. This page is about that decision, because the verb changed and the calculation did not.

01The question and the answer

Final Exam 2, item 3b. "Use the properties of the definite integral to estimate the value of the integral \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x^2+1}\,dx\)."

$$1 \;\le\; \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \;\le\; 2 \qquad\text{so}\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \approx 1.5$$

The true value is \(\pi/2 \approx 1.5707963268\), which sits \(0.5707963268\) above the floor and \(0.4292036732\) below the ceiling.

"Estimate" is the only one of the three that asks for a number, and it is the reason a page that stops at \(1 \le I \le 2\) is an incomplete answer to this question while being a complete answer to Q17.1. One extra line closes it.

the comparison property

If \(m \le f(x) \le M\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\), then \(m\,(b-a) \le \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le M\,(b-a)\).

Read it as two rectangles standing on the same base. The integral is the area under the curve; \(m(b-a)\) is the area of the rectangle that fits underneath it and \(M(b-a)\) is the area of the one that covers it. The \((b-a)\) is the width of both. Leaving it out does not shrink the answer slightly; it turns an area into a height.

04Three numbers, and the one that disappears

The mistake this whole category exists to punish is writing \(m \le I \le M\) instead of \(m(b-a) \le I \le M(b-a)\). On this question the mistake is caught by your own answer. On Q17.1 the identical mistake is invisible. The picture below is the same slip drawn twice, on one shared scale.

Both panels show the correct bound in amber and the bound you get by dropping \(b-a\) in rust, with the true value marked. On Q17.2 the true value falls outside the rust bar. On Q17.1 it falls inside it.

The reason is not luck, it is the width. Dropping the factor divides your interval by \((b-a)\), so the slip survives a self-check exactly when \((b-a)\) is close to \(1\).

This contrast is the strongest argument in the category against trusting a self-check. The same student, making the same mistake, on two questions from the same category, is caught on one and not on the other, and nothing about the answer they wrote tells them which case they are in.

The inversion is structural rather than lucky. On this question the maximum sits exactly at the interval midpoint, so the midpoint rule evaluates \(f\) at its highest point and returns \(M(b-a)\), which is the ceiling itself. A rule that hands you back one end of your own interval has estimated nothing.

So the answer to "which number" is the midpoint of the bounds, because it is the only cheap option that cannot degenerate into a bound you already wrote down.

06What to write, in the order a marker reads it

On \([-1,1]\) the function \(f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2+1}\) is continuous, since \(x^2+1 \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\).

By part (a), its absolute maximum on \([-1,1]\) is \(M = 1\) at \(x = 0\), and its absolute minimum is \(m = \tfrac12\) at \(x = \pm 1\).

The interval has width \(b - a = 1 - (-1) = 2\).

By the comparison property of the definite integral, $$m(b-a) \le \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)\,dx \le M(b-a), \qquad\text{that is}\qquad 1 \le \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \le 2 .$$

Taking the midpoint of these bounds gives the estimate \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \approx 1.5\).

Then, on a separate line, if there is time:

(For comparison, the exact value is \(\big[\arctan x\big]_{-1}^{1} = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.571\), which lies inside the bounds.)

Why the exact value goes beside the estimate and not instead of it. The question says "use the properties of the definite integral". That phrase names the method, so the marks are for producing \(m\), \(M\), \(b-a\) and the comparison property, not for producing a number that happens to be right. A page reading only \(\pi/2\) has skipped the entire requested method. This is an inference, not a quotation: no marking scheme for MATH 265 exists in any of the four course files. It is the safe inference, and it costs one line to satisfy both readings.

07The variation catalog

Read each table as a structure rather than as a list of facts. Every row is the same three steps: find \(m\) and \(M\), find \(b-a\), multiply. What changes between families is only where \(m\) and \(M\) come from.

Family 0monotone integrand

Trigger: \(f'\) has no zero inside \([a,b]\).

Evaluate \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\); the smaller is \(m\), the larger is \(M\). No table, no calculus.

Family 1interior critical point

Trigger: \(f'\) has a zero strictly inside \([a,b]\). This question.

Run the closed-interval method: critical points, points where \(f'\) is undefined, and both endpoints.

Family 2part (a) supplies \(m\) and \(M\)

Trigger: a two-part item on one function. Also this question.

Copy the two numbers, multiply by \(b-a\), stop.

Family 3non-constant comparison

Trigger: the wording asks you to sharpen.

Find \(g \le f \le h\) with \(g,h\) integrable in closed form, then integrate those.

Family 4additivity

Trigger: a long interval, or "as accurately as you can".

Split, bound each piece with its own \(m\) and \(M\), add.

Family 5orientation and sign

Trigger: reversed limits, or a negative or odd integrand.

\(\int_b^a = -\int_a^b\), and multiplying an inequality by \(-1\) reverses it.

Family 6the property does not apply

Trigger: the integrand is unbounded on \([a,b]\).

Say so. No finite \(M\) exists, so there is no ceiling to write.

Family 7the three verbs

Trigger: read the verb before you start.

Interval, estimate, bound. Same calculation, three different last lines.

Family 8the estimate ladder

Trigger: the verb is "estimate" and you must commit.

Midpoint of the bounds, unless the question names another rule.

Family 9symmetry

Trigger: \(f\) even or odd on an interval symmetric about \(0\).

Even doubles the half-integral; odd gives exactly \(0\), which the property alone cannot see.

08What a self-check can and cannot catch

The obvious check is "does my interval contain the true value". It is a genuinely useful check and it is one-sided: it catches a bound that is too tight and can never catch one that is too loose.

Proved rather than asserted. Over 201201 sampled loosenings, with \(m' \le \tfrac12\) in steps of \(0.001\) and \(M' \ge 1\) in steps of \(0.01\), zero failed to contain \(\pi/2\).

The eighteen-row census below takes every way of answering this question that came to hand, with no row dropped as degenerate, and tags each one by computation.

The three that hide are worth knowing by name.

  1. The maximum read at \(x = 0.4\), which is still above the average value, so the ceiling drops but not far enough.
  2. The maximum read at \(x = c\) exactly, the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals point, which gives \([1,\ \pi/2]\) and touches the true value at its upper end.
  3. The answer written as the average value alone, \([\pi/2,\ \pi/2]\), which "contains" the true value by hitting it exactly while justifying nothing.

The dividing line for a mis-read maximum is the point \(c\) where \(f(c)\) equals the average value \(\pi/4\):

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi} - 1} = 0.5227232009 ,$$

which is \(52.27\%\) of the way along the half-interval \([0,1]\), confirmed by an independent 1001-point scan rather than by re-deriving the formula. Reading \(M\) anywhere in \([0,c)\) hides; reading it beyond \(c\) is caught. The boundary is not the midpoint and it is not obvious.

Against Q17.1's census (eighteen rows: 6 valid and containing, 4 invalid but containing, 8 caught), this question catches two more and hides one fewer. The row that flips is the headline one: the dropped \((b-a)\) factor hides there and is caught here. The other difference is in character, not count. Q17.1's hiders are all mis-read extremes; one of this question's three is a student who wrote no bound at all and simply guessed the right number.

Two rows of the census land on the same interval \([0.5,\ 1]\) from different mistakes: dropping \((b-a)\), and using symmetry to reduce to \([0,1]\) and then forgetting to double back. A wrong answer does not identify its own cause.

09The traps, ranked

  1. Dropping \((b-a)\). Caught on this question, invisible on Q17.1. Write \(b-a\) on its own line before multiplying.
  2. Answering with an interval when the verb said "estimate". Costs the last mark, not the method marks. One extra line fixes it.
  3. Answering with \(\pi/2\) alone. The question named the method. Give the sandwich first and the exact value as a bonus.
  4. Reading the minimum at \(x=0\). That is where the maximum is. The resulting bound \([2,2]\) misses \(\pi/2\), so this one is caught.
  5. Reading the maximum away from \(x=0\). Hides if you land before \(c = 0.5227232009\), caught after it.
  6. Sign slips on \(b-a = 1-(-1)\). Two minus signs in one subtraction. Write the substitution out.
  7. Using the property on an unbounded integrand. Screen for poles first. See Family 6.
  8. Believing that containment is a check. It only catches over-tightening. See section 08.

How the true value was pinned, and how many digits it is allowed to print. \(\pi/2\) was checked against four equivalent closed forms and two independent quadratures; tanh-sinh at level 9 and Gauss-Legendre at \(n=40\) agree with the closed form and with each other to under \(10^{-13}\).

Two precision findings were measured rather than assumed. First, Simpson's observed order on this integrand is 6, not the textbook 4: \(5.9948605833\) at \(n = 64 \to 128\) and \(6.1019065780\) at \(n = 128 \to 256\). The reason is analytic and was verified: \(f'''(x) = \frac{24x(1-x^2)}{(x^2+1)^4}\) vanishes at both endpoints \(x = \pm 1\), which kills the \(h^4\) term of the Euler-Maclaurin error expansion. A control integrand whose third derivative does not vanish at the endpoints shows the textbook order 4 on the same meter, so the 6 belongs to this integrand and not to the instrument.

Second, both quadrature ladders had to be bounded before they were read. Simpson reaches the roundoff floor at \(n=256\), and past that rung the measured order goes negative, which is float noise rather than a degraded method. Tanh-sinh is best at levels 8 and 9 and gets worse from level 10 on as summation error accumulates. A digit-lock inherits the precision of the method that produced it, so no decimal on this page was widened past what the computation actually resolved.

The root census, and why its arms are reported separately. \(f'\) has exactly one zero on \([-1,1]\), at \(x=0\), which is the exact centre of the interval. On a grid with an odd number of nodes \(x=0\) is a node, so a strict sign-change arm reports zero changes while an exact-grid-zero arm reports one; on an even grid the two swap. Both grids were run and both arms reported, and their sum is 1 either way. A census run only one way here would have printed \(0\) and looked exactly like a census that never ran. Two further arms: a value-turn arm reports 2, because \(f'\) turns at the inflection points \(x = \pm 1/\sqrt3 = \pm 0.5773502692\); and an undefined-point arm reports 0, because \((x^2+1)^2 \ge 1 > 0\).
