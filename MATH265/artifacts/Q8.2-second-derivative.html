The Second Pass

Three of these four derivatives are one rule each. The fourth asks for a second derivative, and that is a different kind of question: you run the procedure twice, and the rule you need on the second run may not be the rule you used on the first.

What this question adds to the one before it

Q8.1 settled how you pick a rule, how far you simplify, and how you check. All three carry over unchanged, and this page does not repeat them. What Q8.2 adds is the second differentiation, and it is three separate ideas: the simplification policy inverts when \(f'\) is an input rather than an answer; the rule choice can change between the two passes; and the numerical check you use on \(f''\) behaves in the opposite way to the one you use on \(f'\), so reusing Q8.1's advice about step size will actively mislead you.

01Part (a): the same expression, asked twice

The whole of Q8.2's difficulty lives in one sub-question. Here is what happens when you run the last-operation test on \(\cot(2x)\), and then run it again on what came out.

Pass 1chain, named outer

The last operation is \(\cot\) applied to \(2x\). The outer derivative is \({-\csc^2}\) and the inner derivative is \(2\).

\(f' = -2\csc^2(2x)\)
Stop and lookthe step with no analogue in Q8.1

Is \(f'\) as short as it will get? Here, yes, so this costs three seconds and returns nothing. On a quotient the answer is no, and cancelling here is what saves four minutes later.

\(-2\csc^2(2x)\) is already in lowest terms
Pass 2chain, POWER outer

Ask again, from scratch. The last operation in \(-2\csc^2(2x)\) is the squaring, not the \(\csc\). That is a different rule from pass 1.

\(f'' = 8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)\)
Where the 8 comes from, and why the sign is positive

The coefficient is \(2\times 2\times 2\): one factor of 2 from the power rule on the square, one from the chain rule on the inner \(2x\) during pass 2, and one from the coefficient \(-2\) that pass 1 produced. Miss any of the three and you write 4. The sign is \((-2)\times(-1)=+\), where the \(-1\) is the minus in \(\frac{d}{dx}\csc = -\csc\cot\), so two minus signs cancel and the answer is positive, which surprises people who expect the second derivative of a falling function to stay negative.

Cross-check the coefficient against the general formula \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(kx) = 2k^2\csc^2(kx)\cot(kx)\). For \(k=2\) that is 8, arrived at by counting something different, so two independent countings agree. That formula was verified for \(k=1\) through \(5\).

04The simplification policy, inverted

Q8.1's policy is that simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk, so you stop the moment the derivative is written. That is right when \(f'\) is the answer. It is wrong when \(f'\) is an input.

The whole policy in one question

Is there another differentiation coming? If yes, tidy \(f'\) now, because every factor you carry forward gets differentiated too. If no, stop, because Q8.1's risk calculation applies unchanged.

05Checking a second derivative, where Q8.1's advice reverses

The check for \(f'\) is a centred first difference and Q8.1's rule is that \(h=0.001\) gives five good digits and smaller is harmless. The analogue for \(f''\) is the centred second difference, and the second half of that rule is false for it.

The check

$$f''(x) \approx \frac{f(x+h) - 2f(x) + f(x-h)}{h^2}$$

Three evaluations, and a division by \(h^2\) instead of \(h\). Use \(h = 0.001\). Do not go smaller. A calculator holds about ten significant digits, so each \(f\) value carries an absolute error near \(10^{-10}\); dividing by \(h^2\) multiplies that by \(1/h^2\), and at \(h=10^{-5}\) that factor is \(10^{10}\), which is all of your precision.

Every number here was measured on a machine deliberately truncated to ten significant digits, not estimated.

07The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

  1. Not asking the last-operation question a second time. You differentiate \(\cot(2x)\) correctly, then differentiate \(-2\csc^2(2x)\) as if it were still a plain chain rule. This is the trap Q8.2 exists to test.
  2. Losing one of the three factors of 2 in part (a). You write \(4\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)\), which is half the right answer. Route D's \(2k^2\) is an independent count and it takes five seconds.
  3. A calculator in degrees on part (d). \(+0.162\) instead of \(-0.0498\): wrong sign, wrong size, completely plausible. The sign invariant catches it and nothing else will.
  4. Substituting \(x=1\) before differentiating in part (d). \(\frac36\) differentiates to \(0\), and the zero looks like an answer.
  5. Forgetting the product rule on \(v = x\sin 2x\) in part (c). Writing \(v' = 2x\cos 2x\) loses the whole \(\sin 2x\) term.
  6. The sign of \(\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos 3x)\) in part (c). It is \(+3\sin 3x\): two minus signs. \(-3\sin 3x\) is the plausible error.
  7. Reading \(\csc^2(2x)\) as \(\csc\left(4x^2\right)\), or the reverse. It changes which rule pass 2 needs, so it is not a notation slip, it is a wrong method.
  8. Differentiating an untidied \(f'\). Not wrong, just four times the work and four times the risk.
  9. Dropping the inner derivative \((2x-3)\) in part (b). Q8.1's trap 1, still the most common error in the course, in its easiest costume here.
  10. Writing \(\frac{d}{dx}\sec u = \sec^2 u\). That is \(\tan\)'s derivative. \(\sec\) gives a product and \(\tan\) gives a square, and confusing them changes the shape of the answer.
  11. Using \(h\) too small on the second-difference check. \(h=10^{-5}\) gives zero correct figures, so a right answer looks wrong and you "fix" it.
  12. Expanding \(\left(x\sin 2x\right)^2\) in part (c). It gains nothing, and x*sin(2*x)^2 on Möbius is a different function.
  13. Simplifying \(f''\) in part (a) at all. The two \(\csc\) factors have already combined; anything further is pure risk.

In all nine it was the check or the claim that was broken, not a derivative. That is now eight runs in a row in this series where every first-run failure was a verifier defect. Three of the nine were one disease in three costumes: a numerical method used outside the regime where it is valid, once at a fixed step size on a third difference and once as a tolerance tighter than a method's own floor.

Two were invented numbers wearing the costume of measurements. A pole screen demanded that a denominator stay above 0.15, a threshold tied to nothing the method needs; replacing it with a bound derived from the largest step any arm takes exposed four genuinely bad sample points that the guess had waved through. And the claim that "\(h=0.01\) is right and smaller is worse" was written before the measurement, which then refuted it: the table in section 05 is the corrected version.

Two more were gates that could not fail, one ending in and True and one comparing a value to itself. Both would have passed forever. The remaining two were plain slips, a wrong constant in a reconstruction and the backwards concavity claim in F10, and both were caught by arms that do not share code with the thing they check.
