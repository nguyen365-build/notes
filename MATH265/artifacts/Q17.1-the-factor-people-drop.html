The Factor People Drop

Bounding \(\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx\) takes three numbers: the smallest value of the integrand, the largest, and the width of the interval. Almost everyone gets the first two. This page is about the third, and about why on this particular integral forgetting it produces an answer that still looks right.

01The question and the answer

Final Exam 1, item 9 (5 points). "Use the properties of the integral to find an interval where the value of the integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx\) is located."

$$\frac{\pi}{3} \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx \;\le\; \frac{2\pi}{3}$$

that is, the interval \(\left[\tfrac{\pi}{3},\,\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right] \approx [1.0471975512,\ 2.0943951024]\).

02Why the property is true: two rectangles and an area

An integral of a positive function is the area under its graph. If the graph never dips below the horizontal line \(y=m\) and never rises above \(y=M\), then the area under it must be at least the area of the short rectangle and at most the area of the tall one. Both rectangles sit on the same base, of width \(b-a\). That is the entire content of the comparison property.

the comparison property

If \(m \le f(x) \le M\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\), then \(m\,(b-a) \le \displaystyle\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \le M\,(b-a)\).

Read the picture as a claim about area, not about the function. The bound does not say the function is nearly constant; it says the area is somewhere between the two boxes. When the function varies a lot the boxes are far apart and the bound is loose, and a loose bound is still a correct bound.

03The procedure, in five steps

1
State the domain. Write down where the integrand is undefined and check that no such point lies in \([a,b]\). Here \(\sec x = 1/\cos x\) fails exactly where \(\cos x = 0\), that is at \(x = \pi/2 + k\pi\). The nearest is \(\pi/2 = 1.5707963268\), and \(\pi/3 = 1.0471975512\), so the clearance is \(\pi/6\) and the integrand is continuous on the whole closed interval.
2
Find \(m\) and \(M\) by the Q15 recipe. Differentiate, solve \(f' = 0\) on the open interval, separately list every point where \(f'\) fails to exist, then evaluate \(f\) at those points and at both endpoints. Here \(f'(x) = \sec x\tan x\), which vanishes only where \(\tan x = 0\), i.e. at \(x=0\), an endpoint; and \(f'\) exists everywhere on \([0,\pi/3]\). So the candidate list is just \(\{0,\ \pi/3\}\), giving \(m = \sec 0 = 1\) and \(M = \sec(\pi/3) = 2\).
3
Compute \(b-a\) and write it on its own line. \(b - a = \pi/3 - 0 = \pi/3\). This step exists only so you cannot skip it. It is the step the whole question is built around.
4
Assemble. \(m(b-a) \le I \le M(b-a)\), so \(1\cdot\frac{\pi}{3} \le I \le 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{3}\), i.e. \(\pi/3 \le I \le 2\pi/3\). Keep \(\pi\) symbolic.
5
Check. The bounds are in order, the width is \((M-m)(b-a)\), and any other estimate you can make must land inside. The midpoint rule gives \(\sec(\pi/6)\cdot\pi/3 = 1.2091995762\), which does.

Step 2 is where the cost varies, and it varies with the shape of the integrand rather than with how complicated its formula looks.

04The exam fact that halves the work on Final Exam 2

On the second final, this category is welded to the extreme-value category. Two of the twelve items have a part (a) that asks for absolute extremes and a part (b) that asks you to bound an integral, and both times it is the same function over the same interval.

On the other two the wrong interval fails to contain the value, so a student who happens to know the answer catches it. Here it does not. The wrong interval \([1,2]\) is not even a subset of the right one, because \(1 < \pi/3\), and it still covers the truth. The only defence is procedural rather than numerical: make step 3 a line you physically write down.

06A wrong \(m\) or \(M\) usually still gives a correct interval

This is the deeper version of the same problem, and it is worth understanding because it changes what "checking your work" can mean. Eighteen plausible mistakes were enumerated and each one's interval was tested against the true value.

6

4
rows with an invalid m or M that STILL contain it
8
rows actually caught by the containment test

The reason is structural. Loosening a correct bound can never break containment. Any \(m' \le 1\) and any \(M' \ge 2\) still produce a true statement; that was checked across 4002 sampled loosenings with zero failures. Only tightening past the truth is detectable, and the point where that starts is exact:

the tipping point

If you take \(m = \sec(x_0)\) for some \(x_0\) in the interval, the resulting interval still contains the true value if and only if \(x_0 \le c\), where \(c = 0.6515182915\) is the Mean Value Theorem point at which \(\sec c\) equals the average value \(1.2576021548\). That is \(62.22\%\) of the way along the interval.

So taking the midpoint \(\pi/6\) as your minimum hides, and taking \(\pi/4\) is caught. A scan over 1001 choices of \(x_0\) found \(62.24\%\) of them "work", matching \(c/(b-a)\) to within one grid step. The lesson to carry into the exam: an answer that looks reasonable is not evidence that the method was right. Justify \(m\) and \(M\); do not sanity-check them.

08Splitting: how a bound becomes an estimate

Additivity lets you bound each piece of the interval separately with its own \(m_i\) and \(M_i\). For a monotone integrand cut into \(k\) equal pieces the total width of the sandwich is exactly \((M-m)(b-a)/k\), so the bound converges to the value.

09The eight families a reworded version can belong to

Read these as one structure, not as eight facts. Every family answers the same question - what are \(m\) and \(M\), and what is the width - and they differ only in how much work the first half costs.

Family 0
monotone integrand

\(f'\) has one sign. \(m\) and \(M\) are the two endpoint values; which is which depends on the sign, not on habit. \(\sec\) on \([0,\pi/3]\) is here, and so is \(\cos\) on \([0,\pi/3]\), where the ends swap.

Family 1
interior critical point

The full candidate recipe. \(x^2\) on \([-1,2]\), \(\sin\) on \([0,\pi]\), and both of the F2 paired functions live here.

Family 2
the paired F2 items

Part (a) already computed \(m\) and \(M\). Copy them. Two multiplications.

Family 3
dominating function

The wording is "show that \(I < N\)". Use monotonicity with a non-constant comparison, and verify the comparison on the whole interval first.

Family 4
splitting

"Make the estimate as accurate as you like." Width \((M-m)(b-a)/k\).

Family 5
orientation and sign

Reversed limits or a negated integrand. Negate both bounds and swap them; note that \(-\sec\) has its minimum where \(\sec\) had its maximum.

Family 6
the property does not apply

A pole inside \([a,b]\). \(\int_0^{2\pi/3}\sec x\,dx\) is improper: the partial integrals to \(\pi/2-\varepsilon\) run 5.30, 7.60, 9.90, 12.21, 14.51 as \(\varepsilon\) shrinks by decades. There is no finite \(M\), so there is nothing to say.

Family 7
the three verbs

"Interval" wants both bounds as an interval. "Estimate" wants a number or the inequality. "Bound" may want only one side. Q17.1, Q17.2 and Q17.3 use all three on the same property.

The choice of interval in Q17.1 is deliberate, and Family 6 explains it: \(\pi/3\) is the largest friendly angle that stays clear of the pole at \(\pi/2\). Had the examiner written \([0,2\pi/3]\) the question would have no answer at all.

10Before you submit

do
  • Write \(b-a\) on its own line before assembling anything.
  • Justify why \(m\) is at the end you claim, from the sign of \(f'\).
  • Say in one clause why the integrand is continuous on \([a,b]\).
  • Present the answer as an interval, because the question asked for one.
  • Type pi/3 and 2*pi/3, lower bound first.
do not
  • Assume the minimum is at the left end; that is true only for increasing \(f\).
  • Treat a plausible-looking interval as confirmation of the method.
  • Apologise for a loose bound. A correct bound can be very loose and is still correct.
  • Answer with a single number, however accurate.
  • Type 2pi/3. It is one keystroke and the whole mark.
