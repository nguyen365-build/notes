Athabasca MATH 265 · Midterm 1 · Q2 · 6 points

Composite functions and their domains

Building the composite takes fifteen seconds. The domain is the actual assessment, and it is worth about half the marks. The whole trick is that you have to work out the domain before you simplify, because simplifying erases the evidence.

The question and the two answers
Part a - f(g(x)) $$f(g(x))=2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5=\frac{-18x^{2}+200x-355}{(2x-9)^{2}}$$ Domain $$\left(-\infty,\tfrac92\right)\cup\left(\tfrac92,\infty\right)$$ Type this into Möbius 2*(x+5)^2/(2*x-9)^2-5 (-infinity,9/2)U(9/2,infinity)
Part b - g(f(x)) $$g(f(x))=\frac{\left(2x^{2}-5\right)+5}{2\left(2x^{2}-5\right)-9}=\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19}$$ Domain $$x\neq\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\approx\pm2.1794$$ Type this into Möbius 2*x^2/(4*x^2-19) (-infinity,-sqrt(19)/2)U(-sqrt(19)/2,sqrt(19)/2)U(sqrt(19)/2,infinity)
Erratum · your own note sheet is wrong on this line

Block A5 of the MATH 265 Exam Note Sheets artifact prints part b with a 59 in the denominator. It should be 19. The slip is a single dropped distribution, and it is exactly the error this question is built to catch, so do not memorise that line.

What the note sheet says $$\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-59},\quad x\neq\pm\frac{\sqrt{59}}{2}\approx\pm3.8406$$
What it actually is $$\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19},\quad x\neq\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\approx\pm2.1794$$

The arithmetic: \(2(2x^{2}-5)-9\) means multiply both terms by 2, giving \(4x^{2}-10\), and only then subtract 9, giving \(4x^{2}-19\). The independent confirmation is that the excluded inputs are the ones where \(f(x)=\frac92\), and \(2x^{2}-5=\frac92\) gives \(x^{2}=\frac{19}{4}\).

Why the typo survived. The two formulas agree at exactly one place, \(x=0\), where the numerator \(2x^{2}\) is zero and both give 0. A quick spot check at the easiest input is the one check that cannot detect it. At \(x=3\) the correct value is \(\frac{18}{17}\approx1.0588\) and the note sheet's version gives \(\frac{18}{-23}\approx-0.7826\).

00

Two gates in series

This is the whole method, and it is the same two questions every time. Think of the composite as a pipeline: the input has to survive the inner function, and then whatever comes out has to be something the outer function will accept.

Part a: \(f(g(x))\), where \(g\) runs first

\(x\)
→
Gate 1 · dom g · bites \(g\) has a denominator, so \(2x-9\neq0\) x ≠ 9/2
→
Gate 2 · dom f · vacuous \(f\) is a polynomial, it accepts every real number no condition
→
Domain one point removed, so two intervals x ≠ 9/2

Part b: \(g(f(x))\), where \(f\) runs first

\(x\)
→
Gate 1 · dom f · vacuous \(f\) is a polynomial, defined for every real \(x\) no condition
→
Gate 2 · dom g · bites \(g\) refuses the input \(\frac92\), so \(f(x)\neq\frac92\) x ≠ ±sqrt(19)/2
→
Domain two points removed, so three intervals x ≠ ±2.1794

Nothing about the two functions changed between the parts. Only the order changed, and the order is the entire question. In part a the restriction lives in the inner function; in part b it lives in the outer one and you have to pull it back through the inner formula to turn it into a condition on \(x\).

The one habit that ruins it

Do not simplify first and then read the domain off the answer. Simplifying cancels factors, and every cancelled factor is a removed point that has quietly disappeared from the page. It happens to work on this particular question, which is exactly why it feels safe and then fails on the reworded version. Sections 08's families 3 and 4 are built to punish it.

01

What it is really testing

Six points, and roughly half of them are the domains. Every reflex here is charged again later in the paper.

The one sentence that generates every correct answer

The domain of \(f\circ g\) is the set of all \(x\) that are in the domain of \(g\) and whose output \(g(x)\) is in the domain of \(f\).

Two conditions, always, in that order. Almost every wrong answer comes from checking only one of them, or from checking neither and reading the final formula instead.

The instruction says "find the composite functions and their corresponding domains". A perfect composite with no domain is half an answer. On a hand-marked paper that is three of six points. In Möbius the domain is a separate box, and an unanswered box is simply zero.

03

The universal procedure

Six steps, and they work for every composite-and-domain question on either paper.

  1. Name them. In \(f(g(x))\), \(g\) runs first and \(f\) runs second. Say it out loud, because half of all errors here are the two functions swapped.
  2. Gate 1: the inner function's own domain. Look at \(g\) alone, apply the trigger table, write the condition on \(x\).
  3. Gate 2: the outer function's requirement, pulled back. Ask what input \(f\) refuses. Then set \(g(x)\) equal to that forbidden value and solve for \(x\). Every solution is removed.
  4. Intersect. The domain satisfies both gates at once. Write interval notation and count the pieces.
  5. Only now, substitute. Replace every \(x\) in the outer function with the whole inner expression, in brackets. Simplify only as far as the question asked, and never go back to re-read the domain off this formula.
  6. Check by independent evaluation. Pick one easy \(x\) inside the domain. Compute \(g(x)\), then \(f\) of that. Compute your formula at the same \(x\). They must agree.

04

Q2 worked, and which route to actually use

Part a, the algebra

Substitute the whole of \(g\) into the single \(x\) that appears in \(f\):

$$f(g(x))=2\big(g(x)\big)^{2}-5=2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5=\frac{2(x+5)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}}-5$$

That is a complete and acceptable answer. If the box wants a single fraction, put it over the common denominator \((2x-9)^{2}\):

$$2(x+5)^{2}=2x^{2}+20x+50,\qquad 5(2x-9)^{2}=20x^{2}-180x+405$$ $$f(g(x))=\frac{\left(2x^{2}+20x+50\right)-\left(20x^{2}-180x+405\right)}{(2x-9)^{2}} =\frac{-18x^{2}+200x-355}{(2x-9)^{2}}$$

Part b, the algebra

Substitute \(f\) into every \(x\) of \(g\). There are two of them, one on top and one underneath, and both must be replaced:

$$g(f(x))=\frac{f(x)+5}{2f(x)-9}=\frac{\left(2x^{2}-5\right)+5}{2\left(2x^{2}-5\right)-9} =\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-10-9}=\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19}$$

The numerator collapses beautifully, \(-5+5=0\). That is a gift from the examiner and also a distraction: the collapse happens on top while the mark is on the bottom.

Two ways to get the domain

Route A · two gates from the definition

Apply gate 1 and gate 2 before touching the algebra, exactly as in section 00.

Works on every family in section 08, including the ones where a factor cancels or a root disappears. Costs two lines.

Use this one, always.

Route B · read the final formula

Simplify, then set the resulting denominator to zero.

Happens to give the right answer on both halves of this question, because nothing cancelled. Silently gives the wrong answer in families 3 and 4.

Fine as a cross-check. Never as the method.

For part b, Route B does give an honest second opinion: \(4x^{2}-19=0\) gives \(x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\), matching the pullback \(f(x)=\frac92\). Two independent routes to the same answer is a real check, and it is the fastest way to catch the 59-versus-19 slip.

How far to simplify part a

Leave it stacked

\(\dfrac{2(x+5)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}}-5\)

One line. Nowhere for an error to hide. Accepted by Möbius, and it is the composite function the question asked for.

Single fraction

\(\dfrac{-18x^{2}+200x-355}{(2x-9)^{2}}\)

Four lines, and two new places to drop a sign: expanding \(5(2x-9)^{2}\), and negating the whole bracket when you subtract it. Zero extra marks unless the question asked for it.

05

The exam-ready write-up

If the paper you sit asks for method, these are the six sentences that earn the marks. Copy the shape, not just the numbers.

  1. The domain of \(g\) requires \(2x-9\neq0\), so \(x\neq\dfrac92\).
  2. \(f\) is a polynomial, so \(\operatorname{dom}(f)=\mathbb{R}\) and \(g(x)\) is always an acceptable input to \(f\).
  3. Therefore \(f(g(x))=2\left(\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5\) with domain \(\left(-\infty,\dfrac92\right)\cup\left(\dfrac92,\infty\right)\).
  4. For \(g\circ f\): \(f\) is a polynomial so it imposes no restriction, but \(g\) is undefined when its input equals \(\dfrac92\).
  5. Solving \(f(x)=\dfrac92\) gives \(2x^{2}-5=\dfrac92\), hence \(x^{2}=\dfrac{19}{4}\) and \(x=\pm\dfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\); these are excluded.
  6. Therefore \(g(f(x))=\dfrac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19}\) with domain \(\left(-\infty,-\dfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)\cup\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2},\dfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right)\cup\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2},\infty\right)\).

Three things a marker looks for that students routinely skip. Naming which function is inner and which is outer. Stating explicitly that the polynomial imposes no restriction, rather than silently not mentioning it, because silence reads as "did not check". Deriving the part b exclusion from \(f(x)\neq\frac92\) rather than from the final denominator, since that is the step actually being assessed.

Verify before you commit

About fifteen seconds, and it catches every error this question can produce.

The single-point check, which is the important one

Pick an easy \(x\) inside your claimed domain and compute the composite twice, by two different roads.

\((f\circ g)(1)\)\(g(1)=-\frac67\), then \(f\!\left(-\frac67\right)=-\frac{173}{49}\)\(\frac{-18+200-355}{49}=-\frac{173}{49}\)-3.5306 ✓

Confirm each excluded point actually breaks something

An exclusion you cannot justify is an exclusion you invented.

Excluded pointWhat breaks
part a, \(x=\frac92\)the inner denominator \(2x-9\) is 0, so \(g\!\left(\frac92\right)\) does not exist and there is nothing to feed \(f\)
part b, \(x=\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\)\(f(x)=2\cdot\frac{19}{4}-5=\frac92\), which is exactly the input \(g\) refuses

The end-behaviour check, which also answers the asymptote follow-up

As \(x\to\infty\), \(g(x)\to\frac12\), so \(f(g(x))\to f\!\left(\frac12\right)=-\frac92\). The formula agrees: the leading coefficients give \(\frac{-18}{4}=-\frac92\). Going the other way, \(f(x)\to\infty\), so \(g(f(x))\to\frac12\), and the formula gives \(\frac24=\frac12\).

A shortcut worth keeping

The composite's horizontal asymptote is the outer function evaluated at the inner function's limit. You can predict it in your head before simplifying anything, which makes it a genuinely independent check rather than a restatement of the algebra.

The most expensive typo available on this question

2*x^2/4*x^2-19 parses left to right as \(\dfrac{2x^{2}}{4}\cdot x^{2}-19=\dfrac{x^{4}}{2}-19\).

At \(x=1\) that is \(-18.5\) where the true value is \(-\frac{2}{15}\approx-0.1333\); at \(x=3\) it is \(21.5\) where the true value is \(\frac{18}{17}\approx1.0588\). Nothing about the output looks like a typo, which is why it costs the whole mark. Bracket the entire denominator, every time.

Always click the preview icon. An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics. For part b these are all accepted as equivalent, so pick whichever you can type without a slip: 2*x^2/(4*x^2-19), (2*x^2)/(4*x^2-19), 2*x^2/((2*x-sqrt(19))*(2*x+sqrt(19))).

The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable. The families are organised by which gate bites and what kind of condition it produces, not by what the functions look like. Everything below was checked numerically against a direct evaluation of the composition.

How to read eleven families

It is one idea seen eleven times: gate 1, gate 2, then substitute. Only three things ever vary. Which gate is non-vacuous. Whether the condition is an exclusion (a denominator) or an inequality (a root or a log). And whether the simplified formula still remembers the restriction.

09

The trap list

Ranked by how often they actually cost marks.

  1. Reading the domain off the simplified formula. The number one error, and the whole reason the question exists. Simplification cancels factors, and each cancelled factor is a removed point that no longer appears. Families 3 and 4 are built to punish it.
  2. Botching \(2(2x^{2}-5)-9\). The 2 must distribute over both terms, giving \(4x^{2}-10\), and only then subtract 9 for \(4x^{2}-19\). Your own published note sheet gets this wrong, so do not trust a remembered answer here.
  3. Composing in the wrong order. \(f(g(x))\) means \(g\) runs first. This produces two correct functions attached to two wrong labels, which scores zero on both parts.
  4. Multiplying instead of substituting. \(f(g(x))\) is not \(f(x)\cdot g(x)\). Substitution replaces every \(x\), including the ones in a denominator, as in part b where there were two.
  5. Losing the \(\pm\). When the pullback is a square, two values come out. Part b has two exclusions and therefore three intervals.
  6. Giving the composite but not the domain. The instruction says "and their corresponding domains". In Möbius that is a separate box, and an unanswered box is zero.
  7. A decimal endpoint. \(2.179\) is not \(\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\). Exact endpoints unless asked to round.
  8. Wrong bracket at a boundary. Closed for an even root, open for a log, open for anything in a denominator. Family 6 row 1 and family 7 row 2 differ only in this.
  9. Assuming \(f\circ g=g\circ f\). They almost never agree, and this question is an explicit demonstration.
  10. Forgetting the inner function's domain when the outer looks harmless. A polynomial outer function contributes nothing, which tempts you to conclude there is no restriction at all. Gate 1 still applies. That is exactly part a.
  11. Not bracketing the denominator on entry. 2*x^2/4*x^2-19 parses as \(\frac{x^{4}}{2}-19\) and looks like a plausible answer.
  12. Cancelling a factor and calling the result the same function. \(\frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2}\) and \(x+1\) are different functions with different domains, and this course cares about the difference.
