Athabasca MATH 265 · Midterm 1 · Q2 · 6 points

Composite functions and their domains

Almost everyone gets the algebra on this question. The domain is what separates a 6 from a 4, and there is exactly one habit that protects it: write the domain down before you simplify. Simplification is the step that destroys the evidence. This is the method, all thirteen variations of it, and the fifteen-second check.

The question and the answer
Asked

Let \(f(x)=2x^2-5\) and \(g(x)=\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}\). Find the composite functions and their corresponding domains.

a. \(f(g(x))\) $$2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5,\qquad x\neq\tfrac92$$ b. \(g(f(x))\) $$\frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19},\qquad x\neq\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$$
What to type into Möbius 2*((x+5)/(2*x-9))^2-5 2*x^2/(4*x^2-19)
Excluded: 4.5  and  ±2.1794494717…
Write the second one as sqrt(19)/2, never as a decimal.
Also accepted for (a): 2*(x+5)^2/(2*x-9)^2-5

The whole method in four lines

Runnable from memory, no calculator, about thirty seconds.

f(x) = 2x^2 - 5            domain of f: ALL reals   (no denominator, no root)
g(x) = (x+5)/(2x-9)        domain of g: x != 9/2    (denominator dies at 9/2)

a) f(g(x))   substitute g into f    2*[(x+5)/(2x-9)]^2 - 5
   domain    only g can fail        x != 9/2

b) g(f(x))   substitute f into g    (2x^2-5+5) / (2(2x^2-5)-9) = 2x^2/(4x^2-19)
   domain    g must survive f(x)    4x^2-19 != 0  ->  x != +- sqrt(19)/2
The one habit

Write the domain down before you simplify. Cancellation removes a factor from the formula, but it does not remove the point from the domain. Once the factor is gone there is nothing left in the formula to remind you.

01

What this question is really testing

6 of 64 points, and the largest of the four function questions. It looks like bookkeeping. It is the rehearsal for the chain rule.

02

The four things you must have cold

2.1  The three restriction triggers

There are only three things in a first-year formula that can refuse an input. Scan for these three and nothing else.

TriggerWhat you writeWhy
A denominatorset it \(\neq 0\)division by zero is undefined
An even root \(\sqrt{\;}\), \(\sqrt[4]{\;}\)radicand \(\geq 0\)no real even root of a negative
A logarithmargument \(\gt 0\)\(\ln\) of zero or a negative is undefined

Two things that are not triggers, and cost people time every year: odd roots accept everything, so \(\sqrt[3]{x-7}\) has domain \(\mathbb{R}\); and polynomials accept everything, which is what makes part (a) short.

Note the strictness difference. A square root allows the boundary \((\geq)\), a logarithm does not \((\gt)\). That single character is worth a mark on its own, and Family 8 makes the contrast explicit.

2.2  The composition, and the order convention

$$(f\circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$$

Read it right to left: \(g\) runs first, \(f\) runs second. The notation \(f\circ g\) is pronounced “\(f\) after \(g\)”, which is the mnemonic that keeps the order straight. Mechanically: take the formula for \(f\) and replace every \(x\) in it by the whole expression \(g(x)\), brackets and all.

2.3  The domain rule, in one line

$$\text{Domain of } f(g(x)) \;=\; \{\,x \;:\; x\in D_g \;\textbf{ and }\; g(x)\in D_f \,\}$$
The half everyone gets wrong

\(f\)'s restriction does not apply to \(x\). It applies to \(g(x)\), the thing \(f\) is actually being handed.

In part (b), \(g\) refuses the input \(9/2\), and \(g\) is being handed \(f(x)\). So the thing to exclude is not “\(x=9/2\)” but “\(f(x)=9/2\)”, which is a different equation with different answers.

2.4  The domains of the two given functions

FunctionFormulaTriggersDomain
f\(2x^2-5\)none, it is a polynomial\(\mathbb{R}\)
g\(\dfrac{x+5}{2x-9}\)one denominator\(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}\)

\(D_f=\mathbb{R}\) is the single most useful fact in the whole question. It makes condition 2 vacuous in part (a) and condition 1 vacuous in part (b). Each part is therefore only half a domain problem, which is why 6 points covers both.

03

The universal procedure

  1. Write \(D_f\) and \(D_g\) from the raw formulas, before composing. Scan for the three triggers.
  2. Write the two conditions for the composite you were asked for, in words, before doing any algebra.
  3. Solve condition 1: the inner function's own restriction.
  4. Solve condition 2: put the inner formula into the outer's trigger and solve.
  5. Now, and only now, substitute and simplify.
  6. State the domain from steps 3 and 4. Never re-derive it from the simplified formula.

Step 6 is the whole discipline. Steps 1 to 4 cost about fifteen seconds and they are insurance against the one error worth the most marks.

Classify before you compute

One glance at the two functions tells you how much work each part is.

Part (a) and part (b) are deliberately one row apart. The examiner is checking that you know which half of the rule is doing the work in each.

04

Q2 worked four ways

Route A  ·  domain first, then algebra

Part a. \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\) and \(D_g=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}\). Condition 1 gives \(2x-9\neq0\), so \(x\neq\frac92\). Condition 2 is automatic, because \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\) accepts every output \(g\) can produce. Only then substitute:

$$f(g(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^{2}-5 = \frac{2(x+5)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}}-5$$
Domain of f(g(x))  ·  one puncture

Part b. Condition 1 is automatic since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\). Condition 2 says \(f(x)\neq\frac92\), so solve the equality and exclude its solutions:

$$2x^2-5=\frac92 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x^2=\frac{19}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; x^2=\frac{19}{4} \;\Longrightarrow\; x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}$$

Then substitute, replacing every \(x\) in \(g\) by \(2x^2-5\):

$$g(f(x)) = \frac{(2x^{2}-5)+5}{2(2x^{2}-5)-9} = \frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-10-9} = \frac{2x^{2}}{4x^{2}-19}$$
Domain of g(f(x))  ·  two punctures, and they are irrational
Watch the numerator collapse

\((2x^2-5)+5 = 2x^2\) exactly. That is not a coincidence, it is the examiner rewarding you for substituting carefully instead of expanding blindly.

Route B  ·  simplify first, read the domain off the result

Combining part (a) over a common denominator gives a single fraction:

$$f(g(x)) = \frac{2(x+5)^{2}-5(2x-9)^{2}}{(2x-9)^{2}} = \frac{-18x^{2}+200x-355}{(2x-9)^{2}}$$

The denominator is visible, so you read off \(x\neq9/2\) and you are right. Part (b) works the same way.

Right answer, bad habit

This route is correct here only because nothing cancelled. Apply it to \(g(g(x))\), built from this question's own \(g\), and it silently loses an excluded value. See Family 3. A method that is correct by luck is not a method.

Also: combining into a single fraction is not required. The question says “find the composite functions”, not “simplify”. It costs about forty seconds and adds a sign-error opportunity in \(-5(2x-9)^2\), and it buys nothing.

Route C  ·  the formal set-builder

$$D_{f\circ g}=\{x\in D_g : g(x)\in D_f\}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\tfrac92\}$$ $$D_{g\circ f}=\{x\in D_f : f(x)\in D_g\}=\left\{x : 2x^2-5\neq\tfrac92\right\}=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\pm\tfrac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\}$$

This is the definition written out, and it is what earns method marks on a hand-graded paper. Use Route A to think and Route C to write.

Route D  ·  the ten-second cross-check

For part (b) there are two independent ways to the excluded values, and they must agree.

RouteReasoningGives
D1 conceptual\(g\) dies at input \(9/2\), so exclude \(x\) where \(f(x)=9/2\)\(x^2=19/4\)
D2 mechanicalset the simplified denominator to zero: \(4x^2-19=0\)\(x^2=19/4\)

Two genuinely different derivations landing on the same number is a check. Re-reading your own algebra is not.

Which route, and why

A · domain first

Fast, and immune to the cancellation failure. You need \(D_f\) and \(D_g\) anyway to substitute, so it costs nothing extra. Use this one under pressure.

B · simplify first

Fastest, and silently wrong whenever a factor cancels. Never adopt it as a habit.

C · set-builder

Always right, slower to write. Reach for it only if the paper asks you to justify.

D · cross-check

Ten seconds. Always run it on the part with the harder exclusion.

05

The exam-ready write-up

If the paper asks for justification, these are the sentences that earn the method marks.

Part a

\(f\) is a polynomial, so \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\); \(g\) is rational with \(D_g=\mathbb{R}\setminus\{9/2\}\). Substituting, \(f(g(x)) = 2\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2-5\). Since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\), every output of \(g\) is acceptable to \(f\), so the only restriction is \(g\)'s own: \(2x-9\neq0\). Hence the domain is \(\left(-\infty,\frac92\right)\cup\left(\frac92,\infty\right)\).

Part b

Substituting, \(g(f(x)) = \frac{(2x^2-5)+5}{2(2x^2-5)-9} = \frac{2x^2}{4x^2-19}\). Since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\), \(x\) is unrestricted by \(f\); but \(g\) requires its input \(f(x)\neq\frac92\), and \(2x^2-5=\frac92\) gives \(x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\). Hence the domain is \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\left\{\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\right\}\).

The two phrases actually being marked are “since \(D_f=\mathbb{R}\)” and “\(g\) requires its input \(f(x)\neq9/2\)”. The first shows you knew which condition was vacuous and why. The second shows you applied the restriction to \(g\)'s input rather than to \(x\).

The most expensive typo on this question

(x+5)/(2*x-9)^2 squares only the denominator, giving \(\frac{x+5}{(2x-9)^2}\) instead of \(\left(\frac{x+5}{2x-9}\right)^2\). Bracket the entire fraction before raising it to a power.

Row 1 is built from this question's own \(g\), composed with itself, which makes it the single most likely follow-up the examiner can ask. Work it once:

$$g(g(x))=\frac{\frac{x+5}{2x-9}+5}{2\cdot\frac{x+5}{2x-9}-9} =\frac{\frac{(x+5)+5(2x-9)}{2x-9}}{\frac{2(x+5)-9(2x-9)}{2x-9}} =\frac{11x-40}{91-16x}$$

The \((2x-9)\) cancels top and bottom, and with it goes every trace of \(x=9/2\).

What the simplified formula shows you
The truth
The phantom value

Substituting \(x=9/2\) into \(\frac{11x-40}{91-16x}\) gives a perfectly innocent \(\frac{9.5}{19}=\frac12\). That \(\frac12\) is a lie. It is exactly \(g\)'s horizontal asymptote, the one value \(g\) can never output, appearing as a phantom because the cancellation erased the fact that \(g(9/2)\) does not exist.

Row 3 is the purest demonstration: \(\frac{1}{1/x}\) simplifies to \(x\), the identity function, whose formula suggests domain \(\mathbb{R}\). The true domain is \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\).

The rule this family teaches: cancellation removes a factor from the formula but it does not remove the point from the domain, so the domain must be recorded before the cancellation happens.

Family 11The range of the compositetool: push the inner's output set through

Trigger the question says range, not domain. The hardest form this question can take, and listed as a variation in the course notes.

\(f(g(x))\) has range \([-5,\infty)\)

\(g\) is a Möbius transformation with horizontal asymptote \(y=\frac12\), so it produces every real value except \(\frac12\). Squaring that set gives \([0,\infty)\) entirely, because although \(g\) never outputs \(+\frac12\), it does output \(-\frac12\) at \(x=-\frac14\), and \(\left(-\frac12\right)^2=\frac14\) is recovered. So \(2g^2-5\) ranges over \([-5,\infty)\), with the minimum \(-5\) attained at \(x=-5\) where \(g=0\).

An even power heals a one-sided gap

Verified explicitly: \(g(-\frac14)=-\frac12\) and \(f(g(-\frac14))=-4.5\), so there is no hole at \(-4.5\) in the range. The naive answer excludes it. The naive answer is wrong.

\(g(f(x))\) has range \((-\infty,0]\cup\left(\frac12,\infty\right)\)

Write \(t=x^2\geq0\), so the composite is \(h(t)=\frac{2t}{4t-19}\) with \(h'(t)=\frac{-38}{(4t-19)^2}\lt0\), decreasing on each branch. On \(t\in[0,\frac{19}{4})\) it runs from \(h(0)=0\) down to \(-\infty\). On \(t\in(\frac{19}{4},\infty)\) it runs from \(+\infty\) down towards \(\frac12\) without reaching it. The value \(\frac12\) is unattainable because \(\frac{2x^2}{4x^2-19}=\frac12\) reduces to \(0=-19\).

The last two rows are the ones being tested. “Does not exist” is a correct answer, and the question is checking whether you evaluate blindly or check the domain first.

09

The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks, worst first.

  1. Reading the domain off the simplified formula. The cancellation destroyed the evidence. This is the only error in the list that can cost the whole domain mark on a part you otherwise did perfectly.
  2. Applying the outer's restriction to \(x\) instead of to the inner formula. In part (b), writing “\(2x-9\neq0\) so \(x\neq\frac92\)” is the classic. \(g\)'s denominator is \(2\cdot(\text{its input})-9\), and its input is \(f(x)\).
  3. Dropping the \(\pm\). Part (b)'s exclusion came from \(x^2=\frac{19}{4}\), which has two roots.
  4. Composing in the wrong order. If your part (a) answer has \(x^2\) in the numerator, you did part (b).
  5. Giving a decimal where the answer is irrational. \(\pm\frac{\sqrt{19}}{2}\), not \(\pm2.179\).
  6. Bracket errors when squaring a fraction. Typing (x+5)/(2*x-9)^2 squares only the bottom.
  7. Wrong bracket type on an interval boundary. A numerator zero under a root is included; a denominator zero is excluded, even under “\(\geq\)”. A log boundary is always excluded.
  8. Arithmetic in the substitution. \(2(2x^2-5)-9 = 4x^2-19\), not \(4x^2-1\) and not \(4x^2-14\).
  9. Inventing a restriction that is not there. \(\frac{1}{x^2+1}\) has domain \(\mathbb{R}\).
  10. Forgetting that the answer can be “no”. “Does not exist” is a valid final answer.

Drill set

Twelve problems, one per family. Score domain separately from formula: a wrong formula means slow down on the substitution, a wrong domain means you skipped step 1.

Three of these are deliberately nasty

Numbers 3 and 7 are Family 3, where the simplified formula lies about the domain. Number 10 stacks two triggers, so the boundary is strict rather than closed: the radicand needs \(4x+1\geq0\) but the denominator needs \(\sqrt{4x+1}\neq0\), and together those give \(4x+1\gt0\).

If you got the formula right and the domain wrong on more than two, the fix is not more algebra practice. It is mechanical: write \(D_f\) and \(D_g\) as the first line of every answer.
