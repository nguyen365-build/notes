The one question that decides all four parts

If I substituted a number for \(x\), what is the last arithmetic operation I would perform? That operation names the rule, and it names it without you having to recognise anything. A division at the top means the quotient rule. A bracket raised to a power means the chain rule. Then you ask the same question again about each piece the rule hands you, and you stop when a piece is \(x^n\) or a constant.

Everything else on this question is bookkeeping around that one test.

Step 1. Ask what the last operation is. Read the rule off the table above.

Step 2. Write the rule down with blanks, before you fill anything in. For part (b), put \(\frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) on the page first, then name \(u\) and \(v\). Committing to the order before the algebra distracts you is what stops the quotient rule coming out backwards.

Step 3. Recurse. Ask step 1 again about each piece the rule handed you, and stop when a piece is \(x^n\) or a constant.

Step 4. Assemble, and do not touch the algebra. At this point the question is answered and the marks are earned.

Step 5. Simplify only if one factor obviously cancels. Section 05 gives the policy.

Step 6. Check with a difference quotient. Eight seconds, section 06.

The product rule is the one gap, and it is worth noticing

Q8.1 tests the power, sum, quotient and chain rules and not the product rule. It appears instead in Q8.3a, Q8.4a and the review question \(\frac{d}{dx}\,x\cos\sqrt{x-3}\). So drilling only from Q8.1 drills three quarters of the rule set. Family 6 in section 08 closes that gap deliberately.

04Part b, four ways

This is the only part with a real decision in it, so it is the one worth working several ways. All four routes were evaluated numerically at 14 points and agree with each other and with the true derivative to better than \(5\times10^{-16}\) relative.

Route A · the quotient rule, straight

Last operation is a division, so \(\frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\) with \(u = 2x-16\) and \(v = (x+3)^2\). Then \(u'=2\), and \(v' = 2(x+3)\) by the chain rule.

$$y' = \frac{2(x+3)^2 - (2x-16)\cdot 2(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}$$

That line is the answer and it is worth full marks. Now the one simplification worth doing: both numerator terms carry a factor \((x+3)\), so it cancels against the denominator.

$$y' = \frac{2(x+3)-2(2x-16)}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{2x+6-4x+32}{(x+3)^3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

Route B · the product rule on a negative power

Rewrite \(y = (2x-16)(x+3)^{-2}\), turning the division into a multiplication.

$$y' = 2(x+3)^{-2} - 2(2x-16)(x+3)^{-3} = (x+3)^{-3}\big[2(x+3)-2(2x-16)\big] = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

Route C · split the fraction first

Since \(2x-16 = 2(x+3) - 22\), the whole quotient collapses into two power-rule terms.

$$y = 2(x+3)^{-1} - 22(x+3)^{-2} \;\Longrightarrow\; y' = -2(x+3)^{-2} + 44(x+3)^{-3} = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}$$

Route D · the one formula worth memorising

For any quotient over a power, the cancellation is structural rather than lucky, because both numerator terms carry \(w^{n-1}\):

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{u}{w^{\,n}} = \frac{u'w^{\,n} - u\cdot n\,w^{\,n-1}w'}{w^{2n}} = \frac{u'w - n\,u\,w'}{w^{\,n+1}}$$

With \(u = 2x-16\), \(w = x+3\), \(n=2\), that is \(\dfrac{2(x+3)-2(2x-16)}{(x+3)^3}\) in one line. The formula was verified over 144 combinations of \((a,b,c,n)\) at five points each, so it is a family fact and not one coincidence.

Route C is the interesting one to understand and the wrong one to use here: its real value is on integrals, where splitting is not optional.

05How much simplification a marker expects

The policy, and it is the category's policy not this question's

Simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk. A correct unsimplified derivative is worth full marks, because "you may not need to simplify your answers" is printed on the paper. A correct derivative that you then simplify incorrectly is worth fewer. So the default is to stop the moment the derivative is written.

All four Q8 items print that same clause, which is why this is a property of the whole block.

The full-credit write-up

a. By the sum and constant-multiple rules, differentiating term by term with \(\frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}\), and noting the constant \(6\) differentiates to \(0\): \(\;y' = 20x^4 + 12x^3 - 18x^2\).

b. The outermost operation is a division, so this is the quotient rule with \(u = 2x-16\) and \(v = (x+3)^2\). Then \(u'=2\), and \(v' = 2(x+3)\) by the chain rule, since \(v\) is a bracket raised to a constant power with inner derivative \(1\). So \(y' = \frac{2(x+3)^2 - (2x-16)\cdot2(x+3)}{(x+3)^4}\). Both numerator terms share a factor \((x+3)\), so one power cancels: \(y' = \frac{38-2x}{(x+3)^3}\), for \(x\neq-3\).

c. The outermost operation is \(\sin\) applied to a polynomial, so this is the chain rule with \(f=\sin\) and \(g(x)=2x^2-x+1\). The outer derivative is \(\cos\), evaluated at the unchanged inner, and \(g'(x)=4x-1\): \(\;y' = (4x-1)\cos\left(2x^2-x+1\right)\).

d. The outermost operation is a fourth power of a polynomial, so this is the chain rule in its generalized-power form \(\frac{d}{dx}[g]^n = n[g]^{n-1}g'\), with \(g' = -12x^2-2x+3\): \(\;y' = 4\left(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7\right)^3\left(-12x^2-2x+3\right)\).

Naming the rule in each part costs one clause, and it is exactly what Q8.4 asks for explicitly. Getting into the habit here means Q8.4 needs no extra work at all.

06The eight-second check

Pick \(x=1\), or any value that is easy and not near a pole. Compute \(\dfrac{f(1.001)-f(0.999)}{0.002}\) on the calculator and compare it with your \(y'(1)\). Agreement to three or four significant figures means your derivative is right.

Because the quotient is centred, its error falls like \(h^2\) rather than \(h\), so \(h=0.001\) already gives five good digits. Here is what it produces on all four parts, every row verified.

What it catches, seeded and confirmed

Drop the inner derivative in part (d) and you get \(4\cdot5^3 = 500\) instead of \(-5500\). Write the quotient rule backwards in part (b) and you get \(-0.5625\) instead of \(+0.5625\). Leave the calculator in degrees on part (c) and you are wrong by a factor of about 6.6. Each of those three failures was seeded into the verification and confirmed to be rejected.

What it does not catch is method. It confirms the answer, not the route, so on a question that says "show your work" it is a filter and not a proof.

The single most expensive typo on this question

(38-2*x)/(x+3)^3 is right. (38-2*x)/(x+3^3) cubes the 3 instead of the bracket and is silently a completely different function. The preview shows the difference immediately, which is the entire reason to use it.

A genuine safety valve

Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence, not by string match, so unsimplified answers are accepted. If you are unsure of your cancellation on part (b), enter the raw form (2*(x+3)^2-(2*x-16)*2*(x+3))/(x+3)^4 and it marks correct.

Row 3 pays a bonus: \(f''(19) < 0\), which confirms by the second-derivative test that the critical point at \(x=19\) is a maximum.

09The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

  1. Dropping the inner derivative in the chain rule. Writing \(\cos(2x^2-x+1)\) for part (c), or \(4(-4x^3-x^2+3x+7)^3\) for part (d). This is the number one mark-loser in the whole course, and the check in section 06 catches it in one second.
  2. The quotient rule written backwards. \(uv'-u'v\) instead of \(u'v-uv'\). It flips the sign of the whole answer and looks completely plausible. The guard is step 2: write the rule with blanks before you fill it in.
  3. A sign slip distributing the minus. \(-2(2x-16) = -4x+32\), not \(-4x-32\).
  4. Taking \(v'=1\) instead of \(2(x+3)\). This is trap 1 wearing a different hat: the denominator is a composite and it needs the chain rule too.
  5. Expanding part (d). Not wrong, just expensive. Four minutes, twelve terms, no marks, and every term is a chance to slip.
  6. Leaving \((x+3)^4\) after cancelling the numerator. Half a cancellation is worse than none, because the two halves no longer describe the same function.
  7. Differentiating the base inside the bracket in part (d). The outer derivative leaves the inner completely untouched. This is trap 1 seen from the other side.
  8. Calculator in degrees on part (c). The error factor is about 6.6 and the check catches it immediately.
  9. Writing \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin = -\cos\). The minus belongs to \(\cos\), not to \(\sin\). The "co-functions get the minus" rule generates all six signs correctly.
  10. Missing brackets on entry. (38-2*x)/(x+3)^3 versus (38-2*x)/(x+3^3). Preview catches this and nothing else will.
  11. Reaching for the product rule on \((x+3)(x+3)\). It works and it wastes thirty seconds.
  12. Simplifying part (c) or part (d) at all. There is nothing there to simplify, so any algebra you do is pure risk.

11The one-page decision tree

"Compute the derivative of y = <expression>."
|
+-- STEP 1.  If I substituted a number, what would I do LAST?
|      + or -            -> do each term separately, then ask again
|      x by a constant   -> pull the constant out, then ask again
|      x between two     -> PRODUCT   u'v + uv'    (symmetric, no order risk)
|         x-factors
|      /                 -> QUOTIENT  (u'v - uv')/v^2   ORDER MATTERS
|      ( )^n             -> CHAIN     n( )^(n-1) * (inner)'
|      sin/cos/tan/sqrt  -> CHAIN     outer'(inner) * (inner)'
|      nothing left      -> POWER     n x^(n-1)   or   0
|
+-- STEP 2.  Write the rule with BLANKS first.  Then name u and v.
|            This is what stops the quotient rule coming out backwards.
|
+-- STEP 3.  Ask step 1 again about each piece.  Stop at x^n or a constant.
|
+-- STEP 4.  Assemble.  The question is now answered and the marks are earned.
|
+-- STEP 5.  Simplify ONLY if one factor cancels top and bottom.
|      quotient over a power  -> YES, one power of the base comes out,
|                               and the denominator goes 2n -> n+1
|      a bracket to a power   -> NO.  Never expand.
|      a trig chain result    -> NO.  There is nothing to do.
|      a SECOND derivative    -> YES, always tidy f' before differentiating
|
+-- STEP 6.  CHECK.  On the calculator:  [f(1.001) - f(0.999)] / 0.002
             Compare with your y'(1).  Three matching figures is enough.

      SHAPE     a polynomial's degree drops by exactly 1
      POLES     a pole's order RISES by exactly 1
      INNER     the inside of a bracket or trig function is UNCHANGED
      CHAIN     is the inner derivative actually written down?  Look.
      ORDER     quotient rule numerator starts with u', not u

How every number here was checked

276 checks, 0 failures, 20 of them seeded controls

Every derivative was checked by two independent numerical methods: a complex-step derivative \(\operatorname{Im}f(x+ih)/h\) at \(h=10^{-30}\), which has no subtractive cancellation, and a Richardson-extrapolated central difference over a ladder in \(h\). Agreement is better than \(10^{-9}\) relative on the first and \(10^{-7}\) on the second, at 6 to 14 sample points per function.

Every simplification was separately checked as an identity, raw form against tidy form at \(10^{-12}\) relative, because in this family most lost marks come from the algebra after the differentiation. A gate never seen to bite is indistinguishable from a gate that is not there.
