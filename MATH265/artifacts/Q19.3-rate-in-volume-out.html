MATH 265 · FINAL EXAM 1 · ITEM 12 · 3 POINTS · Q19.3

Rate In, Volume Out

A rate tells you how fast something is changing. Integrate it and you get the change in the thing itself. That is the whole question, and it takes one line. The trap is that this question's numbers make three different questions have the same answer, so nothing you can compute will tell you which one you actually answered.

THE QUESTION

Water leaves a tank at $r(t)=180-6t$ L/min. How much leaves in the first 15 minutes?

THE ANSWER

$\displaystyle\int_0^{15}\!(180-6t)\,dt = 2025$ L

TYPE INTO MÖBIUS

2025

01 The theorem, in one sentence

If a quantity $Q$ changes at rate $r$, so that $Q'=r$, then

$$\int_a^b r(t)\,dt \;=\; Q(b)-Q(a)$$

The integral of a rate of change is the net change in the quantity. This is called the Net Change Theorem, and it is not a new theorem. It is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2, with the letters renamed: given a function and an antiderivative, the definite integral is the antiderivative's change across the interval. The only thing this question adds is a physical reading. When $r$ is a flow rate, the antiderivative is a volume.

You can watch that happen in the units, and the units are the cheapest check on the whole page.

Do this as a cancellation, not as a sentence. Writing "the answer is in litres because it is water" is not a check, because it stays true whatever you computed. Writing $\frac{\text{L}}{\text{min}}\times\text{min}=\text{L}$ is a check, because a wrong operation produces a wrong unit. In the verification behind this page the units are represented as exponent vectors over litre and minute, and the gate was proved to reject L/min, L$\cdot$min, min, min/L and the meaningless sum L + L/min before its acceptance of L was trusted.

02 Four lines, and you are done

  1. Name the quantity. $V$ is the volume that has flowed out, in litres, and $r=V'$. If you cannot name the quantity, you do not yet know what the integral means.
  2. Antidifferentiate. $R(t)=180t-3t^2$. Check it by differentiating back: $R'(t)=180-6t$, which is $r$. The check is faster than the antidifferentiation.
  3. Evaluate upper minus lower. $R(15)-R(0)=(2700-675)-0=2025$.
  4. State the units and check the magnitude. 2025 litres, and $135\times15=2025$ agrees with the average-rate shortcut.

The picture below is the same statement drawn. The height of the curve is the rate; the area under it is the volume. The shaded amber region is the answer.

03 Change, final amount, or total moved

Four different things could be asked about this tank, and they are four different numbers on a general question. Knowing which one the wording is asking for is where the mark actually lives.

The wordingWhat to computeNeeds the starting volume?
"the amount that flows from the tank"$\int_0^{15} r$no, and this is the exam question
"the net change in the volume in the tank"$-\int_0^{15} r$no
"how much water remains in the tank"$V(0)-\int_0^{15} r$yes, and this is the classic lost mark
"the total volume of water moved"$\int_0^{15}\lvert r\rvert$no

The integral is a change, not an amount. It does not know how much water was in the tank to begin with, and it cannot tell you that you needed to know. If the question says "how much is left", the integral is half the answer and the starting volume is the other half.

Q19.3 does not ask that, which is exactly why the mark is so easy to lose later. You learn the shape on a question where the starting volume is absent, and then meet one where it is not.

The sign convention, decided before you integrate

Here $r$ is defined as the rate at which water flows from the tank, so $r>0$ means water leaving. Two consistent bookkeepings exist and you must pick one and stay in it.

Both are correct statements about the same event. The exam wants the first, because it asks for an amount of water, and an amount of water is positive. Answering $-2025$ is answering a different, unasked question correctly.

04 Why net change and total moved are the same here

They differ only when the rate changes sign inside the interval. That is the whole condition, and it is worth knowing because it is the one thing this question cannot teach you by example.

IntervalNet change $\int r$Total moved $\int\lvert r\rvert$Do they agree?
$[0,15]$, the exam question2025 L2025 Lyes, no sign change inside
$[0,30]$, to the shut-off2700 L2700 Lyes, the zero is an endpoint

Read the two number columns as a pair rather than as eight facts. The first row is the exam question, the columns agree, and that is the hiding point: on this question there is no computation you can do that would reveal you had confused the two.

Notice the third row. The integral over $[0,50]$ is smaller than the integral over $[0,15]$, despite covering more than three times as much time, because the model runs backwards after $t=30$. That is a genuinely counter-intuitive number and it is worth knowing it exists.

The midpoint shortcut hides because $r$ is linear, so it is genuinely valid here and is not really an error at all until the rate curves. The omitted lower limit hides because $R(0)=0$ exactly. Both are properties of this question's particular numbers, and both disappear the moment the interval does not start at zero or the rate is not a straight line.

Three values are each produced by two different routes: 1350, 2025 and 2700. Eleven of the sixteen wrong answers are positive, so a positivity check rejects fewer than a third of them, and seven of the sixteen lie between half and double the correct answer, so a rough plausibility check is not much better.

06 Q19.3 rewards the habit that Q19.2 punishes

This is the pairing worth carrying away, and it is measured rather than asserted.

Q19.1 · AREA UNDER A PARABOLA

Three separate mistakes all evaluate to $-4.5$ where the answer is $4.5$. The magnitude is right and only the sign is wrong, so "an area is positive" repairs all three into the correct answer. The repair rescues you.

Q19.2 · AREA UNDER A COSINE

Three errors collide at $0.221$ against a true $0.828$. The magnitude is wrong, so the same repair converts a visibly broken answer into a confident, plausible, wrong one. The repair costs the mark.

Q19.3 · THIS QUESTION

Exactly one candidate has the right magnitude and the wrong sign, $-2025$, and "an amount of water is positive" repairs it correctly. This is the Q19.1 pattern, and more forgiving still: nothing here punishes the repair.

That is the danger. A student who learns on this question that a negative answer can simply be made positive will lose the mark on Q19.2, on Q19.8, and on any question where the flow reverses. Learn the rule here, where it is cheap. Do not learn the repair.

The rule itself is one sentence: decide which quantity you are accumulating before you integrate, not after you see the sign.

Say which root you rejected and why; that sentence is the difference between a solved equation and a solved problem.

09 Eight seconds, in the order that catches things

  1. Units. $(\text{L/min})\times\text{min}=\text{L}$. Catches answering with a rate, and catches differentiating instead of integrating. Rejects 4 of the 16.
  2. Magnitude bracket. For a decreasing positive rate the answer is strictly between $r(b)(b-a)$ and $r(a)(b-a)$, so here $1350<A<2700$. Rejects 12 of the 16, which is more than any other single check.
  3. Midpoint. $135\times15=2025$ agrees, which confirms the antiderivative's arithmetic.
  4. Sign. $r>0$ throughout $[0,15]$, so the answer is positive. Decide this before integrating, never repair it after.
  5. The check that cannot fire here. $R(0)=0$, so forgetting to subtract the lower limit costs nothing on this question and everything on an interval starting anywhere else.

Answer entry: type 2025. Not 2025 L, because units are not part of a numeric answer. Not 2,025, because a thousands separator is not a number. Not -2025. The single most expensive typo here is a leading minus, and unlike Q19.2 the fix really is to delete it, because the magnitude is already right. That is a dangerous thing to learn, which is what section 06 is about.

  • The antiderivative was differentiated back at 69 sample points by complex-step differentiation, an arm that never sees the claimed integral. A seeded wrong antiderivative, $180t-6t^2$, was rejected at 68 of the 69 points, agreeing only at $t=0$.
  • The definite integral was computed by eight independent routes, including tanh-sinh quadrature and Simpson at eight values of $n$ chosen odd and off multiples of 8.
  • The quadrature convergence order on this integrand is unmeasurable, and that is the honest report rather than a nominal 4. The integrand is a straight line and Simpson is exact on cubics, so the error is zero at every $n$ and the ratio is $0/0$. The same routine was proved able to report a real order elsewhere: $4.0222, 4.0081, 4.0025, 4.0007$ on $\sin(t^2)$, and $1.6661$ rising to $1.6667$ on $\lvert t\rvert^{2/3}$.
  • A grid that happens to align with a corner reports perfection. The function $\lvert r\rvert$ has a corner at $t=30$, exactly $3/5$ of the way across $[0,50]$. At $n=10,20,40,80,160$, all divisible by 5, the corner lands on a panel boundary and Simpson's error is exactly zero. At $n=6,12,24,48,96$ the errors are $44.4, 11.1, 2.78, 0.694, 0.174$ and the measured order is $2.0000$, not 4. The choice of $n$ has to be made deliberately, not inherited from a habit of powers of two.
  • The sign-change census was controlled to return 0, 1 and 2 on constructed cases, and was checked against a grid-alignment failure: at $n=50$ the grid lands exactly on $t=30$, and a census testing only for a change of sign between neighbours finds nothing at all.
  • Six checker defects were found and fixed during the run, and all six were the check rather than the mathematics: a quadrature routine that never halved its step, two counts typed instead of derived, a boundary comparison with no tolerance, and two defects in the claims checker itself, one of which made every sentence-final number invisible to it.
