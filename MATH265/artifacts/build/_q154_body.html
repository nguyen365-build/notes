MATH 265 · FINAL EXAM 2 · ITEM 5 · TEN POINTS, THE PAPER'S JOINT HEAVIEST

Not A Cube

Every optimization question you have met so far ended in a symmetric shape. The rectangle of least perimeter is a square; the box of least surface area is a cube. So the instinct arrives ready-made, and this question is built to punish it. The cheapest box here is 2 : 4 : 3. Worse, the cube a student reaches for is cheaper than the correct answer while being illegal, so the reflex of asking "does my number look sensible" rewards the mistake. This page is about where the symmetry went.

01 The question, and the four answers it wants

What are the dimensions of the cheapest rectangular box that can be constructed if the material for the box is 1.20 dollars per square cm and the cost for the lid is 1.50 dollars per square cm. The length of the base is twice as long as it is wide and the volume must be \(120\text{ cm}^3\)? What is the minimum cost?Final Exam 2, item 5, ten points. That is the joint heaviest item on the paper: nothing outranks it and three items tie with it. The value is printed on its own line after the item, which is this paper's convention. No "no credit" clause, no hint, no "show your work", no permission to leave an answer unsimplified. The sentence period before "The length of the base" and the question mark after the volume are the paper's own punctuation, quoted rather than repaired.

02 Where the symmetry went

A cube is not a general fact about boxes. It is what you get when two separate conditions both hold, and this stem breaks both of them.

Condition one

A FREE BASE

The base has to be allowed to become square. This stem pins \(l = 2w\), so it cannot. Drop that clause and let \(l\) float, and the same cost function really does want a square base, of side \(\sqrt[3]{288/2.7} = 4.7425244060\) at a cost of \(182.1814557052\). The clause costs \(7.2948937310\) dollars, and that is a measured number, not a rhetorical one.

Condition two

ONE RATE

Every face has to cost the same per unit area. Here the lid costs 25 percent more than everything else, so the two horizontal faces are dearer, on average, than the four vertical ones. An expensive face gets made smaller, so the box shrinks in plan and grows in height.

Write the base as \(w\) by \(l\) with \(l = kw\), let the base cost \(c_b\) per unit area, the lid \(c_t\), the sides \(c_s\), and fix the volume at \(V\). Then the whole family collapses to two formulas.

$$C(w) = (c_b+c_t)k\,w^2 + \frac{2c_s(1+k)V}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{w}, \qquad w^3 = \frac{c_s(1+k)V}{k^2(c_b+c_t)}, \qquad \frac{h}{w} = \frac{k(c_b+c_t)}{c_s(1+k)}.$$

The second formula is the size. The third is the shape, and it is the one to read. Notice what is missing from it: the volume. The proportions of the cheapest box depend on the cost ratios and on \(k\), and not at all on how big the box is. Double the volume and every dimension is multiplied by \(2^{1/3} = 1.2599\) while \(h/w\) does not move, which was checked at \(V = 1\), \(60\), \(120\), \(1000\) and \(99999\).

For this stem \(h/w = 2(2.7)/(1.2\cdot 3) = 3/2\) exactly, and \(l/w = 2\). Halve the width and the three dimensions are 2, 4 and 3.

03 Reading the rates, which is where the marks actually go

The stem gives two prices and no diagram, so the reading has to come from the words. The lid is named separately, so "the material for the box" is everything else: the base and the four sides.

This is the most expensive misreading in the question, and no numeric check catches it. Every alternative reading is a perfectly well-formed problem with a perfectly plausible answer, so nothing downstream complains. The course notes get it wrong: MATH265_Review.md's own setup line reads "Cost = (sides area x 1.20) + (lid area x 1.50)", which leaves the base unpaid and gives a width of \(4.1602\) at a cost of \(155.7630\).

What does catch it is a dimensional check, run before any calculus. A rate is dollars per square centimetre, so multiplying it by an area gives dollars and multiplying it by anything else does not.

Look at the last row. The constant \(432\) is not a plain number of dollars: it carries a length, because it arrived as a rate times an area divided by a width. That is precisely the check that tells you \(432/w\) belongs in a money sum while \(432w\) would not. In this run the gate was proved live against eight seeded rejections before it was trusted, including "cost equals rate times volume", which is the slip it exists for.

04 The cost, drawn

Two things are worth seeing rather than reading. The first is that the cost is a sum of one rising piece and one falling piece, and that at the bottom the falling piece is exactly twice the rising one. The second is that the frame has no ends: the edges are dashed because the domain runs to zero on one side and to infinity on the other, and neither is a place you can evaluate.

Panel A. The two components cross-trade: at a narrow base the four sides are enormous because the box has to be tall, and at a wide base the base and lid are enormous because they are large. The minimum is not where the two curves meet. It is where the falling one is twice the rising one, which is what the tie line marks.

The second figure is the answer against its neighbours. All six boxes hold exactly \(120\text{ cm}^3\); the five solid ones also obey \(l=2w\). The dashed one is the cube.

Panel B. The cube is cheaper, at \(182.4661\) against \(189.4763\), and it is illegal: its length equals its width where the stem demands twice. So the \(l=2w\) clause costs \(7.0102895690\) dollars, and a "my answer is cheaper, so it must be better" check rewards the wrong box. That is the single most useful sentence on this page.

05 No endpoints, so justify instead of compare

The feasible set is \((0,\infty)\), open at both ends, and the stem never prints it. You write it down yourself, and it changes the method.

Q15.1's method

COMPARE

A printed closed interval. Find the critical points, add both endpoints, evaluate everything, pick the largest and the smallest. The Extreme Value Theorem is what licenses the comparison, by guaranteeing the extremes exist in the first place.

Here

UNAVAILABLE

There is no left endpoint and no right endpoint, so the candidate table has two empty rows and the comparison cannot be run at all. Measured: \(C(10^{-9}) > 4\times 10^{11}\) and \(C(10^{9}) > 5\times 10^{18}\), monotone over eight decades in each direction.

Instead

JUSTIFY

Convexity, a sign change, or the limits at both ends. Any one of the three is enough, and the words "for all \(w>0\)" are the entire difference between proving a local minimum and proving the global one.

argumentwhat you writewhen to reach for it
convexity\(C'' = 10.8 + 864/w^3 > 0\) for every \(w>0\), so the local minimum is globalfirst choice: one line, settles everything
sign change\(C' = \dfrac{10.8(w^3-40)}{w^2}\), negative before \(\sqrt[3]{40}\) and positive afteralways available, and survives \(C''=0\)
the two ends\(C\to\infty\) as \(w\to 0^{+}\) and as \(w\to\infty\), so a minimum exists and cannot escapethe honest one: the only argument that explains why a minimum exists
three-term AM-GM\(5.4w^2 + \dfrac{216}{w} + \dfrac{216}{w} \ge 3\sqrt[3]{5.4\cdot 216^2}\), equality at \(w^3=40\)a complete global proof with no calculus at all

Two-term AM-GM does not work here. It gives \(2\sqrt{5.4\cdot 432} = 96.5981\), a true lower bound sitting at roughly half the real minimum, so it bounds nothing useful. Split the reciprocal term into \(q\) copies and the power term into \(p\) copies, and then it is exact.

The general recipe behind that last row: for \(a w^p + b w^{-q}\), use \(p\) copies of \(\tfrac{a}{p}w^p\) and \(q\) copies of \(\tfrac{b}{q}w^{-q}\). Here \(p=2\) and \(q=1\), so it is one copy of the square term and two copies of half the reciprocal term, and each of the three is \(63.1587831454\) at the answer.

06 The write-up, as it should appear on the paper

Let the base be \(w\) wide and \(l\) long and the box \(h\) tall, in cm, with \(w>0\).
The stem gives \(l=2w\) and \(wlh=120\), so \(2w^2h=120\) and \(h=60/w^2\).
The base and lid each have area \(2w^2\); the four sides have total area \(2(w+l)h = 6wh\).
Cost \(= 1.20(2w^2+6wh) + 1.50(2w^2)\), which is \(5.4w^2 + 7.2wh\), and after the height goes in \(5.4w^2 + 432/w\) dollars, for \(w>0\).
The domain is open at both ends, so there are no endpoints to test.
\(C'(w) = 10.8w - 432/w^2 = 0\) gives \(w^3 = 40\), so \(w=\sqrt[3]{40}\), the only real root and positive.
\(C''(w) = 10.8 + 864/w^3 > 0\) for all \(w>0\), so \(C\) is convex and this is the global minimum.
Then \(l = 2\sqrt[3]{40}\) and \(h = 60/w^2 = \tfrac32\sqrt[3]{40}\), and \(w\cdot l\cdot h = 120\) as required.
Dimensions \(3.42\) cm by \(6.84\) cm by \(5.13\) cm. Minimum cost \(16.2\cdot 40^{2/3} \approx 189.48\) dollars.

One step in that is worth pausing on, because it is where the clean answer comes from. Since \(w^3 = 40\) exactly, \(\dfrac{60}{w^2} = \dfrac{60w}{w^3}\), which is \(\dfrac{60w}{40}\), which is \(\dfrac{3w}{2}\), with no decimals anywhere. The height is a rational multiple of the width, so the box is \(2:4:3\) and the volume checks in integers: \(2\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 5 = 120\).

Read the last column. The volume constraint holds exactly for any width you pick, because \(h\) was defined by the constraint: \(3.42 \times 6.84 \times (60/3.42^2)\) is \(120\) on the nose. So the constraint check can only catch a dimension you recovered wrongly. It can never catch a critical point you solved wrongly.

Never validate the minimiser using the minimum, and never validate it using the constraint. Only the derivative sees a nearly-right width.

The cheap version of the derivative check is the ratio. At the minimum of \(aw^2+b/w\) the reciprocal term is exactly twice the power term, so multiply and compare.

The one that hides is the most interesting result of the run. Reading "the length of the base is twice as long as it is wide" backwards gives the base area \(w^2/2\), the height \(240/w^2\) and the objective \(1.35w^2 + 864/w\), whose minimum sits at \(w^3 = 320\). Different equation, different root, and yet the box that comes out is \(6.8399\) by \(3.4200\) by \(5.1299\): the same box, with the labels \(w\) and \(l\) exchanged. The cost is identical to twelve figures.

The reason is worth carrying to any question in this family. The cost sees the base only through its area and its perimeter, and both are symmetric in \(w\) and \(l\). So Q15.2's involution survives into three dimensions after all, and what breaks the symmetry is not the pricing but the constraint. The asymmetry that stops the answer being a cube lives between the horizontal direction and the vertical one, where \(h\) is genuinely not interchangeable with either base edge.

Two wrong models can agree with each other

For \(C = Aw^2 + B/w\) the minimiser is \((B/2A)^{1/3}\) and the minimum is \(3(AB^2/4)^{1/3}\). So the dimensions depend only on \(B/A\) and the cost depends only on \(AB^2\), and those two quantities move independently.

Same \(B/A\)

DIMENSIONS AGREE

The uniform-1.20 reading, the uniform-1.50 reading and the least-area problem all share \(B/A = 90\), so all three return the same width \(3.556893\) with three different costs. Getting the same dimensions twice proves nothing if both routes share a modelling error.

Same \(AB^2\)

COST AGREES

Omitting the lid and counting the sides as \(4wh\) share \(AB^2 = 447897.6\), so both return \(144.597517\) from two quite different widths. Two students with different wrong models can hand in the same wrong cost.

10 How every number here was checked

The constraint and the cost objective were checked against the stem's own numbers in exact rationals, before any calculus, face by face at five widths, with three alternative rate readings explicitly rejected. The dimensional gate was built as exponent vectors over centimetres and dollars and proved live against eight seeded rejections.

\(C'\) was recovered twice without ever seeing its closed form, by complex-step differentiation and by a bounded Richardson ladder whose fourth-order convergence was measured on a separate curved control, since a centred quotient differentiates the quadratic part exactly and would have measured nothing. \(C''\) got its own arm, a Richardson-extrapolated second difference, rather than being obtained by re-differentiating the claimed \(C'\).

The critical point was found four ways: bisection on \(C'\), Newton on \(C'\), a sign-change sweep that steps over an exact zero, and local minima of \(\lvert C' \rvert\) refined by golden section. The census machinery was controlled in both directions first, returning \(3\) on \(x^3-x\) at both grid parities, \(0\) on a touch zero, \(2\) on \(x^2-1\) and \(0\) on \(x^2+1\). The grid-parity trap that decided the answer on Q15.1 and Q15.3 cannot fire here, because \(\sqrt[3]{40}\) is irrational and no rational grid point lands on it, and that was asserted rather than assumed.

Global minimality was established four ways on the actual open domain: the limits at both ends over eight decades each, a sign scan of 2000 widths per side, convexity at 4000 widths over six decades, and a refined dense sweep independent of the candidate list. That sweep was judged against a derived bound, \(\sqrt{2\varepsilon C^{*}/C''}\), which is \(5.0961\times 10^{-8}\), never an invented tolerance.

Forty-four were typed decimal literals sitting beside a correct closed form, which remains by some distance the most repeated defect in this series. Thirty-four of those forty-four came from one habit and were closed by one redesign: the variation catalog originally passed a hand-predicted answer into each row's checker, so the fix was to stop passing expected values at all and let each row compute its own from the face-by-face sum and a blind sweep, print it, and lock the printed digits afterwards.

And one wrong value passed, which is the sharpest finding here: the derived resolution bound was typed as \(5.0967\times 10^{-8}\) where it is \(5.0961\times 10^{-8}\), and the assertion's absolute tolerance of \(10^{-11}\) was larger than the \(5.75\times 10^{-12}\) gap it had to discriminate.
