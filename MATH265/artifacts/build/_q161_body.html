A Search, Not a Lookup

Five integrals, and the first integration question in the bank. Everything before it was differential calculus, so this one is not testing a technique - it is testing whether you own the ladder.

02The 30-second version

Integration is a search, not a lookup. Walk the ladder from the top and stop at the first rung that fires.

RUNG 1  Can ALGEBRA make this a sum of powers?      -> power rule, term by term
        (multiply out, split the fraction, pull constants)
RUNG 2  Is the inner function LINEAR (ax+b)?         -> integrate outside, divide by a
RUNG 3  Is some inner function's DERIVATIVE present  -> u-substitution
        in the rest, up to a constant factor?
RUNG 4  Is it a trig product with no du in sight?    -> IDENTITY first, re-enter at 1

then always:   + C   (indefinite only)
then always:   DIFFERENTIATE YOUR ANSWER BACK

Q16.1's five parts are rungs 1, 4, 2, 1, 3 in order. That is not a coincidence - it is the whole ladder, laid out as one question.

04Why there is no last-operation test here

Q8.1 builds its whole rule-selection method on the last-operation test: ask what the outermost operation is, and the rule follows. It works because differentiation is structural. Q8.1 puts it plainly - the test never looks at the whole expression.

Integration has no such test, and understanding why is worth more than any single technique.

differentiation (Q8.1)integration (Q16.1)
what you inspectthe last operation only the whole integrand at once
rule for a productthe product rule does not exist
rule for a quotientthe quotient rule does not exist
rule for a compositionthe chain rule does not exist - substitution is a guess
the procedurea decision, taken once a search, with backtracking
can it fail?no yes - some functions have no elementary antiderivative

So the mirror of the last-operation test is not another one-glance test. It is the ladder, walked from the top. The question it puts first, every time, is “can algebra kill this before I reach for a technique?” - and on Q16.1 that disposes of two of the five parts.

05The five parts

(a)   $\int (x^2-x)\sqrt{3x}\,dx$   rung 1

It looks like a product. It is not a product problem. Pull the constant out of the root, then multiply out:

$$(x^2-x)\sqrt3\,x^{1/2}=\sqrt3\left(x^{5/2}-x^{3/2}\right)$$

$$\int = \frac{2\sqrt3}{7}x^{7/2}-\frac{2\sqrt3}{5}x^{5/2}+C$$

Domain. $\sqrt{3x}$ needs $x\ge0$. That restriction is real, and section 07 shows it is invisible to every check you can run in the exam.

(b)   $\int \sin(2x)\cos x\,dx$   rung 4, then 3

Both substitution candidates fail. $u=\sin 2x$ needs a $\cos 2x$ that is not there; $u=\cos x$ needs a $\sin x$, and there is a $\sin 2x$ instead. Two failures is the cue for an identity.

$$\sin 2x\cos x = 2\sin x\cos^2 x \;\Longrightarrow\; \int = -\frac{2}{3}\cos^3x+C$$

The product-to-sum route gives $-\tfrac16\cos 3x-\tfrac12\cos x+C$. Substituting $\cos 3x=4\cos^3x-3\cos x$ collapses it to the same function - the two routes differ by a constant, and here that constant is exactly zero. Verified across seven sample points to $10^{-13}$. Worth seeing once, because it is the exception: two correct antiderivatives normally differ by a non-zero constant, and both are still correct.

(c)   $\int_a^b (x+\cos 2x)\,dx$   rungs 1 and 2

A sum splits, so each term goes its own way. The second term is the $1/a$ rule with $a=2$.

$$\left[\frac{x^2}{2}+\frac{\sin 2x}{2}\right]_a^b =\frac{b^2-a^2}{2}+\frac{\sin 2b-\sin 2a}{2}$$

No $+C$ - the limits are there, so the answer is a number. Verified on six $(a,b)$ pairs including $a=b$, which gives exactly $0$, and $b<a$, which negates. Neither was excluded as degenerate.

(d)   $\int (x^2-4)^2\,dx$   rung 1

The bracket's power is 2, so expand.

$$\int\left(x^4-8x^2+16\right)dx=\frac{x^5}{5}-\frac{8x^3}{3}+16x+C$$

The trap this part exists to punish is running the chain rule backwards and writing $\tfrac13(x^2-4)^3$. There is no backwards chain rule. That answer differentiates back to the integrand times $2x$, and the check catches it with a relative gap of 1.120e+01.

(e)   $\int_2^4 x\sqrt{x-1}\,dx$   rung 3

Put $u=x-1$, so $x=u+1$ and $dx=du$. Change the limits: $x=2$ gives $u=1$, and $x=4$ gives $u=3$.

$$\int_1^3\left(u^{3/2}+u^{1/2}\right)du =\left[\frac{2}{5}u^{5/2}+\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}\right]_1^3 =\frac{28\sqrt3}{5}-\frac{16}{15}$$

The step that carries the marks is rewriting $x$ as $u+1$. Leaving the $x$ in place is this part's defining error - and it is exactly what the ladder means by convert $dx$ completely. Differentiating back does catch it, with a gap of 1.384e+00.

Expand whenever the bracket power is 2 or 3. Substitute and change the limits whenever there are limits. Reach for an identity only when two substitution candidates have both failed.

07The check, and its two blind spots

Differentiating your answer back is the only verification available to you in a Mobius exam. It costs about eight seconds per part. Twenty-six wrong answers were seeded and differentiated back; the result is not a matter of degree.

Differentiating back detects every error that changes the function, and is structurally blind to every error that does not.

What to do about each blind spot

THE CONSTANT

$\tfrac{d}{dx}C=0$, so a missing $+C$ is invisible. Adding $10^6$ to part (d)'s answer still differentiates back perfectly.

Remedy: it is a checklist item, not a check. Look at the answer and confirm the letter $C$ is physically there. The control that proves this arm measures something: a non-constant perturbation of the same size is detected.

THE DOMAIN

$F'=f$ is a pointwise identity. It holds wherever both sides are defined and says nothing about where that is. Part (a) needs $x\ge0$, part (e) needs $x\ge1$.

Remedy: read the domain off the original integrand, not off your answer. Every even root and every denominator in the integrand is a restriction.

THE LIMITS

On a definite integral the antiderivative can be flawless while the evaluation runs backwards. $F(2)-F(4)$ on part (e) rests on a perfect antiderivative and is still wrong by a sign.

Remedy: a sign-and-magnitude sanity check. $x\sqrt{x-1}$ is positive on $[2,4]$, so the answer must be positive. It is: 8.63.

The fastest structural check there is: every exponent in your answer must be one higher than the matching one in the integrand. If any is lower, you have differentiated instead of integrated.

09The variation catalog

Read each family as structure, not as a list of facts. Across all six families there are only four distinct tools: the power rule, the $1/a$ factor, a substitution, and a trig identity. Every row is one of those four reached by a different-looking route.

Every antiderivative below was differentiated back by complex-step at many in-domain points, and every definite value was checked against tanh-sinh quadrature that never saw the closed form.

10The trap list

  1. The missing $+C$. Three of five parts need it, nothing you can check will remind you, and it is the cheapest mark on the page to lose.
  2. A stray $x$ after substitution. Part (e)'s defining error. Differentiating back does catch it, so the eight-second check saves it.
  3. The unbracketed fractional exponent. A method-perfect part (a) scoring zero.
  4. Running the chain rule backwards. Substitution only works when $du$ is actually present.
  5. Dropping the $1/a$. Part (c)'s $\cos 2x$ integrates to $\tfrac12\sin 2x$, not $\sin 2x$.
  6. Forgetting to convert the limits. Nothing in the algebra looks wrong. Write the new limits at the moment you write the substitution, not later.
  7. Reaching for a technique before trying algebra. Parts (a) and (d) punish this, and Q16.2c punishes it harder.
  8. Writing $+C$ on a definite integral. The mirror of trap 1.
  9. Missing the domain restriction. Structurally invisible - read it off the integrand.
  10. Swapping the limits. Gives exactly the negative, on a flawless antiderivative.

Methods, and why each is there:

  • Complex-step differentiation at $h=10^{-30}$ for every claim that an antiderivative differentiates back - exact to machine precision, with no subtractive cancellation.
  • A bounded Richardson ladder as an independent second arm, with its convergence order measured (2.000, 2.000, 2.000) rather than assumed.
  • Tanh-sinh quadrature for every $F(b)-F(a)$ comparison, adopted after Gauss-Legendre was measured to stall at 3.7e-06 on integrands with a half-power endpoint while tanh-sinh reached 1.4e-14 on the same integral.
  • Measured convergence order before any digit was locked. Simpson measures 4.00 on a smooth control and 1.67 on a cusp control, confirming the order test discriminates.
  • Every printed decimal computed, printed and then digit-locked. No decimal on this page was typed by hand.
