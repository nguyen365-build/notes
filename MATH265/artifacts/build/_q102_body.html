MATH 265 · Q10.2 · MT2 item 10 · 4 points

The Point Must Be On The Curve

Find the equation of the tangent line to \(y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2\) at \((1,1)\). This is the last question in the implicit-differentiation category, and it invents nothing. It joins the derivative you learned in Q10.1 to the line you learned in Q9.1, and it adds one obligation neither parent had.

The answer\(y = 2x - 1\)
Slope at (1,1)\(m = 2\)
Type into Möbius2*x-1

01 What it adds, what it inherits

Q10.2 is a join. Nothing in it is a new technique, which is exactly why it is only worth four points and exactly why it is easy to drop them. Two halves you already own, plus one obligation that appears the moment they are put together.

Inherited

  • From Q10.1 - differentiate both sides in \(x\), attach \(y'\) to every \(y\), collect the \(y'\) terms, divide.
  • From Q10.1 - the answer legitimately contains \(y\). That is not a mistake and not an unfinished step.
  • From Q9.1 - a slope plus a point makes a line: \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\).
  • From Q9.1 - the geometry around it: normals, parallels, perpendiculars.

Added here

  • It needs both coordinates. An explicit \(f'(x)\) takes one number. An implicit \(y'\) is a function of \(x\) and \(y\), so the point travels as a pair. That is why the stem hands you \((1,1)\) and not just \(x = 1\).
  • The point must be verified. Feed \(y'\) a \(y\) that does not belong to that \(x\) and it returns a confident, wrong number. Nothing in the algebra complains.
  • A slope is not a line. Q9.1 asked for values of \(x\). This asks for an equation. Stopping at \(m = 2\) answers a question nobody asked.

One more difference, and it is real. Q10.1's stem says "using implicit differentiation" - a method instruction. Q10.2's stem says no such thing. Any correct route is legal here, so pick the fastest rather than the one the stem names, because the stem names none.

02 The thirty-second version

GIVEN  y^3 + y x^2 + x^2 = 3 y^2   at (1,1)

  0) CHECK the point:  1 + 1 + 1 = 3  and  3(1) = 3.   ON the curve.

  1) d/dx both sides, y' on every y:
        3y^2 y' + (x^2 y' + 2xy) + 2x = 6y y'

  2) SUBSTITUTE (1,1) NOW, while it is still linear in y':
        3m + m + 2 + 2 = 6m   ->   4m + 4 = 6m   ->   m = 2

  3) BUILD THE LINE:  y - 1 = 2(x - 1)   ->   y = 2x - 1

  4) CHECK:  x = 1 gives y = 1.          ANSWER: y = 2x - 1

Four steps, no fractions, no algebra with letters in it. Step 2 is the whole trick, and section 04 measures what the textbook order costs instead.

03 The curve, and every point that matters on it

This one picture carries most of the tutorial. The relation is quadratic in \(x\), so it can be drawn exactly from \(x = \pm y\sqrt{(3-y)/(1+y)}\) for \(-1 < y \le 3\) - every plotted point satisfies the relation by construction rather than by root-finding.

FIGURE_SVG

The curve is a node: a closed loop for \(0 \le y \le 3\) and two unbounded arms below, crossing themselves at the origin. The amber tangent touches at \((1,1)\) and, being a line against a cubic, is entitled to cross the curve again - it does, at \((2/5, -1/5)\). The two dashed marks are where the tangent turns vertical and the slope form stops applying.

(1, 1)

the exam point

\(F_y = -2 \ne 0\), so the slope is finite and point-slope applies. \(m = 2\).

(0, 3)

horizontal tangent

\(F_x = 0\), \(F_y = 9 \ne 0\). The tangent is \(y = 3\). Also where two wrong answers hide - see section 07.

\(\left(\pm\sqrt{6\sqrt3 - 9},\ \sqrt3\right)\)

vertical tangents

\(F_y = 0\) but \(F_x \approx \pm 6.447 \ne 0\). The answer is \(x = \pm 1.17996\), with no \(m\) anywhere in it.

(0, 0)

singular point

Both partials vanish. \(-F_x/F_y\) is \(0/0\) and there is no unique tangent. Do not report a slope of zero.

04 Three routes, and why the short one wins

Route A textbook order

Solve for \(y'\) as a formula first, substitute afterwards.

$$y'\left(3y^2 + x^2 - 6y\right) = -2x(y+1)$$

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-2x(y+1)}{3y^2 + x^2 - 6y} \;\Longrightarrow\; m = \frac{-4}{-2} = 2$$

Seven lines. A factorisation, then a division of a negative by a negative - two sign opportunities in one step. Worth it only when a later part needs the general formula.

Route B use this one

Differentiate, then substitute the point before solving.

$$3y^2 y' + x^2 y' + 2xy + 2x = 6y y'$$

$$3m + m + 2 + 2 = 6m \;\Longrightarrow\; 4m + 4 = 6m \;\Longrightarrow\; m = 2$$

Three lines. No factorisation, no letters, no fraction. Every sign trap in Route A lives in the step this route never takes.

Route C verification only

Make \(x\) explicit in \(y\) and reciprocate.

$$x = y\sqrt{\frac{3-y}{1+y}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \left.\frac{dx}{dy}\right|_{y=1} = \tfrac12$$

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{dx/dy} = 2$$

Ten-plus lines, and it only exists because this relation happens to be quadratic in \(x\). Most are not. Excellent as an independent check, a trap as an exam route.

Why Route B is legal, and what it is confused with. The differentiated equation is an identity holding at every point of the curve. \((1,1)\) is such a point - you checked in step 0 - and the equation is linear in \(y'\), so solving it there gives \(y'\) there. What is genuinely illegal is substituting into the original relation first: setting \(y = 1\) turns the relation into \(1 + x^2 + x^2 = 3\), an equation in \(x\) alone, and \(y'\) has left the building. Differentiate first, substitute second.

05 The procedure, with the exit nobody draws

Most students draw one path through this question. There are three, and they separate at a single step: what the coefficient of \(m\) turns out to be.

  1. Verify the point. Substitute both coordinates into the original relation. Exact agreement, or stop.
  2. Differentiate both sides in \(x\). A \(y'\) on every \(y\); the product rule on every term where two things vary. Do not simplify, do not solve yet.
  3. Substitute the point. You now hold a linear equation in the single unknown \(m\).
  4. Read the coefficient of \(m\) - and this is where the paths split.

coefficient ≠ 0

\(m\) is a number

Build the line: \(y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)\). This is the exam question, and the answer is \(y = 2x - 1\).

coefficient = 0, rest ≠ 0

vertical tangent

The answer is \(x = x_1\). There is no \(m\), and point-slope form cannot be used at all. This is the cardioid question the course sets.

coefficient = 0, rest = 0

singular point

\(0/0\). No unique tangent exists. Say so - do not report \(m = 0\), which is what the arithmetic tempts you into.

Then, always: put \(x = x_1\) back into your finished line. If it does not return \(y_1\), the line misses the given point and nothing else about it matters.

What the tangency check really is. A tangent line does not just touch the curve - it meets it in a double root. Substituting \(y = 2x-1\) back into the relation gives \(10x^3 - 24x^2 + 18x - 4 = 2(x-1)^2(5x-2)\), and that squared factor is the tangency, stated algebraically. A merely secant line through \((1,1)\) leaves \((x-1)\) to the first power; slopes 1, 3 and 7/4 were each tried and each rejected. The leftover factor \(5x-2\) is a free bonus: it names \((2/5, -1/5)\), the point where the tangent crosses the curve again.

A measurement mistake worth repeating. The Richardson ladder first ran with a fixed step \(h = 0.04\) everywhere. That is fine at \((1,1)\), where the slope is 2, but at \(y = 2\) on the negative branch the slope is 5.196, so a step that size moves \(y\) far enough that Newton lands on a different branch of the curve: error 8.38, apparent order 7.28. The fix is not a looser tolerance - it is a base step derived from the measured slope, \(h_0 = 0.04/|m|\). Then the orders read 4.08 / 4.02 / 4.00. A fixed step is always calibrated on the flattest point you happened to test.

07 Where the marks actually go

  1. Reporting the slope instead of the line. The stem says equation of the tangent line. \(m = 2\) is not an equation. On an answer-only platform this is a clean zero for a question you solved correctly, and it is the single most common way to lose all four points.
  2. Substituting into the original relation before differentiating. Freezing \(y = 1\) destroys the function you were about to differentiate. Note this is not Route B, which substitutes into the already-differentiated equation and is both legal and recommended.
  3. Never checking that \((1,1)\) is on the curve. Here it is, so nothing goes wrong. When it is not, nothing goes wrong either - right up until the mark.
  4. Dropping half the product rule on \(yx^2\). The term the whole curve is built around. Keeping only \(x^2y'\) gives \(m = 1\).
  5. Forgetting \(y'\) on one \(y\)-term but not the others. On \(y^3\) it gives 1.4; on \(3y^2\) it gives 0.5. Both look like plausible answers.
  6. Sign slip collecting \(6yy'\). Gives \(m = -2\) - right magnitude, wrong direction, which is what makes it dangerous.
  7. The intercept subtraction. \(b = 1 - 2 = -1\). Writing \(y = 2x + 1\) puts the line through \((1,3)\), which is not on the curve at all.

Where the wrong answers hide

Whether a wrong answer has a hiding place is a property of this curve, not of the topic. All four slips were scanned over 120 points spanning both branches, with no convenience exclusions.

So do not spot-check an implicit derivative at a horizontal tangent. Two of the four slips are exactly invisible at \((0,3)\), because both collapse to zero wherever the true slope is zero. That point is fully reachable, and it is the second most convenient point on this curve - precisely the one a student reaches for. \((1,1)\) is a good check point because none of the four survives there.

08 The variation families

Read this as structure, not as eleven facts. Every family is the same two-part machine: get a slope from \(-F_x/F_y\), then do something geometric with it. What changes is only which half the examiner hides.

F0 · point given, on the curve

"at the point \((a,b)\)"

The exam question. Differentiate, substitute the pair, solve, build the line. Circle, ellipse, folium, \(x^2+xy+y^2=3\) - one method, not six.

F1 · one coordinate given

"at the point where \(x = a\)"

Solve for the missing coordinate first, and expect several. At \(x = 1\) this curve gives \(y = 1\) and \(y = 1 \pm \sqrt2\) - three points, three tangents. That ambiguity is why the real stem hands you the pair.

F2 · horizontal tangents

numerator \(F_x = 0\)

Solve \(F_x = 0\) with \(F = 0\), then confirm \(F_y \ne 0\). Here \(F_x = 2x(y+1)\), and \(y = -1\) is not on the curve at all, so only \(x = 0\) survives.

F3 · vertical tangents

denominator \(F_y = 0\)

The answer is \(x = c\); there is no \(m\). Confirm \(F_x \ne 0\) so the ratio is infinite and not \(0/0\). The course sets exactly this on the cardioid.

F4 · the normal line

"normal", "perpendicular to the curve"

Flip: \(m_n = -1/m = -\tfrac12\), giving \(y = -\tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac32\). Q9.1's condition reused unchanged.

F5 · a prescribed slope

"parallel to", "perpendicular to \(y = \ldots\)"

Two equations in two unknowns: \(-F_x/F_y = k\) together with \(F = 0\). The half people forget is the second one.

F6 · transcendental relations

\(\sin\), \(\cos\), \(e^x\), \(\ln\)

Identical method; only the outer derivative changes. Examiners pick the point so the transcendental evaluates to 0 or 1 - if you are computing \(e^{2.7}\), you substituted the wrong coordinate.

F7 · the second derivative

\(y''\), concavity

Quotient rule on \(y'\), then substitute the \(y'\) you already have. Here \(y'' = 6 > 0\): concave up, tangent below the curve.

F8 · meets the curve again

"the other intersection"

Divide out the double factor. \(2(x-1)^2(5x-2)\) gives \((2/5, -1/5)\). Also the cleanest self-check on F0: if \((x-x_1)\) does not divide twice, the slope is wrong.

F9 · the point is not on the curve

nothing in the wording - that is the point

Every later line still works. \(y'\) returns a number, the line looks tidy, nothing complains. Only possible because implicit \(y'\) takes two inputs and validates neither.

F10 · tangent as an approximation

"estimate \(y\) when \(x = 1.05\)"

Linearization with the derivative arriving implicitly: \(y \approx 1.1\). And \(y'' > 0\) tells you for free that it is an underestimate.

09 Answer entry

Möbius is answer-only and machine-marked, so a right method with a bad keystroke scores nothing.

Do

  • 2*x-1 - explicit multiplication
  • Give the whole line when an equation is asked for
  • Lower-case x
  • Preview, then read what actually rendered

Do not

  • 2x-1 - implied multiplication may not parse
  • 2 - that is the slope, not a line
  • y= twice, when the box already prints it
  • Submit without previewing

If the platform splits slope and intercept into two boxes, they are \(2\) and \(-1\). No marking scheme exists in any of the four course files, so every claim here about what earns marks is reasoned inference, flagged as such rather than quoted.
