MATH 265 · Q11.1 · MT1 item 7 · 5 points

Always Versus When

Gravel falls onto a pile at \(0.5\ \mathrm{m^3/min}\). The pile is a cone whose base diameter and height are always equal. How fast is the height rising when the pile is 4 m high? This is the first related-rates question in the bank, and it is a different animal from everything before it: the calculus is one power rule, and every single mark is in the reading.

The answer\(\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{8\pi}\ \mathrm{m/min}\)
Decimal0.0398 m/min
Type into Möbius1/(8*pi)

01 Two words decide the whole question

A related-rates stem hands you numbers of two completely different kinds, and it does not label them. Some are rates and fixed relationships, true at every moment. Others are snapshots, true only at the instant you are asked about. Telling them apart is not a detail of this question; it is this question.

“Always” — true at every instant

  • “at a rate of \(0.5\ \mathrm{m^3/min}\)” → \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=0.5\), and it stays 0.5 forever.
  • “base diameter and height are always equal” → \(2r=h\), so \(r=\dfrac{h}{2}\).

You may substitute these into the equation before you differentiate. That is the whole point of the word “always”.

“When” — true at one instant only

  • “when the pile is 4 m high” → \(h=4\), and only at one moment out of all time.

Substituting this early turns \(h\) into a number, so \(V\) becomes a constant, so \(\dfrac{dV}{dt}=0\), and you are left solving \(0.5 = 0\). The question is destroyed and there is nothing to salvage.

This is the defining error of the entire related-rates category. It is not a slip in the algebra — it is a misreading, committed before any calculus starts. If you take one thing from this page: a variable may not be replaced by its value until after you differentiate.

02 Write four lines before you touch calculus

Every related-rates solution starts the same way, and the four lines below are cheap insurance. If you cannot fill them in, you have not finished reading the question yet.

1 · Name

\(h\) = height in m, \(r\) = base radius in m, \(V\) = volume in m\(^3\). Each is a function of time \(t\) in minutes.

2 · Given

\(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 0.5\ \mathrm{m^3/min}\). A rate, with units, holding at all times.

3 · Want

\(\dfrac{dh}{dt}\) in m/min, at the instant \(h = 4\) m.

4 · Relate

One equation joining what you have to what you want: \(V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h\).

The formula is not printed on this question. The other cone question in the course, MT2 item 2, prints the hint “The volume of a cone is \(V = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}\)”. MT1 item 7 does not. You have to know it.

03 Eliminate, then differentiate, then substitute

The relating equation has two variables, \(r\) and \(h\), and you only want one. The fixed ratio is what removes the extra one. Diameter means \(2r\), so “the base diameter equals the height” is \(2r = h\), which is \(r = h/2\) — not \(r = h\).

Eliminate

\(V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi\left(\dfrac{h}{2}\right)^{2}h = \dfrac{\pi h^{3}}{12}\)

Differentiate in \(t\)

\(\dfrac{dV}{dt} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}h^{2}\,\dfrac{dh}{dt}\)

Substitute the instant

\(0.5 = \dfrac{\pi}{4}(4)^{2}\dfrac{dh}{dt} = 4\pi\dfrac{dh}{dt}\)

Solve

\(\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0398\)

Where the chain rule enters. \(h\) is not a plain variable here — it is \(h(t)\), a function of time. So differentiating \(h^3\) with respect to \(t\) gives \(3h^2\) times \(dh/dt\), and that trailing factor is the thing you are solving for. This is exactly the \(y'\) from Q10.1 wearing different clothes. If your differentiated equation does not contain the rate you want, you dropped it: go back.

Both MT1 questions that test this chain factor are worth 5 points and sit two items apart: item 7 is this one, item 8 is Q10.1's implicit differentiation. Revise them together.

04 What is actually happening

FIGURE_SVG

Left: because the diameter equals the height, the pile's cross-section is drawn to scale — as wide as it is tall, at every size it ever reaches. Right: the real growth curve \(h(t)=(6t/\pi)^{1/3}\) for a pile fed at a constant \(0.5\ \mathrm{m^3/min}\) from empty. The answer is the slope of that curve at one point. The curve visibly flattens, which is the physical content of the answer: the taller the pile, the wider its base, so the same half cubic metre per minute buys less height.

05 Two other routes, and why you should not use them

Route B — differentiate first, eliminate afterwards. Keep both variables and use the product rule:

\[\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(2rh\,\frac{dr}{dt} + r^{2}\,\frac{dh}{dt}\right)\]

Then put in \(r = h/2\) and its own derivative \(\dfrac{dr}{dt} = \dfrac12\dfrac{dh}{dt}\), and it collapses to the same \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}h^2\dfrac{dh}{dt}\). This is worth doing once, because it proves the early substitution in Route A was legal rather than lucky. It is a bad exam route: it needs the product rule and it needs the ratio's derivative, and forgetting the second is a fresh way to lose the mark.

Route C — the base-area shortcut. For any cone that keeps its shape as it grows, differentiating \(V = \frac13\pi k^2h^3\) gives \(\dfrac{dV}{dh} = \pi(kh)^2 = \pi r^2\), which is exactly the base area. So

\[\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{dV/dt}{\text{base area}} = \frac{0.5}{4\pi} = \frac{1}{8\pi}\]

in a single line. Do not write this as your method — the marker wants the differentiation. Use it as an eight-second check.

06 Check it in eight seconds

Magnitude

Half a cubic metre spread over a base of \(4\pi \approx 12.6\ \mathrm{m^2}\) must raise the pile about \(0.5/12.6 \approx 0.04\) m each minute. Four centimetres a minute is right for a gravel pile. Four metres a minute is not.

Units, worked not guessed

\(\left[\dfrac{dh}{dt}\right] = \dfrac{\mathrm{m^3/min}}{\mathrm{m^2}} = \mathrm{m/min}\). Not m\(^3\)/min just because the given rate was. This is the classic lost mark and no numerical check catches it.

Sign

Positive. The pile is growing. A negative answer means you inverted a rate somewhere.

Is the instant reachable?

Do not assume it. \(V = 0.5t\) and \(\pi h^3/12 = 0.5t\) give \(h = 4\) at \(t = \frac{32\pi}{3} \approx 33.5\) min. Finite and positive, so yes. Run the same screen on a shrinking pile asked to reach a greater height and it correctly returns “never”.

Scaling

\(dh/dt \propto 1/h^2\), so doubling the height divides the rate by exactly 4. Check your own two answers against that.

Answer the right question

“How fast is the height increasing” is \(dh/dt\). Not \(dr/dt\) (half as big), not the base-area rate (0.25), and not \(dV/dt\), which you were given.

08 Every variation the examiner can reach for

Read these as structure, not as a list of facts. For a self-similar cone there is only ever one formula, \(\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{dV/dt}{\pi k^{2}h^{2}}\) with \(r = kh\), and every row in Family 1 is that formula with a different \(k\). The magnitudes are generated by \(k^{-2}\) alone; the signs are generated entirely by whether material is being added or removed. Every value below was verified twice — once from the closed form, and once formula-blind, by building an explicit \(h(t)\) and differentiating it numerically.

09 Where a wrong answer hides

Seven plausible wrong formulas were scanned across nine heights and five volume rates. The degenerate cases were deliberately included rather than skipped for tidiness, because that is exactly where a wrong answer becomes invisible.

At a zero rate, everything looks right

All seven wrong formulas differ from the truth by a multiplicative constant, so at \(dV/dt = 0\) every one of them returns 0 — the same as the truth. A spot-check performed on a stationary pile cannot fail, so it proves nothing at all.

At \(h = 1\), the exponent slips vanish

Writing \(h^3\) or \(h\) where \(h^2\) belongs is invisible at \(h = 1\), and only there, because that is where the extra factor equals one. Never sanity-check a related-rates formula at \(h = 1\).

This stem is itself a degenerate case

Because the diameter equals the height, \(d = h = 4\) numerically at the instant asked. A student who reads the pile's “width” as its height gets the right number here by luck. Change the ratio to \(r = h\) and the same confusion costs a factor of 4.

10 The trap list, ranked

  1. Substituting \(h = 4\) before differentiating. The defining error of the category. A snapshot may only be substituted after you differentiate; a relationship holding at all times may be substituted before. The word “always” is the only thing distinguishing them.
  2. Using \(r = h\) instead of \(r = h/2\). The stem says the diameter equals the height. This is the single most common wrong answer here, a factor of 4 too small.
  3. Never substituting the ratio at all. Treating \(r\) as a constant gives an answer three times too large, and it looks like a complete solution, which is what makes it dangerous.
  4. Dropping the chain factor. If the rate you want is missing from your differentiated equation, you dropped it. Re-differentiate rather than pressing on.
  5. Dropping the \(\frac13\) and integrating a cylinder's worth of gravel instead.
  6. Reporting m\(^3\)/min because that is what the given rate was in. The answer is a length per time. No numerical check catches this, so units get their own line.
  7. Answering the wrong question — giving \(dr/dt\), or the base-area rate, or restating \(dV/dt\).
  8. The missing bracket in the Möbius box.

11 Drill set

Score sign separately from magnitude. A wrong sign is a reading problem; a wrong magnitude is an algebra problem. They need different fixes, so do not lump them together.

  1. The same gravel pile: how fast is the height increasing when the pile is 2 m high?
  2. The same pile at \(h=4\): how fast is the base radius increasing?
  3. The same pile at \(h=4\): how fast is the base area increasing?
  4. Gravel at 2 m\(^3\)/min forms a cone whose base radius always equals its height. How fast is the height rising at \(h = 3\)?
  5. Water enters an inverted conical tank of top radius 3 m and depth 6 m at 0.8 m\(^3\)/min. How fast is the depth rising when it is 2 m?
  6. A spherical balloon is inflated at 10 cm\(^3\)/s. How fast is the radius growing when \(r = 5\) cm?
  7. Same balloon, same instant: how fast is the surface area growing?
  8. A 13 ft ladder leans on a wall; the foot is pulled away at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 5 ft out?
  9. A rocket rises vertically, tracked by a radar station 5 mi away. How fast is it rising when it is 4 mi high and its distance from the station grows at 2000 mi/h?
  10. A cone has \(r=2\), \(h=4\), with \(\dot r = 0.1\) and \(\dot h = 0.05\) independently. How fast is the volume growing?
  11. A 5 m lamp post, a 2 m person walking away at 1.5 m/s. How fast is the tip of the shadow moving?
  12. For the original pile, at what height is the height increasing at exactly 1 cm/min?

13 Provenance and verification

The paper

Midterm Exam 1, item 7. MT1 prints ten point values — 5, 6, 5, 16, 8, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5 — summing to 64, and item 7 is worth 5, or 7.8 percent of that total. Parsed from the paper file rather than typed.

Clauses that do not apply

MT1 carries exactly one “no credit will be given” clause and it is on item 3. Item 7 also lacks MT1's “You may not need to simplify” permission, which sits on item 5. Do not import a neighbour's clause.

Its sibling

Item 8 is Q10.1, implicit differentiation, also 5 points. Both test the same chain factor — written \(y'\) there and \(dh/dt\) here. Revise them as a pair.

What the course files get right

Both of \(\texttt{MATH265\_Review.md}\)'s statements of the cone method are correct, and so is the paper file's walkthrough. This is one of the cleaner topics in the course material.

What they leave out

No course file ever finishes the arithmetic — the value \(1/(8\pi)\) appears nowhere in any of the four — and neither related-rates passage mentions units at all.

And one dangerous phrasing

“Substitute \(r\) to get \(V\) in terms of \(h\) before differentiating” is correct, but a student who generalises it into “substitute before differentiating” will put \(h=4\) in early and destroy the problem. No course file draws that distinction. This page exists largely to draw it.

No marking scheme exists anywhere in the course material — zero occurrences of “rubric”, “marking scheme” or “part marks” across all four files — so any claim about which sentences earn which marks is inference, and is flagged as such rather than asserted.
