Both Ends Count

Q15.1 asks for all maxima and minima of two functions on two closed intervals. Between them the two parts have four absolute extremes, and three of the four sit at an endpoint of the interval. Part (a) punishes you for leaving the right endpoint off the list; part (b) punishes you for assuming a critical point must be an extreme. That pair of punishments is the whole question, and it is why the method always tests both ends.

01 The question, and the four numbers per part

Find all maxima and minima of the functions listed below on the indicated intervals.
a. \(f(x) = 2x^{5/3} - 5x^{4/3}\) on \([-1, 20]\).     b. \(f(x) = x + \cos x\) on \([-\pi, 2\pi]\).

An extreme has two answers, and they are different kinds of thing. The value is a number that \(f\) outputs. The point is the input where it happens. Write both, every time, because "the maximum is 20" is the most expensive slip on this question and no magnitude check can catch it.

The two parts are near-exact complements, and that is the design. Part (a) has an interior minimum and a critical point that is a genuine local maximum; part (b) has neither, and both of its extremes are ends. Each part's empty cell is the other part's content.

02 Both parts, drawn to the same idea

The figure exists to make one thing visible: where the winning points are. On part (a) the curve dips to its minimum in the middle and then climbs past everything it has been to reach the right edge. On part (b) it never stops climbing, so the two ends are the only places an extreme can be, and the flat tangent in the middle is a shelf on the way up.

The maximum level is drawn all the way across. Nothing on part (a)'s curve reaches it until the last point of the interval, which is exactly why a candidate list without \(x=20\) produces a plausible and wrong answer.

Part (b) rises throughout. The horizontal tangent at \(\pi/2\) is a genuine critical point and it is neither a maximum nor a minimum, because \(f'(x) = 1 - \sin x\) touches zero without changing sign.

03 The closed-interval method

This is the method the whole category is built on, and Q15.1 is where it gets established. It needs no derivative test at all. You build a short list of numbers and pick the biggest and the smallest, and the Extreme Value Theorem is what guarantees there is a biggest and a smallest to pick.

  1. Check the theorem applies. Closed interval, bounded, \(f\) continuous on it. One sentence. If any of the three fails you are not in this method at all, and section 08's families 6 and 9 say what to do instead.
  2. Differentiate, and factor. You are about to set \(f'\) to zero and also to look for where it blows up, and a factored form answers both at a glance.
  3. Solve \(f' = 0\). Substitute if a fractional power makes the algebra easier.
  4. Find where \(f'\) is UNDEFINED. A denominator, a negative power, an absolute value, an exponent strictly between 0 and 1. If there are none, write that down rather than skipping the step.
  5. Discard critical points outside \((a,b)\). A critical point off the interval is simply not relevant.
  6. Build the candidate list: \(a\), \(b\), then the surviving critical points. Write the endpoints FIRST, so you cannot forget them.
  7. Evaluate \(f\) at every candidate. Exactly, in a table.
  8. Compare. Largest is the absolute maximum, smallest the absolute minimum. Report each as a value AND a point. If a value ties, list every point.
  9. Only if the stem said "all maxima and minima": classify the interior critical points that lost, using a sign chart of \(f'\).

Step 9 is conditional and the wording decides it. Q15.1 says "find all maxima and minima", so the losers need naming. Q15.3 and Q15.5 say "the absolute extreme values", so they do not. Read which one you were asked.

The Extreme Value Theorem, and what each hypothesis buys

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\), then \(f\) attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on \([a,b]\). All three conditions are load-bearing, and the verification harness removed them one at a time.

DROP CLOSED
\(f\) on \([-1,20)\) has no maximum. The supremum 23.2808 was approached over 200,000 samples and never attained.
DROP BOUNDED
\(f\) on \([-1,\infty)\) has no maximum. Measured: \(f(10^6) > 10^9\).
DROP CONTINUOUS
\(1/x\) on \([-1,1]\) has neither. The interval is closed and bounded and it does not help.

The theorem is one-directional. It promises the extremes exist; it says nothing about where. That is why a candidate list is still needed, and why "the EVT does not apply" never means "there is no answer" but only "you have lost the guarantee and must argue".

Three ways \(f'\) can fail to exist, and all three are critical points

A centred difference quotient cannot see any of them. At the cusp of \((x^2+2x)^{2/3}\) it returns \(5.3\times 10^{-5}\) while both one-sided slopes exceed 150 in magnitude, an understatement by a factor above a million. At a corner it returns the average of the two slopes, which is exactly zero only when the corner is symmetric; on an asymmetric one it returns 1.5, which is neither slope. Test one-sided slopes separately.

04 Part (a), worked

\(x^{5/3}\) and \(x^{4/3}\) are continuous for every real \(x\), because the cube root is, and \([-1,20]\) is closed and bounded. The theorem applies, so both extremes exist.

$$f'(x) = \tfrac{10}{3}x^{2/3} - \tfrac{20}{3}x^{1/3} = \tfrac{10}{3}\,x^{1/3}\!\left(x^{1/3} - 2\right)$$

So \(x^{1/3} = 0\) or \(x^{1/3} = 2\), giving \(x = 0\) and \(x = 8\). The substitution \(t = x^{1/3}\) is worth naming: it turns the whole family into a polynomial, \(f = 2t^5 - 5t^4\) with \(\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}t = 10t^3(t-2)\), and because \(t = x^{1/3}\) is increasing it preserves the order of the values, so the comparison could be done in \(t\) too.

Step 4, and this is the step the course notes get wrong. Every power of \(x\) in \(f'\) is positive, \(2/3\) and \(1/3\). There is no denominator and no negative power, so \(f'\) is defined everywhere and there are no cusps, corners or vertical tangents. The one-sided difference quotients at \(x=0\) have closed forms,

$$\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h} = -5h^{1/3} + 2h^{2/3}, \qquad \frac{f(0)-f(-h)}{h} = +5h^{1/3} + 2h^{2/3}$$

both confirmed to \(10^{-14}\) over a ladder from \(h=10^{-3}\) down to \(10^{-30}\). They approach zero from opposite sides and agree in the limit, which is what "differentiable at 0, with \(f'(0)=0\)" means. Note the rate: each factor-of-1000 cut in \(h\) divides the quotient by only 10, because the convergence is \(O(h^{1/3})\). That is why a spot check at \(h=10^{-6}\) shows a slope of \(-0.0498\) and looks like it is not converging at all.

The general rule is worth carrying: an exponent below 1 gives you an undefined derivative and an exponent above 1 does not. Part (a)'s exponents are \(5/3\) and \(4/3\), both above 1, which is why its cusp box is empty. Q15.5's are \(2/3\), which is why its is not.

So \(x=0\) is a local maximum with value 0, and \(x=8\) is a local minimum with value \(-16\). The endpoints are one-sided local extremes. Two sign changes, two interior extremes, and the intervals tile \([-1,20]\) breaking only at the critical points.

The second-derivative test would have been a poor choice here. \(f''(x) = \frac{20}{9}x^{-1/3} - \frac{20}{9}x^{-2/3}\) blows up at \(x=0\), the very point you most want classified, so the test is unusable there. It does work at \(x=8\), where \(f''(8) = \frac{5}{9} > 0\). The sign chart handles both points with one piece of machinery.

One extra fact that explains the shape. \(f(x) = x^{4/3}\left(2x^{1/3}-5\right)\) is zero at \(x=0\) and where \(x^{1/3} = 5/2\), that is \(x = 125/8 = 15.625\). The curve dips below the axis after the origin, bottoms out at \((8,-16)\), and climbs back through zero at 15.625 before reaching 23.2808. That single number is why the maximum is at the endpoint: the interval reaches past it.

05 Part (b), and the critical point that wins nothing

\(f'(x) = 1 - \sin x\), defined everywhere, and zero exactly where \(\sin x = 1\), that is \(x = \frac{\pi}{2} + 2k\pi\). The interval is only \(3\pi\) long, so exactly one of those lands inside it: \(k=0\), giving \(x = \pi/2\). (\(k=-1\) gives \(-3\pi/2 < -\pi\); \(k=1\) gives \(5\pi/2 > 2\pi\).)

\(x\)why\(f(x)\) exact\(f(x)\)
\(-\pi\)left endpoint\(-\pi - 1\)\(-4.1415926536\)  MIN
\(\pi/2\)\(f' = 0\)\(\pi/2\)\(1.5707963268\)
\(2\pi\)right endpoint\(2\pi + 1\)\(7.2831853072\)  MAX

The sentence that earns the marks: \(\sin x \le 1\) for every \(x\), so \(f'(x) = 1 - \sin x \ge 0\) everywhere, with equality only at \(\pi/2\). Therefore \(f\) is increasing on the whole interval, confirmed over 5,000 consecutive sample pairs. The critical point \(\pi/2\) is a horizontal tangent that is neither a maximum nor a minimum, and its value sits strictly between the two endpoint values. There are no local extremes in the interior at all.

The second-derivative test is silent, and structurally so: \(f''(x) = -\cos x\) and \(f''(\pi/2) = 0\). What settles it is the sign chart, or the third derivative, \(f'''(x) = \sin x\) with \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1 \ne 0\), which makes \(\pi/2\) an inflection point.

Why you cannot find this critical point by looking

A sign-change census of \(f'\) over \([-\pi,2\pi]\) returns zero critical points at every resolution tried, from 37 subintervals up to 99,999, because \(f'\) never changes sign. That is Q14.1's finding recurring exactly, and it is why the verification here needed a third arm: a search for local minima of \(\lvert f'\rvert\) refined by golden section, which is the only one of the three that finds \(\pi/2\).

And there is a coincidence in this particular interval that sharpens the point rather than softening it. \(\pi/2\) is exactly the midpoint of \([-\pi, 2\pi]\), since \(\left(\pi/2 + \pi\right) / 3\pi = 1/2\) precisely. So any grid with an even number of subintervals lands on it, \(1 - \sin x\) evaluates to a hard zero, and a value-scan "finds" it. Any odd grid misses it entirely and reports a strictly positive minimum. The verdict of a sampling test on part (b) is decided by the parity of the grid. Solve \(\sin x = 1\) instead.

09 The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks. Every entry was measured, not guessed.

  1. Leaving an endpoint off the candidate list. The question's designed failure. On part (a) dropping the right endpoint costs the whole maximum; on part (b) dropping either endpoint costs an answer. Write both ends first.
  2. Reporting the point as if it were the value. "The maximum is 20" instead of 23.2808. The wrong answer is only 14.1 percent below the right one on part (a) and off by exactly 1 on part (b), so no magnitude check will save you.
  3. Assuming a critical point must be an extreme. Calling \(\pi/2\) a maximum on part (b) is 5.712 below the truth. It is the one classification error there that changes the answer.
  4. Never looking for points where \(f'\) is undefined. Part (a) has none, which is why writing "\(f'\) is defined for all \(x\)" is cheap and correct. Q15.5 has two, and drill 2 has one.
  5. Getting \((-1)^{4/3}\) wrong. Cube root first: \((-1)^{5/3} = -1\) and \((-1)^{4/3} = +1\). Odd numerator keeps the sign, even numerator kills it. In floating point the naive decimal exponent returns a complex number whose modulus is right and whose value is wrong.
  6. Solving \(x^{1/3} = 2\) as \(x = 6\). \(f(6) = -14.890491\), which is 1.11 above the true minimum, so this one does show.
  7. Reaching for the second-derivative test. Unusable at part (a)'s \(x=0\) where \(f''\) blows up, silent at part (b)'s \(\pi/2\) where \(f''=0\). The closed-interval method needs no derivative test, and the sign chart does step 9 for both parts.
  8. Reading \(f''=0\) as "an inflection, therefore not an extreme". On part (b) that reaches the right conclusion by luck. It is invalid in general: \(x^4\) has \(f''(0)=0\) and a genuine minimum.
  9. Forgetting a tie. When a value is attained at more than one point, name every one. Three of the twelve drills tie, and so does Q15.3.
  10. Confusing "all maxima and minima" with "the absolute extreme values". The first needs step 9 and the second does not. The stem wording is the instruction.
