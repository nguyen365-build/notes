The Hand-Off

The acceleration is given in two pieces, so the velocity and the position have to be built one piece at a time. The whole question is the moment between the pieces: the speed and the distance the sprinter has at the end of phase 1 are the starting values for phase 2. Eight questions in this category let you forget that constant. This one does not.

A sprinter in a 100 m race explodes out of the starting block with an acceleration of 4 m/s2, which she sustains for 2 seconds. Her acceleration then drops to zero for the rest of the race. What is her time for the race?

The whole method in five steps

  1. Write the initial state. "Explodes out of the starting block" means \(v(0) = 0\) and \(x(0) = 0\). Write both down.
  2. Phase 1, on \([0,2]\), with \(a = 4\). Integrate once for \(v = 4t\), again for \(x = 2t^2\).
  3. Evaluate at the boundary. \(v(2) = 8\) m/s and \(x(2) = 8\) m. This line is the hand-off, and it is very likely the line the marker is looking for, though that is inference: no rubric exists.
  4. Phase 2, on \([2, T]\), with \(a = 0\). Zero acceleration means the velocity stops changing, not that it becomes zero. So \(v = 8\) still, and \(x = 8 + 8(t-2)\). The two eights are the numbers from step 3.
  5. Solve. \(8 + 8(T-2) = 100\), so \(T - 2 = \tfrac{92}{8} = 11.5\) and \(T = 13.5\) seconds.
Eight-second check. Her top speed is 8 m/s, so she cannot beat \(100/8 = 12.5\) s. She also does better than standing still for 2 s and then cruising, which would take 14.5 s. And 13.5 is the midpoint of those two, which is not a coincidence.

The picture is the answer

On a velocity-time graph, distance is the area underneath. The ramp encloses a triangle and the cruise encloses a rectangle, and those two areas are the 8 m and the 92 m. You can read the whole solution off the shape without integrating anything.

The reason the previous question was easier

Q19.8 gave you \(v\) and asked for displacement. Displacement is \(x(3) - x(0)\), so whatever \(x(0)\) was, it subtracted itself away, and a student who never wrote it down still got full marks. Q19.9 gives you \(a\) and asks for a race time, which is a position question wearing a disguise. Both constants are now load-bearing. This is the same omission finally being caught.

What must be continuous, and what may jump

A body cannot teleport, and it cannot change speed instantaneously without infinite force. Those two sentences are the whole content of "\(x\) and \(v\) are continuous", and they are why the hand-off values are what they are.

The ramp costs exactly half its own duration

Work the algebra in symbols instead of numbers and something clean falls out. If a body starts from rest, accelerates uniformly for \(T_1\) seconds to a top speed \(v_{\text{top}}\), and then cruises to a line at distance \(L\):

$$T = \frac{L}{v_{\text{top}}} + \frac{T_1}{2}$$

The acceleration does not appear at all. Only the ramp's duration and the top speed matter, and the ramp costs you exactly half of its own length in time. Here that is \(\tfrac{100}{8} + \tfrac{2}{2} = 12.5 + 1 = 13.5\).

It also explains the check. The slowest she could be is standing still for the ramp and then cruising; the fastest is cruising the whole way. The true answer sits exactly halfway between them, always.

When the law does not apply

Check first that the finish line falls in the cruise phase. If the ramp is long enough that she crosses the line while still accelerating, the law is simply wrong: for \(a = 4\) sustained 20 s it predicts 11.25 s against a true \(\sqrt{50} \approx 7.0711\) s. A rolling start or a head start also breaks it, because it assumes she begins from rest at the line.

Every way to get this wrong

Thirty-two methods were enumerated and each one solved exactly, with no candidate excluded for being silly: three correct routes and twenty-nine wrong ones. The result is unusual for this category.

Nothing hides

Zero of the twenty-nine wrong methods returns 13.5. Every other question in this block had at least one mistake that produced the right number for the wrong reason, and one of them had eight. Here there are none. But that is not the same as the wrong answers being informative.

Twenty-six of the wrong methods produce a number at all, and between them those twenty-six produce only eleven distinct values. Five of those values are each reached by two to five completely different mistakes.

Call it wrong-answer clustering, and it is the seventh distinct behaviour this category has produced. It is the mirror image of the previous question, where eleven wrong methods landed on a number the student had already written down and believed. Here no mistake reaches the truth, but a wrong number is nearly useless as a diagnosis.

What that means for recovery

If you get 12.5, do not go hunting for a lost sign. Ask the two questions that actually separate the five methods that give it: did I use phase 1 at all, and did I compute \(x(2)\) with both the one-half and the square.

Three mistakes produce no number at all

Restarting the velocity at zero in phase 2 leaves her at rest with zero acceleration, so she never moves again and the equation has no solution. That is a loud failure: your algebra dies in front of you. Restarting the position at zero is the quiet one, and it gives 14.5.

Positivity is the check most students reach for first, and on this stem it catches nothing at all: every wrong method here returns a positive time.

The one survivor

The single wrong answer the sandwich cannot reject is 14.0 s, from computing \(x(2)\) as \(\tfrac12 a t = 4\) instead of \(\tfrac12 a t^2 = 8\). It sits inside the physically possible band, so no plausibility check can catch it. Only the units catch it: \(\tfrac12 a t\) is a velocity, not a length. Run your dimensional check on the intermediate quantities, not on the final answer, because the answer and every plausible error are all times.

The traps, ranked

  1. Restarting phase 2 from zero. The defining error. Restarting \(x\) gives 14.5; restarting \(v\) gives no answer at all.
  2. Answering 11.5. The phase-2 duration is a number you computed and believed one line before the answer, and five different wrong methods also land there.
  3. Dropping the one-half or the square in \(x(2)\). Gives 16 or 4 instead of 8, and the resulting 14.0 is the one wrong answer no plausibility check rejects.
  4. Treating \(a\) as constant over the whole race. Gives \(\sqrt{50} \approx 7.0711\) s, which is badly wrong but sounds plausible for a sprint.
  5. Using the half-ramp law without checking the phase.
  6. Wrong limits on the phase-2 integral. \(\int_0^t\) instead of \(\int_2^t\) shifts the clock and gives 11.5.
  7. Forgetting to check whether she finishes at all when a later phase decelerates.
  8. "\(a = 0\), so nothing happens." Zero acceleration is not zero motion, it is unchanged motion. This misconception is the root of trap 1.

Closing the category, and the bank

This is the ninth of the nine applications-of-integration questions and the forty-ninth of forty-nine in the bank. All nine are the same three moves.

  1. Name the rate and the quantity. Something is given as a rate of change and something else is asked for.
  2. Build the integrand. Turn the physical description into an expression in one variable, with the right limits. Nearly all the marks are here.
  3. Integrate, then answer the question that was asked, not the one that is easiest to compute.

One sentence for the whole category

The integral of a rate of change is the net change in the quantity. Everything else is deciding what the rate is, over what interval, and in what pieces.
