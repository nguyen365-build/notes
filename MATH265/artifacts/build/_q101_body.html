MATH 265 · Midterm 1, item 8 · 5 points

The y-prime Ledger

Implicit differentiation is not a new rule. It is the chain rule applied to a function nobody gave you a formula for, and every mark on this question is won or lost by whether four terms in a row remember that.

Find \(y'\) using implicit differentiation:   \(x^3 y + x y^2 = 4xy + 7\)

The answer
\(y' = \dfrac{4y - 3x^2 y - y^2}{x^3 + 2xy - 4x}\)

It contains both \(x\) and \(y\), and that is correct, not unfinished. Factored, it is \(\dfrac{y(4-3x^2-y)}{x(x^2+2y-4)}\).

Type into Möbius
(4*y-3*x^2*y-y^2)/(x^3+2*x*y-4*x)

Bracket the whole numerator and the whole denominator. Leave y in as a variable. Preview before submitting.

01The one idea

If you take away one sentence, take this one.

\(y\) is secretly \(y(x)\). So differentiating a \(y\) is a composite-function problem, and the \(y'\) that appears is nothing more exotic than the inner derivative.

You already do this without noticing. When you write \(\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2) = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\), the \(2x\) is the derivative of the inside. When you write \(\frac{d}{dx}[y^2] = 2y\cdot y'\), the \(y'\) is the derivative of the inside. Same rule, same reason, same line of your own notes from six weeks ago.

Why this matters more than five marks

MT1 item 7, the gravel cone, asks you to get \(\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{4}h^2\frac{dh}{dt}\). That \(\frac{dh}{dt}\) is the same object as this \(y'\), arriving for the same reason. Two items on the same paper, five points each, one idea. Related rates is this question wearing a hat.

02The term ledger

This is the whole method as a bookkeeping exercise. Each term of the equation produces one or two pieces, and each piece either carries a \(y'\) or it does not.

Read the two right-hand columns as the two sides of the answer. The \(y'\) column becomes the denominator. The no-\(y'\) column becomes the numerator, with its signs flipped as it crosses the equals sign.

Step 1 · differentiate

\(3x^2y + x^3y' + y^2 + 2xy\,y' = 4y + 4xy'\)

Both sides, term by term, exactly as printed. Move nothing first.

Step 2 · split

\(x^3y' + 2xy\,y' - 4xy' = 4y - 3x^2y - y^2\)

Every \(y'\) piece left, everything else right. Flip signs as they cross.

Step 3 · factor

\(y'\left(x^3+2xy-4x\right) = 4y - 3x^2y - y^2\)

The bracket is the \(y'\) column of the ledger, verbatim.

Step 4 · divide, then STOP

\(y' = \dfrac{4y-3x^2y-y^2}{x^3+2xy-4x}\)

Do not try to eliminate \(y\). It cannot be done and it is not wanted.

The two-second audit that catches almost everything

Three of the four terms are mixed: they hold an \(x\) and a \(y\) at once. A mixed term always produces exactly two pieces. So after differentiating you must have \(3\times 2 = 6\) terms. Count them: \(3x^2y,\; x^3y',\; y^2,\; 2xy\,y',\; 4y,\; 4xy'\). Six. If you have five, you dropped a \(y'\), and that is the single most common way this question is failed.

03Why the answer contains \(y\)

Students delete the \(y\) because the answer looks unfinished. Here is the picture that shows why it cannot be deleted.

An implicit equation usually defines more than one function of \(x\). This one is a quadratic in \(y\), namely \(xy^2 + (x^3-4x)y - 7 = 0\), so above almost every \(x\) there are two points on the curve, with two different slopes. No formula in \(x\) alone could report both. The \(y\) is what tells them apart.

FIGURE_SVG

At \(x = 2\) the \(x^3y\) and \(4xy\) terms cancel exactly, leaving \(2y^2 = 7\), so \(y = \pm\frac{\sqrt{14}}{2}\). Feed each into the answer and the general formula collapses to \(y' = \frac{-8-y}{4}\), giving \(-2-\frac{\sqrt{14}}{8}\) and \(-2+\frac{\sqrt{14}}{8}\). Both slopes were confirmed three independent ways.

The same fact, run backwards, is your fastest check

\(x=2\) is the easy point on this curve because two terms annihilate each other there. Substitute \(x=2\) into your denominator before simplifying: \(8 + 4y - 8\) must collapse to \(4y\). If it does not, you have mis-transcribed a coefficient, and you know that in about four seconds without a calculator.

05Answer-entry rules

Möbius is answer-only and machine-marked, so a right derivative with a bad keystroke scores zero.

The most expensive typo here is the missing bracket around the numerator. 4*y-3*x^2*y-y^2/(...) renders as \(4y-3x^2y-\frac{y^2}{\,\cdots\,}\), which looks plausible and is wrong. The second most expensive is entering the factored form as y*(4-3*x^2-y)/x*(x^2+2*y-4), where the trailing bracket multiplies instead of dividing. Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence, not string match, so the expanded form is always a safe choice and it has fewer brackets to get wrong.

06The variation catalog

Read this as structure, not as fifteen facts. Every family below is the same four steps. What changes is only which rule step 1 needs, and there are exactly five possibilities: power, product, quotient, chain, and a nesting of those. Every derivative shown was checked numerically at a point first confirmed to lie on its curve.

Family 1

Pure powers, no mixed term

Trigger: \(x\) and \(y\) never multiply each other.

\(x^2+y^2=25 \Rightarrow y' = -\frac{x}{y}\). Also \(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}=4 \Rightarrow y'=-\frac{\sqrt y}{\sqrt x}\), and \(y^2=x^3(2-x) \Rightarrow y' = \frac{6x^2-4x^3}{2y}\).

The circle is the cheapest proof that a \(y\) in the answer is normal.

Family 2

A mixed product term: this is Q10.1

Trigger: \(x\) and \(y\) side by side.

\(xy=1 \Rightarrow -\frac{y}{x}\).   \(x^2+xy+y^2=7 \Rightarrow -\frac{2x+y}{x+2y}\).   \(x^3+y^3=6xy \Rightarrow \frac{6y-3x^2}{3y^2-6x}\).   \(x^2y^3-x=6 \Rightarrow \frac{1-2xy^3}{3x^2y^2}\).

The folium is the classic exam version: it is symmetric, so swapping \(x\) and \(y\) must turn \(y'\) into \(1/y'\). That is a free check.

Family 3

A quotient containing \(y\)

Trigger: a \(y\) in a denominator.

\(\frac{x}{y}+\frac{y}{x}=3 \Rightarrow y' = \frac{y}{x}\), which is startlingly simple until you notice the relation is homogeneous: with \(t=y/x\) it reads \(t+1/t=3\), so \(t\) is constant and the "curve" is a pair of straight lines through the origin.

Do not use the quotient rule here. Multiply through by the denominator first, then differentiate. It is faster every time.

Family 4

\(y\) inside a named function

Trigger: \(\sin y\), \(\cos y\), \(\tan y\), \(e^y\), \(\ln y\).

\(\sin y + x = y \Rightarrow y' = \frac{1}{1-\cos y}\).   \(x\sin y = y \Rightarrow y' = -\frac{\sin y}{x\cos y - 1}\).

The second is Family 4 and Family 2 at once, which is the commonest way an examiner raises difficulty without changing topic.

Family 5

\(x\) and \(y\) inside the same bracket

Trigger: \((x+y)^n\), \(\sin(xy)\), \(\tan(xy)\).

\((x+y)^3=x \Rightarrow y' = \frac{1-3(x+y)^2}{3(x+y)^2}\), because the inner derivative is \(1+y'\).   \(\tan(xy)=x \Rightarrow y' = \frac{1-y\sec^2(xy)}{x\sec^2(xy)}\).

The most-failed family. The \(y'\) arrives one level down and is easy to lose. Write the inner derivative on its own line before you multiply.

Family 6

The slope at a given point

Trigger: "find \(y'\) at \((a,b)\)".

Q10.1 unchanged, then substitute both coordinates. Collect first, substitute last.

Check the point is actually on the curve before you use it. On Q10.1, \((1,\,4.5413813)\) is on the curve and gives \(-2.6439899\); \((1,4)\) is not, and any slope you compute there is meaningless.

Family 7

The tangent line: this is Q10.2

Trigger: "find the equation of the tangent line".

\(y^3+yx^2+x^2=3y^2\) at \((1,1)\) gives \(y' = \frac{-(2xy+2x)}{3y^2+x^2-6y} = \frac{-4}{-2} = 2\), so the tangent is \(y = 2x-1\).

Answer with the line, not the slope. Answering with the wrong kind of object is the largest single loss on the Q9.1 family too.

Family 8

Horizontal and vertical tangents

Trigger: "where is the tangent vertical?"

Numerator zero for horizontal, denominator zero for vertical, and in both cases check the other one is not zero.

This is section 04's table, and it is the family the course notes never mention despite setting it as a question.

Family 9

The second derivative

Trigger: "find \(y''\)".

Quotient-rule your \(y'\), then substitute the expression for \(y'\) back in. That substitution is the entire family.

On the circle: \(y''=-\frac{y-xy'}{y^2} = -\frac{y^2+x^2}{y^3} = -\frac{r^2}{y^3}\), and the middle step is where the substitution happens.

Family 10

Swap the roles, or add a third variable

Trigger: "find \(dx/dy\)", or anything with "how fast".

\(\frac{dx}{dy}\) is just \(1/y'\), and it equals \(0\) at a vertical tangent, which is the clean way to state one without dividing by zero.

For related rates the chain factor is \(\frac{dh}{dt}\) instead of \(y'\) and nothing else changes. Doing Q10.1 means you have already done the hard half of category 11.

07Where the marks actually go

Ranked by how often each really costs you. The first four are one mistake wearing four coats: a \(y\) got differentiated as though it were a constant, or as though it were \(x\).

  1. Forgetting the \(y'\) on one termYou have five terms where you should have six. Count them; each mixed term must yield exactly two.
  2. Forgetting the product rule on a mixed term\(\frac{d}{dx}[x^3y]\) written as \(3x^2y'\) or as \(3x^2y\). Either way you lost half of it.
  3. Algebra when collectingA sign that did not flip crossing the equals sign. Move one term at a time, and re-read the line you just wrote.
  4. Forgetting the chain factor inside \(y^2\)\(\frac{d}{dx}[xy^2]\) written as \(y^2+2xy\). \(y^2\) is a composite; its derivative is \(2y\cdot y'\), always.
  5. Treating the answer as unfinishedTwo branches, two slopes, so the \(y\) has to stay. Section 03 is the picture of why.
  6. Losing the \(y'\) one level down\((x+y)^3\) differentiated as \(3(x+y)^2\). Write the inner derivative \(1+y'\) on its own line.
  7. Using a check point that is not on the curveYour numeric check disagrees and you doubt correct work. Verify the point satisfies the relation before you use it.
  8. Calling a vertical tangent an errorDenominator zero with numerator non-zero is a real feature of the curve, not a slip.
  9. Solving for \(y\) firstThe stem says using implicit differentiation. Right slope, method mark lost.
  10. Leaving a constant in the answer\(\frac{d}{dx}[7]=0\), always. A 7 anywhere in your \(y'\) is a copy error.
Measured, not asserted, and this curve is unusually forgiving

Five of those mistakes were built as working formulas and compared against the truth at 44 points on the curve. Worst relative deviations: 15.5 (no \(y'\) in \(x^3y\)), 5.01 (no chain factor on \(y^2\)), 1.96 (no product rule), 3.92 (sign slip), 122 (no \(y'\) in \(4xy\)).

None of them has a hiding place on this curve. The two "forgot the \(y'\)" errors differ from the truth only by an \(x^3\) or a \(-4x\) in the denominator, so they would agree exactly at \(x=0\), and \(x=0\) is not on this curve, because \(F(0,y) = -7\) for every \(y\). One numeric check anywhere legitimate catches all five.

That is a property of this curve, not of the topic. Control, verified: on \(x^2+y^2=25\) the analogous mistake gives \(y'=-x\) instead of \(-x/y\), and those agree exactly at \(x=0\), the top of the circle and the most convenient point on it. The convenient input is the blind spot. Check somewhere awkward.

09How this page was checked

There is no explicit \(f(x)\) here, so the usual two-arm numeric check does not apply directly. It was replaced by a residual gate plus three arms, none of which ever sees the claimed formula.

320 assertions, 0 failures, of which 15 are seeded controls proving the gates can bite. Coverage: 44 on-curve points over 22 values of \(x\), both branches at every \(x\), spanning \(x\in[0.25,\,12]\) on the right component and \(x\in[-25,\,-2.70]\) on the left. The ladder's measured error ratios were 15.94 / 15.98 / 15.99 at \(x=2\) and 16.62 / 16.15 / 16.04 at \(x=-3\), against a theoretical 16 for a fourth-order method.

Two defects, both in the checking, neither in the mathematics

The first draft sampled points that are not on the curve. The discriminant is \(x\left[x(x^2-4)^2+28\right]\), which is negative for \(-2.688 < x < 0\), so the whole initial negative-\(x\) sample was empty and the run died on a square root of a negative number. The curve exists only for \(x>0\) or \(x \le -2.688358\). A slope comparison at a point that is not on the curve measures nothing, which is why the residual gate has to run first and has to be believed.

A fixed tolerance on a fourth-order ladder is calibrated on the flattest sample point. A \(2\times10^{-7}\) bound passed at \(x=2\) and failed at \(x=-3\) by \(6\times10^{-7}\), purely because the branch is steeper there. The tolerance now scales as \(h^4\), and three seeded \(\pm 0.1\%\) perturbations confirm it can still reject a wrong slope.
