Nine Is Not Evidence

A rod 6 m long is at $3x$ degrees C at distance $x$ from one end. Its average temperature is 9 degrees C. So is the answer you get by averaging the two ends, by dividing by 6 instead of by $6-0$, by reading the temperature at the middle, and by five other wrong methods. This page is about the one check that still works.

1. An average is the height of the equal-area rectangle

That one sentence generates the formula, so it is the thing to remember.

$$f_{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$$

The area under $T = 3x$ from 0 to 6 is a right triangle of base 6 and height 18, so it is 54. A rectangle 6 wide with area 54 must be 9 tall. That is the whole calculation, and it needs no antiderivative at all.

The figure below is the argument, not an illustration of it. Draw the triangle, then lay the rectangle over it. What is left over is two wedges, one inside the rectangle and one inside the triangle, and they are congruent right triangles of area 13.5 each. Equal leftovers is exactly what "same area" means, and the place where the line crosses the rectangle's top edge is the point at which the rod actually reaches its average.

2. Why you divide by the interval length

Three readings of the same division. The third one is the one worth carrying, because it is the only one that explains why the denominator is $b-a$ and not $b$ or $n$.

READINGTHE STATEMENTWHAT IT RULES OUT
Geometric$f_{\text{avg}}$ is the height of the equal-area rectangleReporting 54, which is an area
Dimensional$\int f\,dx$ carries the units of $f$ times a length; dividing by a length gives the units of $f$ backAny answer in degrees C times metres
Discrete limitThe plain mean of $n$ samples is $\frac{1}{n}\sum f(x_i) = \frac{1}{b-a}\sum f(x_i)\,\Delta x$, which tends to $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$Any answer that is not a weighted mean of the values of $f$

Verified directly rather than asserted: the mean of $n$ midpoint samples of $3x$ on $[0,6]$ returns 9 at $n = 10$, 100, 1000, 10000 and 100000, and the one-sided sample means bracket 9 from both sides with the error shrinking by a measured factor of exactly 10 per tenfold refinement, which is the $O(1/n)$ of a one-sided rule.

3. The average of a function is not the average of its two end values

$\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}$ is the mean of two numbers. $f_{\text{avg}}$ is the mean of infinitely many. They agree exactly when $f$ is affine, and on this exam question $f$ is affine, so the mistake is invisible.

The three panels below are the mechanism. All three functions agree at both endpoints, so all three share the same chord and the same endpoint mean of 0.50. Only the straight one has its true average on that level. For a convex function the chord sits above the curve and the endpoint mean comes out too high; for a concave one it sits below and the mean comes out too low.

4. The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ there is a $c$ in $[a,b]$ with $f(c) = \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$, equivalently $\int_a^b f = f(c)(b-a)$.

The rectangle in figure 1 is not a fiction. Its top edge crosses the curve, and the crossing is that $c$. Here $3c = 9$ gives $c = 3$ metres, so the rod really is at 9 degrees at the 3 m mark.

Is $c$ the midpoint? Here yes, and that is another degeneracy of this stem rather than a general fact. For $f = x^p$ on $[0,6]$ the average is $\frac{6^p}{p+1}$ and $c = 6/(p+1)^{1/p}$, which drifts right as the function gets more convex, because a convex function spends more of the interval below its average and has to climb further to reach it.

Strict positivity is not safe here. It rejects row 10's value of 0, and 0 is a legal value of this $f$, so "the average is positive" over-rejects. Non-negativity is the honest form, and the sandwich is a strict superset of it. This is the third instance in category 19 of the pattern first measured on Q19.4, positivity near-inert and the bracket helping, but the bracket's reach collapses here: 3 of 23, against Q19.5's 14 of 20, and it cannot touch a single one of the eight hiders because every one of them equals the truth.

The conclusion, which is the reason this page exists. On this question no check that looks only at the answer can help you, because the wrong answers are the right answer. The only discriminator is the method: did you write $(b-a)$, and did you divide.

6. The two checks that still work

1The sandwich. The rod runs from 0 to 18 degrees, so the answer must land in $[0,18]$. This is the comparison property of Q17.1, item 9 on this same paper, with the width divided out: $m \le f \le M$ on $[a,b]$ gives $m \le f_{\text{avg}} \le M$. Here 9 sits 9 above the floor and 9 below the ceiling, exactly halfway, and that halfway position is a property of an affine $f$: $x^1$ sits at 0.5 of the band, $x^2$ at $\frac13$, $x^3$ at $\frac14$.
2The units. The constant 3 is a gradient in degrees C per metre; $3x$ is a temperature; $\int_0^6 3x\,dx$ is degrees C times metres, which is not a temperature; dividing by 6 metres gives a temperature back. If your answer carries degrees times metres you skipped the division, and no amount of re-checking the arithmetic will find it. The gate was seeded: it rejects degrees C times metres, degrees C per metre, metres per degree C, and the inhomogeneous sum $T + x$, and accepts the correct relation.
3And the limit of both. Dividing by $b$ instead of $b-a$ is dimensionally correct, because both are lengths, and on this stem it is also numerically correct. Nothing cheap catches it. What catches it is writing $b-a$ symbolically before you substitute.

How $m$ and $M$ were established, and why it needed three arms. A complex-step derivative at 401 points gives $f' = 3$ with maximum deviation below $10^{-12}$, so there are no interior critical points; a sign-change sweep of $f'$ at four grid sizes finds none; and a left-versus-right difference-quotient scan at 800 interior points finds no kink, so there is no point where $f'$ fails to exist either. The census was controlled both directions, on $3x - \frac12 x^2$, on $x^3-9x$ over two intervals, and on $|x-2|$ against $\sin x$.

One finding from that census worth carrying. On an even grid the critical point of the control function is a node, so $f'$ is exactly zero there, the strict sign-change test skips it, and the census reports 0. On an odd grid it reports 1. A sign-change census must count exact grid zeros as well as strict changes, and be run at both parities, or grid parity silently decides your answer.

7. The average of a function versus the average rate of change

These are the two objects a student most often merges, and they are averages of different things over different objects.

They coincide exactly when the $f$ you are averaging is the derivative of the $P$ whose rate you are quoting, which is the Fundamental Theorem in one sentence. That is why the average value of a velocity equals the average rate of change of position, and it is why Q19.3's water-tank rate and Q19.8's velocity can be done either way.

Applying Q6.1's formula to this question gives $\frac{18-0}{6-0} = 3$, which is the temperature gradient in degrees C per metre, not the average temperature. That is census row 11, and 3 is one of the piled-up wrong values.

Family 12 is the cleanest explanation of the whole formula. A weighted average is $\frac{\int f w}{\int w}$, and a plain average is the case $w \equiv 1$, where the denominator is exactly $b-a$. So $(b-a)$ is not an arbitrary divisor: it is the total weight. An average is always the total of the thing over the total of the weight, and for a plain average the weight is length.

Family 11's first instance is a genuine hazard. The mean of an arithmetic progression equals its middle term, so sampling a linear temperature at equally spaced points including both ends returns the true average exactly. It is right for the wrong reason, and it breaks the moment $T$ curves.

9. Traps, ranked

  1. Writing 54. Forgetting the division is the most common error on every average-value question ever set, and it is the only one of the four named errors this stem catches. It is caught twice over, by the units and by the band.
  2. Averaging the endpoint values, and writing 9. Right here and wrong almost everywhere else. If you got 9 this way you have learned nothing, and family 2 will end you.
  3. Dividing by $b$ instead of $b-a$, and writing 9. Also right here, because $a = 0$. A sweep over 501 left endpoints with the width held at 6 found the two agree at exactly one value of $a$, namely 0.
  4. Using the midpoint value, and writing 9. Right here because $f$ is affine. The shortcut is legitimate only with the affineness stated.
  5. Reporting the MVT point instead of the average, or the reverse. On this question the two are 3 and 9, so a slip is a whole mark.
  6. Confusing the average value with the average rate of change. Gives 3, which is a plausible-looking number and is actually the gradient.
  7. Dropping the $\frac12$ in the antiderivative. $\int 3x\,dx = \frac32 x^2$, not $3x^2$. Gives 18, which is also $f(6)$, so it looks defensible.
  8. Averaging $m$ and $M$. Gives 9 here, because $f$ is affine and monotone. It is trap 2 in a different hat.
  9. Reversing the limits. Gives $-9$, and it is the one error non-negativity catches.
  10. Averaging a nonlinear function of $T$. Averaging does not commute with squaring: the average of $T^2$ on this rod is 108, while the square of the average is 81.
