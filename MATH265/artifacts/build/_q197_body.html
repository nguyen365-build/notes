The Interval Runs Backwards

Find a positive $k$ such that the average of $f(x) = 5/x^2$ over the interval between 1 and $k$ is 32. The average works out to $5/k$, so $k = 5/32$ - which is less than 1, so the interval runs from $5/32$ up to 1, not from 1 up to $k$. This is Q19.6 read backwards, and running it backwards changes three things: you set up an equation instead of evaluating one, you have to decide whether the answer is unique, and your only check is substitution.

1. The average-value formula, read as an equation

Nothing about the formula changes. What changes is which symbol is unknown.

$$\frac{1}{k-a}\int_a^k f(x)\,dx \;=\; T$$

In Q19.6 everything on the left was known and you computed $T$. Here $T$ is given, $a = 1$ is given, and $k$ is what you are solving for. So you carry $k$ as a letter all the way through the integration and only put numbers in at the end.

1Write the constraint with $k$ still a letter.
2Integrate symbolically: $\int 5x^{-2}dx = -\dfrac{5}{x}$, so $\displaystyle\int_1^k = 5 - \dfrac{5}{k} = \dfrac{5(k-1)}{k}$.
3Look for the factor $(k-1)$ and cancel it. This is the step the whole question turns on.
4Solve what is left: $\dfrac{5}{k} = 32$, so $k = \dfrac{5}{32}$.
5Count the solutions, and use the stem's word "positive" to discard any others.
6Substitute back and recompute the average, not the integral.

The general statement, verified on 92 instances: the average of $\dfrac{c}{x^2}$ over the interval between $a$ and $k$ is $\dfrac{c}{a\,k}$. With $c=5$ and $a=1$ that is $5/k$, the exam's own equation. A control confirms it is false for $c/x^3$, so it is not a tautology.

2. One picture settles both the trap and the uniqueness

Plot the average against $k$. Because the average is $5/k$, the picture is a hyperbola, and everything the question asks can be read straight off it.

That third row is the cheapest guard on the question. A bigger average needs a smaller endpoint, because $f$ is large near zero. Saying that sentence out loud kills the single most common wrong answer, $32/5 = 6.4$, in about two seconds.

3. Reversing the limits changes nothing, and that is worth knowing

Suppose you never noticed that $k < 1$ and wrote the interval as $[1,k]$ anyway. Then

$$\frac{1}{k-1}\int_1^k \frac{5}{x^2}\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{1-k}\int_k^1 \frac{5}{x^2}\,dx \;=\; \frac{5}{k}$$

Reversing the limits flips the sign of the integral and the sign of the width, and the two flips cancel. Verified at 501 sampled values of $k$, worst mismatch below $10^{-9}$.

So the orientation error is the one candidate in the census that returns the right answer, and it hides structurally rather than by luck. It costs you nothing on this stem. It costs you everything on a follow-up that asks which interval you used, or asks for the mean-value point, because then you have to name $\left[\frac{5}{32},1\right]$ correctly.

4. Uniqueness has to be argued, not assumed

It is tempting to solve, get one answer, and move on. On this question that happens to be right. On a question one word away it is wrong.

The engine behind both panels is one derivative. Differentiating the average with respect to the moving endpoint gives

$$\frac{d}{dk}\,f_{\text{avg}}(k) \;=\; \frac{f(k) - f_{\text{avg}}(k)}{k - a}$$

which says the average moves toward the value you are adding. Verified numerically at 14 points across three integrands, worst mismatch $7.702\times10^{-9}$. If $f$ never crosses its own running average, the average is monotone and there is at most one solution. If $f$ dips below it and comes back, there can be two, or none.

5. The check you actually have, and what it cannot see

Put $k = \frac{5}{32}$ back in. The integral over $\left[\frac{5}{32},1\right]$ is $\left[-\frac{5}{x}\right] = -5+32 = 27$, the width is $\frac{27}{32}$, and $27 \div \frac{27}{32} = 32$.

This confirms the algebra of the solve, not the setup. If you set the integral equal to 32 instead of the average, got $k = \frac{5}{37}$, and then "checked" by recomputing the integral, it would agree with itself perfectly. The check only bites when you recompute the average - integral divided by width - from scratch.

This stem does not inherit Q19.6's degeneracies, and that was swept rather than assumed. Q19.6 had eight hiders, traced to two properties of its stem: $f$ affine, and the fixed endpoint $a = 0$. Neither holds here. $5/x^2$ is not affine, so the endpoint mean gives $104.9$ and the midpoint value gives $14.9598246896$ where the truth is 32. The fixed endpoint is 1, not 0, so dividing by the endpoint instead of the width matches the truth at zero of 500 positive left endpoints swept over $[0,5]$ - with a control confirming it does agree in the limit $a \to 0$, which is exactly Q19.6's case.

Two rows deserve a second look. C1 is the dangerous one: $\frac{5}{37} = 0.1351351351$ is positive, less than 1, and passes the sandwich, so only substitution catches it. And C15 lands exactly on the mean-value point $\sqrt{5/32}$, which is the $x$ where $f$ equals its average - a wrong answer that is a genuinely meaningful number, which is what makes it feel right.

9. Units, and the gate's named blind spot

Write $[x] = L$ and $[f] = F$. Then the constant 5 carries $F\!\cdot\!L^2$, the integral carries $F\!\cdot\!L$, the average carries $F$, and $k$ carries $L$ - it is a length, so the answer is "$\frac{5}{32}$ of whatever unit $x$ is measured in", not a pure number.

A dimensional gate over these vectors accepts the correct relation and rejects four seeded candidates, including the interesting one: the expression "$5 - \frac{5}{k}$" exactly as written is inhomogeneous, because 5 carries $F L^2$ and $\frac{5}{k}$ carries $F L$. It is only legal as $\frac{5}{x_0} - \frac{5}{k}$ with $x_0 = 1$ understood as a length. The numeral 1 in "between 1 and $k$" is not a pure number.

A conversion arm confirms it: measure $x$ in centimetres, so the interval runs from 100 to 15.625 and $f$ becomes $50000/x^2$, and the average is still 32. Forgetting to rescale the constant gives 0.0032, a factor of $10^4$ out.

The blind spot, stated so silence is not mistaken for coverage. Dividing by $k$ instead of by the width $(1-k)$ is dimensionally legal - both are lengths - so the units gate structurally cannot see that error. Only substitution can. A gate's blind spot deserves an assertion of its own.
