Two Answers, One Integral

Displacement asks where the particle ended up. Distance asks what the odometer read. They are the same integral with one difference: whether you keep the sign. On this question the velocity changes sign twice inside the interval, so the two answers are forced apart, and eleven of the nineteen wrong ways to do part (b) hand back part (a)'s number.

A particle moves along a line so that its velocity at time \(t\) is \(v(t) = t^2 - 3t + 2\) (meter/sec). (a) Find the displacement of the particle during the time period \([0, 3]\). (b) Find the distance traveled during this same time period.

The whole method in four steps

  1. Factor. \(v = t^2-3t+2 = (t-1)(t-2)\), so \(v = 0\) at \(t=1\) and \(t=2\). Both are strictly inside \([0,3]\), which is the sentence that decides the mark.
  2. Antidifferentiate once. \(S(t) = \dfrac{t^3}{3} - \dfrac{3t^2}{2} + 2t\), and \(S' = v\) checks in one line.
  3. Part (a) needs no split. \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 v\,dt = S(3)-S(0) = 9 - \tfrac{27}{2} + 6 = \tfrac{3}{2}\).
  4. Part (b) splits at every sign change. The three pieces are \(\tfrac{5}{6}\), \(-\tfrac{1}{6}\) and \(\tfrac{5}{6}\); add their absolute values to get \(\tfrac{11}{6}\).

Eight-second check. distance \(-\) displacement \(= \tfrac{11}{6} - \tfrac{3}{2} = \tfrac{1}{3}\), which is exactly twice the one negative piece. If the gap is not twice something you already computed, a piece was dropped or double-counted.

Why the difference is twice the negative part

Split the interval into the part where \(v \ge 0\) and the part where \(v < 0\). Write \(A\) for the integral over the first and \(B \le 0\) for the integral over the second.

\[\text{displacement} = A + B, \qquad \text{distance} = A - B, \qquad \text{distance} - \text{displacement} = 2\lvert B\rvert.\]

So the two answers agree exactly when \(B = 0\), that is, exactly when the velocity never changes sign on the interval. Nothing else matters: not the degree of \(v\), not whether a root exists, only whether a root lies inside and the sign actually flips there.

Eleven of the nineteen wrong values for part (b) are exactly \(\tfrac{3}{2}\), the correct answer to part (a). Call it a cross-part collision. It is worse than a scattered wrong answer, because the number is one you have already written down and checked, and every cheap sanity test it meets, it passes. The last two rows are the sharp ones. A double root is a zero where the sign does not flip, and it splits nothing — the condition is a sign change, not a zero. And the sine row shows the extreme: zero displacement with a positive distance. The ratio has no upper bound.

What the numerical check actually does

\(\lvert v\rvert\) has corners at \(t=1\) and \(t=2\), so its quadrature behaviour is not the textbook one. It was measured, not assumed.

RuleAlignmentObserved order Evidence
composite Simpson\(n\) a multiple of 6 exacterror 0 at every \(n\) from 6 to 768
composite Simpson\(3 \nmid n\)3 error \(\times\, n^3 = 5.333333\) at \(n = 200\) through 3200
Gauss-Legendre, 16 nodesunsplit2 1.9747, 2.0126, 1.9937, 2.0032
any rulesplit at \(t=1,2\)exact error \(2.2 \times 10^{-16}\)

Simpson is exact when the corners land on even node indices. With \(h = 3/n\) the corner at \(t=1\) sits at node index \(n/3\); Simpson pairs its panels, so a corner only hurts when it falls strictly inside a pair.

The off-alignment order is 3, not the 2 you would expect, and that is an accident of this interval. The local Simpson error on a pair of half-width \(h\) with a corner at offset \(s h\) from the midpoint is \(e(s) = \tfrac{h^2}{3}(4\lvert s\rvert - 1 - 3s^2)\), which has a root at \(\lvert s\rvert = \tfrac{1}{3}\). On \([0,3]\) the corner index is always \(n/3\), so every even \(n\) not divisible by 3 puts it at exactly that offset — checked for all 1334 such \(n\) up to 4000. The leading term vanishes, and order 3 is what is left. Move the same integrand to \([0,\pi]\), where the corners fall at irrational fractions of a panel, and error \(\times\, n^2\) oscillates in the band [0.0094, 1.3000] without settling: that is the generic order 2.

The practical lesson is one line

Split at the corners. Unsplit 16-point Gauss-Legendre — a rule exact for polynomials up to degree 31 — is dragged to order 2 by a single corner and still carries an error of \(3.1 \times 10^{-6}\) at 40 panels. Splitting takes it to \(2.2 \times 10^{-16}\): ten orders of magnitude, for no extra work.

Read literally, \(v(t) = t^2 - 3t + 2\) is dimensionally inhomogeneous: for the sum to be a velocity, the numeral 1 must carry \(L\,T^{-3}\), the 3 must carry \(L\,T^{-2}\) and the 2 must carry \(L\,T^{-1}\). None of them is a pure number.

Named blind spot

The units gate structurally cannot tell part (a) from part (b): displacement and distance are both lengths. All eleven cross-part errors pass it cleanly. Only the sign-change argument catches them.

The trap list

  1. Answering part (b) with part (a)'s number. Eleven distinct wrong methods produce it, and nothing about the answer looks wrong.
  2. Never looking for the zeros. The upstream cause of trap 1. Factor \(v\) before anything else; it is a habit, not a technique.
  3. Splitting at one root, or at the vertex. Half the work, none of the benefit.
  4. Writing \(\lvert\int v\rvert\) instead of \(\int\lvert v\rvert\). Trap 1 in symbols.
  5. Forgetting the absolute values after correctly splitting. The most painful one: all the hard work done, and the answer is still \(\tfrac{3}{2}\).
  6. Forgetting an initial condition when the wording says position. Invisible here, fatal on Q19.9.
  7. Reading "speeding up" as \(a > 0\). It is \(v\) and \(a\) sharing a sign, so here that is \((1,\tfrac{3}{2})\) and \((2,3)\) — and on the first of those the acceleration is negative.
  8. Rounding \(\tfrac{11}{6}\). Möbius is machine-marked; 1.83 is not \(\tfrac{11}{6}\).
