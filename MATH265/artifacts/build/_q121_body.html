Approximate a square root with differentials

"Use differentials to find the approximate value of $\sqrt{9.2}$." One derivative, one well-chosen anchor, and one sentence about which way the answer leans. The anchor is the whole question.

The answer$3 + \tfrac{1}{30} = \tfrac{91}{30}$
As a decimal3.0333
Type into Mobius91/30
Which way it leanstoo big, by 0.00018

01 · The thirty-second version

Everything in this question is one line of setup and one derivative. If your route needs a lookup, you chose the wrong anchor.

1 · Name f

You are asked for a number that is $f$ of something. $\sqrt{9.2}$ means $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$.

2 · Choose the anchor

The nearest $a$ where $f(a)$ is exact and you know it cold. $9$ is a perfect square, so $\sqrt{9} = 3$. Then $dx = 0.2$.

3 · Differentiate once

$f'(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$, so $f'(9) = \dfrac{1}{6}$. Evaluate at the anchor, never at the target.

4 · Build dy and add

$dy = f'(9)\,dx = \dfrac{1}{30}$, so $\sqrt{9.2} \approx 3 + \dfrac{1}{30} = \dfrac{91}{30}$.

Then say which way it leans. $f''(x) = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0$, so $\sqrt{x}$ is concave down, so the tangent line sits above the curve, so the estimate is too big. It is, by $0.00018$. That sentence costs eight seconds and is the only part of the answer that shows you know what an approximation is.

02 · One formula, three costumes

The exam asks for this in three different wordings across three different papers. They are the same object.

Written asWhat it saysReach for it when

$dy = f'(a)\,dx$, with $\Delta y \approx dy$just the change, not the valueyou want an error or a change

These are not three formulas to memorise. $dy$ is the second one with $f(a)$ moved across. Q12.1 says "use differentials", Q12.2 says "use linearization", Q12.3 says "the method of differentials" — all three want the same five lines.

Category 12 is worth 19 points across three separate papers. Q12.1 is MT1 item 9 (5 points), Q12.2 is MT2 item 8 (6 points, estimate $\sin 62^{\circ}$), Q12.3 is Final Exam 1 item 2 (8 points, "to four decimal places"). Learning this once pays three times.

03 · Choosing the anchor is the whole craft

Step 2 is where the marks are, and it is the only step with a decision in it. The test is not "is $a$ a tidy number". The test is do I know $f(a)$ exactly, without a calculator.

FIGURE_SVG

Panel A is the geometric content of the whole topic: the linearization is the tangent line, and because $\sqrt{x}$ is concave down that line never dips below the curve. The vertical gap is the error. It is invisible at $x = 9.2$ and obvious at $x = 25$, because the error is second order — double the distance and the gap quadruples. Panel B turns that into a measurement. All six anchors are perfect squares, so all six are legal; $a = 9$ wins by three orders of magnitude over $a = 16$, purely because it is near.

Choose the nearest exact point

  • $a = 9$ for $\sqrt{9.2}$, because $\sqrt{9} = 3$ and $9$ is closest.
  • $a = 27$ for $\sqrt[3]{26.8}$, stepping down — a negative $dx$ is perfectly normal.
  • $a = \pi/3$ for $\sin 62^{\circ}$, in radians.
  • $a = 2$ for $(2.01)^5$, because $2^5 = 32$.

The tell that you chose well: $f'(a)$ comes out a unit fraction or a small integer. Every anchor in the catalog below does. If $f'(a)$ is ugly, look again.

Do not choose the prettier one

  • $a = 9.61 = 3.1^2$ is genuinely exact — and 3.94 times worse, because it is twice as far.
  • $a = 16$ is exact and 638 times worse.
  • $a = 10$ is nearest of all and unusable, because $\sqrt{10}$ is not a number you know.

Nearest wins, always. The error goes as $(dx)^2$, so distance is punished twice over. Exactness is the entry requirement; nearness is the tie-break.

04 · Four routes, and which to use under pressure

A · Differentials — the wording the exam uses

$$f(9) = 3,\quad f'(9) = \tfrac16,\quad dy = \tfrac16(0.2) = \tfrac{1}{30},\quad \sqrt{9.2} \approx \tfrac{91}{30}$$

B · Linearization — the wording Q12.2 uses

$$L(x) = 3 + \tfrac16(x-9),\qquad L(9.2) = 3 + \tfrac{1}{30}$$

C · Factor out the anchor

$$\sqrt{9.2} = 3\sqrt{1 + \tfrac{1}{45}} \approx 3\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{90}\right) = 3 + \tfrac{1}{30}$$

D · One Newton step — the same arithmetic

$$x_1 = \tfrac12\left(3 + \tfrac{9.2}{3}\right) = \tfrac{91}{30}$$

Route D is not a coincidence. Linearizing $\sqrt{x}$ at $a$ gives $\tfrac12\!\left(\sqrt a + \tfrac{N}{\sqrt a}\right)$, which is exactly one Babylonian step — verified identical to machine precision at ten separate $(a, N)$ pairs. That makes Q13.1, Newton's method, this question's neighbour, and it makes route D the best self-check available: it shares none of route A's algebra.

05 · Checking yourself in eight seconds

Bracket it

$3^2 = 9$ and $3.1^2 = 9.61$, so the answer lives in $(3,\,3.1)$. Kills every gross slip.

Square it back

$\left(\tfrac{91}{30}\right)^2 = 9.2011$, a little above 9.2 — which confirms the value and the overshoot at once.

Newton cross-check

$\tfrac12\!\left(3 + \tfrac{9.2}{3}\right) = 3.0\overline{3}$. Different algebra, same answer.

Is f'(a) clean?

$\tfrac16$ is a unit fraction. If yours is not, the anchor is wrong.

Never spot-check this method at the anchor itself. Setting $dx = 0$ makes five of the eight standard wrong answers agree with the truth exactly, because they all differ from it only by a factor multiplying $dy$ — and $dy$ is zero there. Verified over a 64-point sweep with the degenerate rows deliberately left in; removing them reports no blind spot at all. This is the same trap as the zero-rate blind spot in related rates, where a stationary pile makes every wrong cone formula give zero.

06 · What the estimate is actually worth

The error is second order in $dx$, and both its sign and its size are available without knowing the true value.

$$\text{true} - \text{estimate} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(dx)^2 \quad\text{for some }\xi\text{ between }a\text{ and }a+dx$$

Halve $dx$ and the error drops by four. Measured at $dx = 0.4,\ 0.2,\ 0.1,\ 0.05$: the ratio came out $4.00$ every time. And the direction comes from concavity alone, never from the sign of $dx$ — verified at 72 anchor-and-offset pairs in both directions, all 72 above the curve, with four convex functions seeded as controls to prove the test can report the opposite.

The bound uses the endpoint where $|f''|$ is largest, which is not always the anchor. For a root $|f''|$ falls as $x$ grows, so an anchor-based bound is valid when $dx > 0$ and invalid when $dx < 0$. Worked instance: estimating $\sqrt{48.6}$ from $a = 49$, the anchor bound is $5.8309 \times 10^{-5}$ and the actual error is $5.8548 \times 10^{-5}$ — the bound is violated. Using the far endpoint $48.6$ gives $5.9030 \times 10^{-5}$, which holds.

Final Exam 1 item 2 is worth 8 points and says: estimate each value to four decimal places. That phrase needs reading carefully, because on its own two parts the method cannot deliver four correct places.

So write the estimate to four decimals and stop. For this question that is 3.0333, not 3.0332. The question asked for the differential estimate, and the marker's own answer is the differential value. Do not "improve" your answer toward the true one.

One subtlety that looks like a contradiction: $(32.2)^{1/5}$ agrees with its true value at four places and disagrees at three, because $2.0025$ sits exactly on a three-place rounding tie. Rounding boundaries are not nested, so four-place agreement does not imply three-place agreement.

08 · The variation catalog

Eight families, grouped by the tool the variation forces rather than by how the question looks. Every estimate below was built from a closed form and recovered by a complex-step derivative that never sees the formula; every true value comes from an independent evaluation.

Read this as structure, not thirteen facts. Every $f'(a)$ is a unit fraction. Every error is negative, in both directions of travel, because a root is concave down. The errors sort exactly by $(dx)^2 / a^{3/2}$, so big steps off small anchors are the bad ones.

$\sqrt{0.26}$ is the trap row. At $a = 1/4$ the derivative is exactly $1$, so a student who forgets to multiply by $f'(a)$ still gets $0.5 + 0.01 = 0.51$ — the right answer for the wrong reason, confirmed bit-identical. The general rule, which recurs all over this course: a mistake hides wherever the factor it touches equals one.

Only two distinct increments appear in the whole family: $\pi/180 \approx 0.0174533$ and $\pi/90 \approx 0.0349066$. Learn those two and the family costs nothing. The signs are generated by concavity: on $(0, \pi/2)$ both $\sin$ and $\cos$ are concave down and overshoot, while $\tan$ is convex and undershoots — so "trig always overestimates" is false, and the two tan rows prove it.

The catastrophic slip is $dx = 2$ instead of $2^{\circ}$. That gives $\sin 62^{\circ} \approx 1.866$, which is not even in $[-1,1]$ and sits $0.98$ from the truth. If a trig estimate leaves $[-1,1]$, you forgot to convert.

One rule generates every direction in that table: $x^p$ is convex for $p > 1$ or $p < 0$ and concave for $0 < p < 1$. So every root overshoots and every whole power and reciprocal undershoots. $(8.06)^{2/3}$ is the only concave row, and the only "over".

$(1.02)^{10}$ is this family's warning. $dx = 0.02$ is tiny and the estimate is still 1.6 percent low, because $f''(1) = 90$. A small $dx$ does not by itself make a good estimate — the error is $\tfrac12 f''(dx)^2$, and $f''$ gets a vote.

Family 3A named f with the anchor supplied

Trigger: "Let $f(x) = \dfrac{x-1}{\sqrt{x}}$. (a) Find the tangent line at $(4, f(4))$. (b) Use differentials to estimate $f(4.02)$." Tool: nothing new — but notice that (a) and (b) are the same computation asked twice.

$$f = x^{1/2} - x^{-1/2},\quad f'(4) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac1{16} = \tfrac5{16},\quad f(4.02) \approx \tfrac32 + \tfrac1{160} = \tfrac{241}{160} = 1.50625$$

The tangent line is $y = \tfrac{5}{16}x + \tfrac14$, and evaluating it at $4.02$ gives the part-(b) answer exactly. True value $1.5062391$, so the estimate overshoots by $1.09 \times 10^{-5}$, as $f'' < 0$ predicts. Rewriting the quotient as $x^{1/2} - x^{-1/2}$ before differentiating is the only real work in the question — it avoids the quotient rule entirely.

09 · The trap list, ranked by what it actually costs

  1. Evaluating $f'$ at the target instead of the anchor. $f'(9.2)$ gives $3.0329690$, off by $+1.8\times10^{-4}$ — the same size as the correct answer's error, in the opposite direction, so it looks entirely plausible. Worse, it becomes invisible as $dx \to 0$, so shrinking the step cannot expose it. The anchor is where you know things; the target is where you want an answer, and both $f$ and $f'$ are evaluated at the anchor.
  2. Forgetting the $\tfrac12$ in $\tfrac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x}$. Gives $3.0667$, inside the plausible band and exactly twice the correct $dy$. Squaring back gives $9.404$, which kills it.
  3. Using degrees where the formula needs radians. $\sin 62^{\circ} \approx 1.866$. The tell is an answer outside $[-1,1]$.
  4. Choosing a prettier anchor rather than a nearer one. $a = 9.61 = 3.1^2$ is exact and 3.94 times worse; $a = 16$ is exact and 638 times worse. The error goes as $(dx)^2$, so distance is punished twice.
  5. Spot-checking at the anchor. $dx = 0$ hides five of the eight standard wrong answers.
  6. Reporting $dy$ instead of $f(a) + dy$. Answer $0.0333$. The bracket check catches it instantly.
  7. Sign slip on a negative $dx$. For $\sqrt{24.6}$ from 25, $dx = -0.4$; adding $+0.04$ gives $5.04$ instead of $4.96$. Write $dx = \text{target} - a$ and let the sign fall out.
  8. Claiming the true value because you checked on a calculator. The question asked for the estimate. Write $3.0333$.
  9. Getting the over/under backwards by reasoning from the sign of $dx$ instead of the sign of $f''$.
  10. The invisible one: $f'(a) = 1$. At $a = 1/4$ for $\sqrt{x}$, forgetting to multiply by $f'(a)$ is undetectable. Nothing catches this except doing it right.

The single most expensive typo is 1/2*sqrt(9) for $f'(9)$. It evaluates to $1.5$ rather than $0.1\overline{6}$ — a factor of nine — and yields $3.3$, which is still inside the plausible range for a square root and so will not look wrong. Write $f'(9)$ as 1/6 and the typo cannot happen.

The verification did not merely re-evaluate the answer. It parsed the provenance from the paper; proved $f(9) = 3$, $f'(9) = \tfrac16$ and $91/30$ in exact rational arithmetic; recovered $f'(9)$ formula-blind by a complex-step derivative and by a Richardson ladder with its convergence order measured on a curved control; computed $\sqrt{9.2}$ to 60 digits and squared it back; located the mean-value $\xi = 9.0997$ and the Lagrange $\xi = 9.0664$ by root-finding and confirmed both lie strictly inside $(9,\,9.2)$; proved the tangent line lies above $\sqrt{x}$ at 72 anchor-and-offset pairs in both directions, with four convex functions seeded as controls to prove the test can report the opposite; ranked ten competing anchors by measured error; and swept eight plausible wrong answers over a 64-point grid with the degenerate rows left in, which is what found the $dx = 0$ and $a = 1/4$ blind spots.

All 30 apparent errors during the run turned out to be defects in the check rather than in the mathematics — eighteen of them decimal literals whose tails had been extended past what the source printout actually showed.

Two long-standing harness failures were diagnosed along the way. The sin62 and cos62 checks near the top of the shared verification file have been failing for the whole life of this series, recorded as "a linearization compared against the true value at too tight a tolerance". That diagnosis is wrong. Both compare a correct linearization against a typed decimal literal that is neither the linearization nor the true value; the gaps are $3.20\times10^{-6}$ and $1.03\times10^{-5}$, both just over the harness's $10^{-6}$ default. The correct literals pass the same tolerance, and both are now asserted as controls so the next reader does not re-diagnose them.
