Which Factor Varies

Work is force times distance, and an integral appears the moment one of those two stops being constant. Q19.4's chain had a constant force per slice and distances that changed from slice to slice. The spring is the mirror image: the force changes along the path while every element travels the same tiny step. Same integral, opposite factor.

THE STEM
Assume that 20 ft-lb of work is required to stretch a spring 1 ft beyond its natural length. (a) What is the spring constant? (b) How much work is required to stretch the spring 2 ft beyond its natural length?
PART A
$k = 40\ \text{lb/ft}$
From $20 = \tfrac12 k (1)^2$.
PART B
$W = 80\ \text{ft-lb}$
From $\int_0^2 40x\,dx$.

The mirror

Both work problems in this category compute the same thing: a sum of (force acting on a piece) times (distance that piece moves). What separates them is which of the two factors is a function.

That table is a measurement, not a slogan. Differentiate each factor with respect to its own slicing variable and one derivative comes out zero in each problem: for the chain the force factor has derivative $0$ and the distance factor has derivative $1$, and for the spring the force factor has derivative $40$ while the displacement element has derivative $0$.

Why the answer is not force times distance

At the end of the stretch the force is $F(2) = 80$ lb, and the distance is $2$ ft. Multiply those and you get $160$ ft-lb, which is exactly twice the truth. The picture below is the whole explanation: the work is the triangle under Hooke's line, and force times distance is the whole box.

$$W = \int_0^2 F(x)\,dx = \int_0^2 40x\,dx = \Big[20x^2\Big]_0^2 = 80\ \text{ft-lb}$$

Hooke's law, and the one word that costs the mark

$$F(x) = kx, \qquad x = (\text{current length}) - (\text{natural length})$$

$x$ is the extension, never the total length. This stem is generous: it says "1 ft beyond its natural length" and "2 ft beyond its natural length", so both numbers are already extensions and it never even tells you the natural length. A version that gives you lengths instead is where the mark actually goes, and it is Family 3 below.

Everything then turns on finding $k$ first, and the formula depends on what kind of datum you were handed. Getting this wrong is not a partial error: $W = \tfrac12 k a^2$ is linear in $k$, so a $k$ that is half the truth gives an answer that is half the truth, with nothing to catch it downstream.

The pound is a force, so $g$ must not appear. Q19.4 gave a mass in kilograms and had to insert $g = 9.8$ to turn it into a weight. Q19.5 is stated in ft-lb, and the pound is already a force, so inserting $g = 32.2$ is the same mistake running backwards. It produces $2576$, which nobody will mistake for $80$.

The procedure, once, for both work questions

1Draw the final state and name a slicing variable, saying where its origin is. For the chain that is a height; for the spring it is an extension.
2Ask which factor varies. If the force per piece is constant and the distances differ, you are slicing the object. If every piece moves the same $dx$ and the force differs, you are slicing the path. The varying factor is the one under the integral sign.
3Find any constant the stem only implies - the linear density $\lambda$ for a chain, the spring constant $k$ for a spring - and write its units down beside it.
4Put that constant back into the given datum and reproduce the given number. One line, and it catches every wrong-formula error.
5Write and evaluate the integral of (force) times (distance) over the range of your variable.
6Bracket the answer between smallest-force-times-distance and largest-force-times-distance. There is no sign to check on a work question, so this is the only cheap test you have.

Route B is the real check on the setup. It derives the integrand a second, independent way, and the answer must not depend on the natural length $L_0$. Evaluated at nine values of $L_0$ from $0$ to $1000$, it returned $80$ every time. Forget the shift and integrate $\int_1^3 40\ell\,d\ell$ instead, and you get $160$.

The average-force shortcut is exactly right here

Not approximately. $F(x) = kx$ is a linear function, and the mean of a linear function over an interval is its value at the midpoint, which is also the mean of its endpoint values. So $\bar{F}(a-b) = \tfrac12 k(a^2-b^2)$ is an identity. Tested over 24 intervals, the worst disagreement was $2.274 \times 10^{-13}$, which is roundoff.

It stops being exact the moment the force stops being linear, which is why knowing why it works matters more than knowing that it does:

One theorem, two disguises. This is the same statement as Q19.4's centre-of-mass shortcut, which is exact because the rise there is linear in the slicing variable - not, as it is tempting to assume, because the chain's density is uniform.

The check that works, and the one that does nothing

On an area or a net-change question you can ask "is it positive?" and catch a sign error for free. Work, as this question asks for it, is unsigned. Of the twenty wrong-answer candidates built for this page, nineteen are strictly positive and one is zero, so a positivity check rejects none of them.

Answer entry, and the expensive typo

Both exams are Möbius and answer-only, so a right method with a bad keystroke scores nothing. Part a takes 40 and part b takes 80, both as plain integers, in two separate boxes, with no units unless the box asks for them.

The most expensive typo is answering part b with 40. It is the spring constant, you just wrote it down, and it is also what five separate wrong methods produce, so it looks more familiar than any other wrong answer on the page.

A reading trap that is not a typo. "Stretch the spring 2 ft beyond its natural length" means from the natural length to an extension of $2$ ft, so the limits are $0$ to $2$ and the answer is $80$. If a version asks for the additional work, or "from 1 ft to 2 ft", the limits are $1$ to $2$ and the answer is $60$. The two readings differ by exactly the stem's own $20$ ft-lb, because $80 = 20 + 60$.
