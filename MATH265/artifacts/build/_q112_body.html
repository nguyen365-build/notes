Eliminate Or Compute

A rocket rises vertically. A radar station sits on the ground 5 mi from the pad. How fast is the rocket rising when it is 4 mi high and its distance from the station is growing at 2000 mi/h? Q11.1 taught you to get rid of the unwanted variable before differentiating. This question is built to teach you when not to.

The answer\(\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 500\sqrt{41}\ \mathrm{mi/h}\)
Decimal3201.5621 mi/h
Type into Möbius500*sqrt(41)

01 The fork this question exists to teach

Both related-rates questions in the bank give you one equation and one variable too many. Q11.1 removes the extra variable symbolically, before differentiating, using a ratio the stem says holds “always”. Q11.2 keeps it, differentiates, and then computes its value at the instant. Knowing which move a question wants is the whole of this page.

Eliminate early · Q11.1

  • The stem says the shape holds at all times: “base diameter and height are always equal”, so \(r = h/2\).
  • A relation true at every instant survives differentiation, so it may go in first.
  • Doing so makes the equation simpler: \(V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h\) collapses to \(V = \frac{\pi h^3}{12}\).

Eliminate when eliminating makes the equation simpler.

Compute at the instant · Q11.2

  • There is no fixed ratio between \(z\) and \(y\). The angle changes at every height.
  • You could still write \(z = \sqrt{25+y^2}\) - but then you must differentiate a radical.
  • Keeping the implicit form \(z^2 = 25 + y^2\) differentiates in one clean line, and \(z=\sqrt{41}\) is found afterwards.

Compute at the instant when eliminating would introduce a square root.

The substitution licence, sharpened. Q11.1 split the stem's numbers into two boxes: things true always, and things true only when. Q11.2 needs four. The cruelty of this question is that two of them look identical on the page.

The quantityExample hereSubstitute itWhy
A constant valuex = 5before differentiatingits rate is zero, so nothing is lost
A constant relationr = h/2 (Q11.1)before differentiatingit holds at every instant
An instantaneous valuey = 4only afterits rate is what you are solving for
A derived instantaneous valuez = √41only afterit inherits the restriction from \(y\)

Both \(x = 5\) and \(y = 4\) are bare numbers. One may go in before you differentiate and the other destroys the problem, and nothing about how they look on the page tells you which. Only the sentence each came from does. Substitute \(y = 4\) early and \(z^2 = 41\) becomes a constant, so \(dz/dt = 0\), and you have written \(0 = 2000\).

02 Read the geometry before you read the numbers

The station is on the ground, 5 mi from the launch pad, and the rocket rises vertically. Those three phrases put the right angle at the pad. So the 5 and the 4 are both legs, and the radar-to-rocket line is the hypotenuse.

FIGURE_SVG

Left. The two arrows are drawn at their true relative lengths. The radar measures only the part of the rocket's velocity that points along its own line of sight, and that line is slanted, so the radar always reads less than the true speed - here by a factor of \(4/\sqrt{41} = 0.6247\). Right. The same fact as a curve: for a fixed 2000 mi/h radar reading, the rocket's speed falls toward 2000 as it climbs and never gets there.

The 3-4-5 decoy. The stem prints a 5 and a 4, and every student has 3-4-5 burned in. But 3-4-5 needs the 5 to be the hypotenuse, and here it is a leg. The third side is \(\sqrt{41} \approx 6.403\), not 3. Writing \(z=3\) gives 1500 mi/h, 53 percent low. Had the radar been 3 mi away the answer really would have been 2500, so this is a reachable mistake rather than an absurd one.

03 Four lines before you touch calculus

Unchanged from Q11.1. If you cannot fill these in, you have not finished reading the question.

1 · Name

\(y\) = height in mi, \(z\) = radar-to-rocket distance in mi, \(x = 5\) mi fixed. Each a function of time \(t\) in hours.

2 · Given

\(\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2000\ \mathrm{mi/h}\). Note it belongs to the radar distance, not the rocket.

3 · Want

\(\dfrac{dy}{dt}\) in mi/h, at the instant \(y = 4\) mi.

4 · Relate

\(z^2 = x^2 + y^2 = 25 + y^2\). Pythagoras, not a volume formula.

04 The solve, in four moves

Relate

\(z^{2} = 5^{2} + y^{2} = 25 + y^{2}\)

Differentiate in \(t\)

\(2z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} \;\Longrightarrow\; z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = y\dfrac{dy}{dt}\)

Now compute \(z\)

\(z = \sqrt{25 + 4^{2}} = \sqrt{41}\)

Substitute and solve

\(\sqrt{41}\,(2000) = 4\dfrac{dy}{dt} \;\Longrightarrow\; \dfrac{dy}{dt} = 500\sqrt{41}\)

The 25 contributed nothing to the second line, because the derivative of a constant is zero. That is not a licence to ignore the 5 - it is the chain rule applied to \(x^2\), giving \(2x\,\frac{dx}{dt}\) with \(\frac{dx}{dt} = 0\). Writing it out once is worth four seconds, because it is the same reasoning that tells you \(y = 4\) may not go in early: there the rate is \(dy/dt\), which is exactly what you are solving for and is certainly not zero.

The rocket is rising at \(500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.56\) miles per hour. Answer in a sentence, with units. The units were checked dimensionally rather than by eye: \([z][\dot z][y]^{-1} = \mathrm{mi}\cdot\mathrm{mi\,h^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{mi^{-1}} = \mathrm{mi\,h^{-1}}\), and the same gate explicitly rejects mi, mi/h\(^2\), h/mi and mi\(^2\)/h.

05 Why the answer must be bigger than 2000

This question has an unusually strong sanity check, and it is physical rather than algebraic. The radar sees only the component of the velocity along its line of sight. The rocket goes straight up; the line of sight is slanted. So the radar always reads low.

$$\frac{dy/dt}{dz/dt} = \frac{z}{y} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{25}{y^{2}}} > 1 \quad\text{at every finite height}$$

The floor

Any answer at or below 2000 mi/h is wrong before you check the algebra. Here the factor is \(\sqrt{41}/4 \approx 1.6008\).

The round trip

Multiply your answer by \(y/z = 4/\sqrt{41}\). You must land back on the given 2000. Eight seconds, and it catches every inverted ratio.

Is \(z\) the longest side?

\(\sqrt{41} \approx 6.403\) exceeds both 4 and 5. If your hypotenuse is shorter than a leg, your triangle is wrong.

Is the instant reachable?

Do not assume it. With \(\dot z = 2000\) from launch, \(y = 4\) arrives at \(t = (\sqrt{41}-5)/2000 \approx 2.53\) s. Finite and positive.

That single check kills four of the eight plausible wrong answers outright.

06 The variation catalog

Read each table as structure, not as a list of facts. For a right triangle with a fixed leg there is only one formula, and only the ratio \(x/y\) enters it - which is why \((5,4)\) and \((50,40)\) give the same amplification.

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dz}{dt}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}}{y} = \frac{dz}{dt}\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^{2}}$$

Every value below was verified twice: once from the closed form, and once formula-blind, by building an explicit time parametrisation and taking a complex-step derivative of it.

07 Where marks actually die

  1. Reading 5 as the hypotenuse. The radar is 5 mi from the launch pad, not from the rocket. Using \(z=5\) gives 2500 mi/h, about 22 percent low. The most common wrong answer, and it survives every check except “is \(z\) longer than both legs”.
  2. The 3-4-5 decoy. 4 and 5 are both legs here, so the third side is \(\sqrt{41}\), not 3. Gives 1500, which is 53 percent low.
  3. Substituting \(y=4\) before differentiating. The defining error of the whole category, carried over unchanged from Q11.1. It makes \(z^2=41\) constant, so \(dz/dt=0\), and you have written \(0=2000\).
  4. Inverting the ratio. \(\frac{y}{z}\dot z\) instead of \(\frac{z}{y}\dot z\) gives 1249.39. The physical check kills it instantly: the answer must be larger than 2000.
  5. Dividing by \(x\) instead of \(y\). Gives \(400\sqrt{41} \approx 2561.25\), exactly 20 percent low. This one has a hiding point, below, and is the most under-detected slip on the question.
  6. Answering the given rate. “How fast is the rocket rising” is \(dy/dt\). It is not the 2000 you were handed, and it is not \(d\theta/dt\).
  7. Dropping the chain factor. Writing \(2z\dot z = 2y\) leaves an equation with no unknown in it. If the rate you want is not in your differentiated equation, re-differentiate.
  8. Reporting the wrong units. The answer is a length per time, mi/h. No numerical check catches a unit error, which is why units get their own gate.

Where a wrong answer hides

Eight wrong formulas were scanned across a grid of eleven heights and five rates, deliberately including the degenerate points rather than excluding them.

A zero rate hides everything

At \(\dot z = 0\), all eight wrong formulas agree with the truth, because every one of them differs by a multiplicative factor. A spot-check on a stationary rocket cannot fail, so it proves nothing.

\(y = 5\) hides one slip exactly

Dividing by \(x\) instead of \(y\) is invisible at \(y=5\), the one height where they are the same number. And 5 is the number printed in the stem, so it is the most tempting height to spot-check at.

\(y = 1\) hides another

Forgetting to divide by \(y\) is invisible at \(y=1\), for the same reason it was at \(h=1\) in Q11.1: the hiding point sits wherever the erroneous factor equals 1.

Near the pad, the hypotenuse slip vanishes

As \(y\to 0\), \(\sqrt{25+y^2}\to 5\). At \(y=0.001\) the wrong and right answers agree to one part in a million. Four miles up they differ by 22 percent.

The other six slips have no height-dependent hiding point at all - six of eight, measured rather than assumed. The general rule, now confirmed on both questions in this category: a hiding point exists wherever the factor your error touches happens to equal 1, or wherever two of the stem's own numbers coincide. Compute where that is before choosing a check point.

09 Drill set

Score sign and magnitude separately. A wrong sign means you mis-read which side was fixed, which is a reading problem. A wrong magnitude means you dropped or inverted a factor, which is an algebra problem. They need different remediation.

  1. The same rocket and radar: how fast is it rising at 12 mi high? 6500/3 ≈ 2166.6667 mi/h · F1
  2. The same rocket at 4 mi, radar distance growing at only 500 mi/h. 125√41 ≈ 800.3905 mi/h · F1
  3. At the exam instant, how fast is the angle of elevation changing? 2500/√41 ≈ 390.4344 rad/h · F5, note the units
  4. Radar 12 mi from the pad, rocket at 5 mi, \(\dot z = 2000\). 5200 mi/h exactly · F1
  5. Radar 3 mi from the pad, rocket at 4 mi, \(\dot z = 2000\). 2500 mi/h exactly · F1, the genuine 3-4-5
  6. Rocket at 4 mi rising at 3000 mi/h: how fast is \(z\) growing? 12000/√41 ≈ 1874.0851 mi/h · F2, must be less than 3000
  7. A 13 ft ladder, foot pulled out at 2 ft/s, foot 5 ft from the wall. -5/6 ≈ -0.8333 ft/s · F3, fixed hypotenuse
  8. A winch 5 ft above a boat's bow hauls rope at 2 ft/s, boat 12 ft out. -13/6 ≈ -2.1667 ft/s · F1, fixed leg
  9. Softball diamond 18 m; runner 3 m from second at 7.5 m/s; rate of change of her distance from home. 112.5/√549 ≈ 4.8014 m/s · F2
  10. One car east at 60 mph, one north at 80 mph, from the same point. How fast do they separate after 1 h? 100 mph exactly · F4
  11. At what height is the rocket rising exactly twice as fast as \(z\) grows? 5/√3 ≈ 2.8868 mi · F7
  12. Gravel at 0.5 m\(^3\)/min, cone with diameter equal to height, \(h=4\). 1/(8π) ≈ 0.0397887 m/min · F6, eliminate early

Drills 7 and 8 are the discrimination pair, and the most valuable two minutes here. Both are right triangles with a 5, a 12 and a 13. In drill 7 the 13 is fixed and the answer is \(-5/6\); in drill 8 the 5 is fixed and the answer is \(-13/6\). Same numbers, different fixed side, answers differing by a factor of 2.6. If you can sort those two on sight, you have the family.
