MATH 265 · Midterm Exam 2 · Item 3 · 4 points

Average Versus Instantaneous

The only question on either midterm that asks you to compute nothing at all. There is no algebra to hide behind, so a vague sentence is the whole answer, and the marks live almost entirely in two words and a pair of units.

QThe question, as printed

The population \(P\) of a city in the year \(y\) is \(P(y)\). Write a sentence (in layman's terms) that explains the meaning of the expressions
a
\(\dfrac{P(15) - P(2)}{13} = 1{,}431\)
Averagea difference over a difference, so a rate across a whole interval
b
\(P'(10) = 14{,}000\)
Instantaneousa prime on a single input, so a rate at one instant

The one sentence that decides this question. A difference divided by a difference is an average rate over an interval; a prime attached to a single number is an instantaneous rate at an instant, and both of them are measured in output units per input unit. Everything else on this question is bookkeeping around those two readings.

AThe answer

What to write · two sentences, two marks each

(a) Between year 2 and year 15, the city's population grew by an average of 1,431 people per year.

(b) In year 10, the city's population was growing at an instantaneous rate of 14,000 people per year.

Three things a marker looks for that students routinely skip: the word average in (a) and instantaneous in (b), because those two words are the entire distinction being examined; the units, written out as "people per year" rather than left implied; and the interval named in (a), which is the only thing that shows you noticed that \(13 = 15 - 2\).

Möbius cannot mark a sentence, so on the live platform this question comes back as its numeric skeleton. Every value below was recomputed and checked.

The most expensive keystroke available here

That last row is the one students throw away. Both are rates, so both carry the same units, and both need those units stated.

They are not two topics. One is the other, squeezed.

$$P'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{P(a+h)-P(a)}{h}$$

Part (b) is part (a) with the interval shrunk to a point. Saying that out loud in your answer is free credit, and it is the sentence that shows you understand rather than recognise.

2The picture: a secant and a tangent on one city

Part (a) is the slope of the line joining the two endpoints. Part (b) is the slope of the line that just touches at year 10. Here is one city that satisfies both given facts exactly, with both lines drawn on it.

Why 14,000 and 1,431 are not in conflict

The tangent is 9.78 times steeper than the secant, and that feels wrong until you look at the shape. A curve can be nearly flat for years and then very steep for one of them. An average over thirteen years and a rate at one instant are different quantities, and nothing forces them to be close.

3The procedure, and the units rule that carries the marks

Look at the SHAPE of the expression.

  [ P(b) - P(a) ] / (b - a)   ->  AVERAGE rate, over the INTERVAL [a,b]
  P'(a)                       ->  INSTANTANEOUS rate, at the INSTANT y = a

Then fill in one template, in this order:

  1. WHEN      "Between year 2 and year 15"     or  "In year 10"
  2. WHAT      "the city's population"
  3. HOW       "grew by an average of"          or  "was growing at a rate of"
  4. NUMBER    "1,431"                          or  "14,000"
  5. UNITS     "people per year"                <- this is where the marks are

The units of any rate of change are (units of the output) per (one unit of the input). That is the whole rule, and it never has an exception.

Row four is the sharpest distinction on the table. \(P(15)-P(2)=18{,}603\) is a number of people. \(\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=1{,}431\) is a number of people per year. Dividing by the width is what turns a count into a rate, and that is the only difference between the two.

The Mean Value Theorem row is worth stating properly, because it is the one consequence you can assert. If \(P\) is continuous on \([2,15]\) and differentiable on \((2,15)\), then there is at least one year \(c\) strictly between 2 and 15 with \(P'(c)=1{,}431\). In plain words: at some moment during those thirteen years the city really was growing at exactly the average pace. The theorem gives existence, never a location, so "at year 8.5" is not an answer.

Read the structure, not the four numbers. The forward and backward errors are \(+60\) and \(-60\), equal and opposite, which is exactly why averaging them is exact here. The fourth row is exact for a related reason: on a quadratic, the average rate over an interval equals the derivative at its midpoint, and 10 is the midpoint of \([8,12]\).

Both of those exactnesses are quadratic-only accidents

On City B from section 4, the average rate over \([8,12]\) is 4,628 while \(P'(10)\) is 14,000. Do not carry the midpoint identity into a general question.

Annual data can miss the answer badly

City B, tabulated year by year, reads 50,045 / 50,874 / 59,302 / 67,729 / 68,558 at years 8 through 12. Its centred estimate of \(P'(10)\) is \(\frac{67{,}729-50{,}874}{2}=8{,}427.5\), which understates the true 14,000 by 39.8 percent, because the whole boom is narrower than the one-year sampling step. A difference quotient can only see what the sampling interval resolves.

Which estimate is better is a question about ORDER, not about one number

MethodErrors as \(h\) halvesRatioOrder
forward \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\)31, 15.25, 7.56, 3.77\(\approx 2\)first order
centred \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}\)1, 0.25, 0.0625, 0.0156\(\approx 4\)second order

Errors halving means first order; errors quartering means second order. A single accurate estimate proves nothing, and here is the proof: on City B the forward estimate at year 9 has ratios 2.08, 2.04, 2.02, while at year 10 the same method on the same function has ratios 3.97, 3.99, 4.00. Year 10 is that model's inflection point, where \(P''(10)=0\) kills the first-order error term, and the model is odd-symmetric about it, so the forward quotient is literally equal to the centred quotient there. Test only at year 10 and you would conclude that forward differences are second order.

And smaller h is not always better

For that same centred estimate at year 10, the error is \(2.5\times10^{-3}\) at \(h=10^{-6}\) but \(2.6\) at \(h=10^{-12}\). Past a point, subtracting two nearly equal large numbers and dividing by a tiny one loses more to rounding than it gains in truncation. Test convergence on a bounded ladder, and stop before the arithmetic falls apart.

Family 6A formula is given, compute bothOne derivative rule

Take \(P(y)=40y^{2}+500y+30{,}000\). Then \(P(2)=31{,}160\) and \(P(15)=46{,}500\), so the average rate on \([2,15]\) is \(\frac{15{,}340}{13}=1{,}180\), and \(P'(y)=80y+500\) gives \(P'(10)=1{,}300\).

There is a shortcut worth knowing for a quadratic \(Ay^{2}+By+C\): the average rate over \([a,b]\) is exactly \(A(a+b)+B\), because the \(y^2\) difference factors as \((b-a)(b+a)\). Here \(40(2+15)+500=1{,}180\), with no evaluation of \(P\) at all. Notice that the instantaneous rate exceeds the average, which always happens past the interval's midpoint when the rate is increasing, and \(10 > 8.5\).

Family 8Relative, or percentage, rateThe units cancel
$$\text{relative rate at } y=a \;=\; \frac{P'(a)}{P(a)}$$

For City B, \(\frac{14{,}000}{59{,}302}=0.2361\), so the population was growing at about 23.6 percent per year at year 10. The average rate as a percentage of the starting population is \(\frac{1{,}431}{50{,}000}=2.862\) percent per year. A relative rate is a pure number per year, because the people cancel, and that is why demographers quote percentages: 14,000 people a year means something very different for a city of 50,000 than for one of 5,000,000.

The trap a numerical check will not catch

For the corner \(P(y)=\lvert y-10\rvert\), the centred difference quotient at year 10 is \(\frac{h-h}{2h}=0\) for every \(h\). It reports a derivative of zero where none exists, and it does so no matter how small you make \(h\), so no amount of refining will reveal the problem. The one-sided quotients, \(+1\) from the right and \(-1\) from the left, are the only ones that see the corner. When existence is the question, check the two sides separately and never rely on a symmetric estimate.

Family 11The average of the rateSame fact, other side

Wording like "on average, how fast was the population growing" is the same question as (a), not a new one. The average value of \(P'\) over \([2,15]\) equals the average rate of \(P\) over \([2,15]\):

$$\frac{1}{15-2}\int_{2}^{15}P'(y)\,dy=\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=1{,}431$$

Both cities in section 4 were checked against this numerically and both give exactly 1,431. This is why an average rate deserves the word "average" at all: it is the mean of the instantaneous rates.

6The traps, ranked by what they actually cost

  1. Dropping the units. "Grew by an average of 1,431" is not an answer; "1,431 people per year" is. This costs more marks here than everything else combined.
  2. Turning a rate into a count. "The population grew by 1,431 people" describes \(P(15)-P(2)\) divided by nothing. The division by 13 is what makes it a rate.
  3. Losing "average" in (a) or "instantaneous" in (b). Those two words are what the question is examining.
  4. Not naming the interval. "Over 13 years" is weaker than "between year 2 and year 15", and only the second shows you spotted \(13=15-2\).
  5. Treating an average as a guarantee. "The population grew by 1,431 every year" is refuted outright by City D.
  6. Reading \(P'(10)=14{,}000\) as "the population rose by 14,000 during year 10". An instantaneous rate is not a year's worth of change.
  7. Restating the symbols. "The derivative of \(P\) at \(y=10\) equals 14,000" is a translation into the same language, and "in layman's terms" is an explicit instruction against it.
  8. Assuming "per year" when the input is not time. \(C'(80)\) is dollars per chair.
  9. Getting the interval width wrong on a reword. "Between year 4 and year 9" has width 5.
  10. Declaring the two numbers inconsistent. They are not, and section 4 exhibits two cities that satisfy both.
  11. Trusting a centred difference at a corner. It returns 0 for every \(h\) and hides the non-differentiability completely.
  12. Concluding a method is accurate from one good estimate. Order is a statement about how the error behaves as \(h\) shrinks, not about a single value.

7The decision tree

An expression about a changing quantity. What does it MEAN?
|
+-- Does it DIVIDE by a difference of two inputs?
|     YES -> AVERAGE RATE over an INTERVAL.

|

|            4. Optional gloss: "if that pace held, about <value> over
|               the next <one input unit>."  Keep BOTH hedges.
|     TWO primes -> the rate OF THE RATE. Units take "per <input unit>" twice.
|

|     YES -> a NET CHANGE, not a rate. No "per" in the sentence.
|            f(b) - f(a) = (average rate) x (b - a).
|

|     YES -> a RELATIVE rate: a percentage per input unit. The units cancel.

Before the final sentence, check three things:
  UNITS    output unit "per" one input unit, written out in full.
  SIGN     positive -> grew/rose;  negative -> fell/shrank.
  SCOPE    (a) names an interval;  (b) names one instant. Never swap them.

8Provenance, and where the course files are imprecise
