01

The question, and the thing it is really asking

Here is the item, word for word from the paper.

Use the method of differentials to estimate each of the values below to four decimal places (show the process).
a. \(\cos 62^\circ\)    b. \(\sqrt{16.4}\)

Two parts, one stem, eight points. That makes it the heaviest question in the linear-approximation category, ahead of Q12.1 (5 points on Midterm 1) and Q12.2 (6 points on Midterm 2). Nineteen points across three separate papers all test the same five moves.

So why is this one worth more? Because of five words the other two do not have: to four decimal places.

The uncomfortable fact. Round the differential estimate to four places and round the true value to four places, and on both parts the two strings differ. Part (a) gives 0.4698 against a true 0.4695. Part (b) gives 4.0500 against a true 4.0497. The method is being asked for a precision it cannot supply, and the marker knows it.

The resolution is not clever. "To four decimal places" means write four digits down. It cannot mean guarantee four correct ones, because on this question nothing can. Section 03 measures that rather than asserting it.

Point value

Parsed from the paper, not typed. F1's fourteen printed values sum to 124, and item 2 takes 8 of them, which is 6.4516 percent. Four items are heavier; item 10 ties it.

02

The five moves, run twice

Nothing carries between the two parts except the shape of the work. Different function, different anchor, different units.

1 · name f

Part (a) is \(f(x) = \cos x\). Part (b) is \(f(x) = \sqrt{x}\). The quantity asked for has to be \(f\) of something.

2 · choose the anchor

The nearest point where you know \(f(a)\) exactly. Part (a): \(60^\circ\), since \(\cos 60^\circ = \tfrac12\). Part (b): \(16\), since \(\sqrt{16} = 4\).

3 · convert, if it is an angle

Only part (a) needs this, and it is where the question is lost. \(dx = 2^\circ = \pi/90\) radians.

4 · differentiate once

At the anchor, never at the target. \(f'(\pi/3) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) and \(f'(16) = \tfrac18\).

5 · add, then round

\(f(a) + f'(a)\,dx\), rounded to the places asked for and padded with zeros. \(4.05\) is written \(4.0500\).

Part (a) · cos 62 degrees

\(dx = 2^\circ = \dfrac{\pi}{90}\), and \(f'(a) = -\sin 60^\circ = -\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\).

$$\cos 62^\circ \approx \frac12 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{90} = \frac12 - \frac{\sqrt3\,\pi}{180}$$

\(= 0.46977001\ldots\), so you write 0.4698.

\(f'' = -\cos x < 0\) here, so the graph is concave down, the tangent line sits above it, and the estimate is too big.

Part (b) · the square root of 16.4

\(dx = 0.4\), and \(f'(a) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{16}} = \dfrac18\).

$$\sqrt{16.4} \approx 4 + \frac18\cdot\frac25 = 4 + \frac{1}{20} = \frac{81}{20}$$

\(= 4.05\) exactly, so you write 4.0500.

\(f'' = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}} < 0\), so this one is concave down too, and it is too big as well.

Both parts lean the same way here, and that is a coincidence of this stem rather than a rule. Pair a square root with a tangent and one overshoots while the other undershoots. Section 07, family 5, has the table.

The single most expensive mistake in this category. Skipping the conversion on part (a) gives \(\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}(2) = -1.2321\). That is 5701 times the method's own error, and a cosine is never below \(-1\), so the check is free. Look at your answer and ask whether it is between \(-1\) and \(1\).

03

What "four decimal places" is worth

Here is the whole question in one picture. Round the estimate and the truth to \(k\) places for \(k = 0\) through \(4\), and mark where the two strings agree.

Every value in that figure was computed in 60-digit decimal arithmetic and quantized with round-half-up on the decimal expansion, not with a floating-point round().

Read part (a) first. It agrees at zero, one and two places, then fails at three and stays failed. That is the ordinary pattern, and it means the answer is correct to two decimal places.

Now read part (b), which is the strange one. It agrees at zero. It fails at one. It agrees again at two and three. Then it fails at four.

The cause is a rounding tie. The estimate is exactly \(4.05\), which rounds up to \(4.1\), while the true \(4.0497\) rounds down to \(4.0\). So if you check three places you conclude the answer is fine, and if you check one place you conclude it is wrong. Rounding agreement is not nested, and you cannot infer a place count from the size of the error.

An honest count is the largest \(k\) such that every place up to \(k\) agrees. On that count part (b) is correct to zero decimal places, despite having the smaller relative error of the two.

Is the method simply incapable of four places?

No, and that is worth knowing, because it turns a complaint into a rule. Four of the twelve drill problems in section 09 do reach four correct places. What separates them is one quantity.

The discriminator is \(\dfrac{|f''(a)|\,(dx)^2}{2}\). Below about \(5\times10^{-5}\) the estimate reaches four places. Both of this question's parts sit near \(3\times10^{-4}\), roughly ten times too coarse. That number takes one line to compute and it tells you exactly how much of your answer to believe.

Could a better choice have rescued it?

Four repairs were tested rather than assumed. None of them works within the method the stem names.

So write the estimate, not the truth. Writing \(0.4695\) or \(4.0497\) because you checked on a calculator is the second most expensive thing you can do on this question. The marker's answer is the differential value.

04

The error bound, settled

Taylor's theorem says the error is $$\text{true} - \text{estimate} = \frac{f''(\xi)}{2}(dx)^2 \quad\text{for some } \xi \text{ strictly between } a \text{ and } a+dx.$$ That \(\xi\) is somewhere you cannot name, so people reach for the value of \(|f''|\) at an endpoint instead. Twice in this category that produced a "bound" the actual error exceeded, and the two failures pointed in opposite directions. Here is what is actually going on.

Each track is rescaled to its own interval, so only the marker's position inside the bar is comparable between rows.

Neither endpoint is "the bound". Because \(\xi\) is strictly inside the interval, the larger endpoint value is an upper bound and the smaller one is a lower bound. The error is strictly bracketed: $$\frac{\min\big(|f''(a)|,|f''(b)|\big)}{2}(dx)^2 \;<\; |\text{error}| \;<\; \frac{\max\big(|f''(a)|,|f''(b)|\big)}{2}(dx)^2$$ Five instances, five brackets. Nothing was ever violated. A lower bound was read as an upper one.

Two things fall out, and both were measured rather than reasoned about.

The sign of dx does not decide it

\(\sqrt{9.2}\) steps up and \(\sqrt{48.6}\) steps down on the same falling \(|f''|\), and the larger endpoint is different in the two cases.

What does decide it

Whether \(|f''|\) grows along the direction of travel. If it does, the target is the larger endpoint; if it falls, the anchor is.

On this question

Both parts have their larger endpoint at the anchor, so the anchor bound is the one to quote: \(3.05\times10^{-4}\) and \(3.125\times10^{-4}\).

Where the bracket stops working

It needs \(|f''|\) of one sign and monotone. An interval straddling \(90^\circ\), where \(|\sin|\) peaks inside, exceeds both endpoints. Neither of this question's intervals does that.

The bracket was checked on 56 root pairs, 54 sine pairs and 54 cosine pairs, and it held on all 164. The rule to carry into the exam is one line: take the maximum of \(|f''|\) over the whole interval, and quote \(\tfrac{M}{2}(dx)^2\). It comes out within 2 percent of the observed error every time, so it is worth writing down.

Choosing the anchor, and the rule that finally survives

Q12.1 concluded "nearest exact point wins, always". Q12.2 found a trigonometric counterexample. This question breaks the rule on both of its parts, including the square-root family the rule came from.

Two inversions. \(90^\circ\) is eleven degrees further away than \(45^\circ\) and 1.45 times better. \(120^\circ\) is twenty-six degrees further than \(30^\circ\) and better again.

The reason is that the error goes as \(\tfrac{|f''(a)|(dx)^2}{2}\), and for a cosine \(f'' = -\cos\). So an anchor where \(|\cos a|\) is small buys back a lot of distance, and \(|\cos 90^\circ| = 0\).

Note this points the opposite way from Q12.2. There \(f'' = -\sin\), so a small \(|\sin a|\) helped and \(30^\circ\) beat \(90^\circ\). Here it is \(|\cos a|\) that matters, so \(90^\circ\) beats \(30^\circ\). Same rule, different second derivative, reversed answer. That is why the rule is the thing to remember, not the ranking.

Q12.1 only ever ranked anchors that were close together, where \(|f''|\) barely changes. That is precisely why it saw distance and error agree, and drew a rule that does not hold.

The rule that survives. Minimise \(|f''(a)|\,(dx)^2\). "Nearest" is the special case that holds when \(|f''|\) is roughly the same across the candidates, which is most of the time and is why it is still what you do at speed. In practice: take the nearest anchor where \(f(a)\) is exact, and if two candidates are close in distance, take the flatter one.

One caution, measured: the quadratic model predicts \(90^\circ\) would be perfect, since \(\cos 90^\circ = 0\) kills the whole quadratic term. The real error there is \(1.9\times10^{-2}\), because the cubic term takes over, and it predicts that error to within 1.2 percent. So \(|f''|\) is a tie-breaker, not a replacement for looking at the distance.

06

Checking yourself in the last thirty seconds

(a) is it in [-1, 1]?

The highest-value check in the category. It catches the unconverted answer \(-1.2321\), and no amount of careful arithmetic rescues that one.

(a) did it move the right way?

You stepped away from \(0^\circ\), so the cosine should have fallen a little below \(\tfrac12\). It did, by about \(0.03\).

(b) square it back

\(\left(\tfrac{81}{20}\right)^2 = 16.4025\), a little above \(16.4\). That confirms the value and the overshoot at once, and it is the best check in the question.

(b) is it between 4 and 4.1?

\(4^2 = 16\) and \(4.1^2 = 16.81\), and \(16 < 16.4 < 16.81\). Four seconds, kills every gross slip.

The two parts need different checks, and that is worth knowing in advance. Part (b) squares back for free. Part (a) has no equivalent, because \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\) needs the sine as well. Reaching for the wrong check is a real way to waste the time you have.

The check that cannot fail. Spot-checking at \(dx = 0\) agrees with the truth for five of the eight standard wrong answers, because all five differ from the truth only by something multiplying \(dy\), and \(dy\) is zero there. Never sanity-check a linearization at its own anchor. The good news is specific: at this question's own numbers, not one of the eight slips coincides with the truth, so \(60^\circ\) with \(dx = 2^\circ\) and \(16\) with \(dx = 0.4\) are safe places to check.

Family 2Tangent, cotangent, secant, cosecant

Trigger: any of the other four near a special angle. Tool: the same five moves, but the direction flips. All four are concave up on the first quadrant, so all four undershoot.

\(\tan 46^\circ \approx 1 + \tfrac{\pi}{90}\), and \(\tan 44^\circ \approx 1 - \tfrac{\pi}{90}\). One steps up, one steps down, and both undershoot. Convexity fixes the direction; the sign of \(dx\) has no say, and neither does the sign of \(f'\), which is negative for cotangent and cosecant and changes nothing.

Family 3Powers, reciprocals, mixed exponents

Trigger: \((a + dx)^p\) for any real \(p\). Tool: \(f' = p\,a^{p-1}\), and the direction comes from the sign of \(p(p-1)\).

\(p > 1\) is convex and undershoots. \(0 < p < 1\) is concave and overshoots, exactly like a root. \(p < 0\) on positive \(x\) is convex and undershoots.

\((1.02)^{10}\) kills the idea that a small step means a good estimate. Its step is \(0.02\) and the answer is still 1.6 percent low, because \(f''(1) = 90\). The error is \(\tfrac12 f''(dx)^2\), and \(f''\) gets a vote.

Family 4"Estimate to N decimal places"

Trigger: any precision demand attached to the stem. This is this question's own family, and no course file covers it.

Compute the estimate, round it to \(N\) places padding with zeros, and separately compute \(\tfrac{|f''(a)|}{2}(dx)^2\) so you can say what it is worth. What you write is always the estimate. If there is room, add one clause quoting the bound rather than claiming a place count.

Trigger: the direction asked, with or without the number. Tool: the sign of \(f''\), and one sentence about the tangent line.

Answer it with the tangent line, never with the arithmetic: "\(f'' < 0\) here, so the graph is concave down, so the tangent lies above it, so the estimate is too large." That works for a negative \(dx\) too. Four of the eight overshoot and four undershoot, so "linearization overestimates" is a coin flip.

Family 6Bound the error

Trigger: "how accurate is this", "bound the error", or a precision demand you want to justify. Tool: \(\tfrac{M}{2}(dx)^2\) with \(M\) the maximum of \(|f''|\) over the whole interval. See section 04 for why the endpoint matters.

Family 7Two parts that do not have to agree

Trigger: two lettered parts under one stem, which is exactly this question's shape.

This question's parts happen to agree, both overshooting. Pair \(\sqrt{16.4}\) with \(\tan 46^\circ\), or with \((2.01)^5\), and one overshoots while the other undershoots. Treat the parts as two independent questions that share only the shape of the work.

Family 8The change, not the value

Trigger: "by approximately how much does it change", or an error-propagation stem. Tool: \(dy = f'(a)\,dx\) alone. Do not add \(f(a)\).

A circle of radius 10 with \(dr = 0.1\) changes area by \(2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi\). A cube of side 5 with \(ds = 0.02\) changes volume by \(3s^2\,ds = 1.5\). For a power the relative form is clean: \(\tfrac{dy}{y} = n\,\tfrac{dx}{x}\).

08

Where the marks actually go

  1. Not converting to radians on part (a). Gives \(-1.2321\), which is 5701 times the method's own error. Available on exactly one of the two parts, and free to catch.
  2. Writing the true value. \(0.4695\) and \(4.0497\) are the truth, not the estimate. The question asked for the estimate.
  3. Evaluating \(f'\) at the target instead of the anchor. The error it makes is about the same size as the correct answer's, pointing the other way, so it looks entirely plausible. Worse, it becomes invisible as the step shrinks.
  4. Not padding to the requested number of places. Writing \(4.05\) where \(4.0500\) was asked for. Free marks, one keystroke.
  5. Carrying part (a)'s setup into part (b). Different function, different anchor, different units.
  6. Choosing a prettier anchor rather than a better one. Subtler than it looks: see section 05, where the nearest anchor loses twice.
  7. Spot-checking at the anchor. \(dx = 0\) hides five of the eight standard slips. Use the stem's own numbers instead.
  8. Reporting \(dy\) instead of \(f(a) + dy\). Answers \(-0.0302\) and \(0.05\). The magnitude check catches it instantly.
  9. Getting the direction backwards by reasoning from the sign of \(dx\) rather than the sign of \(f''\). Both parts step up and overshoot; \(\sqrt{24.6}\) steps down and also overshoots.
  10. Assuming both parts lean the same way. Here they do. Change one function and they do not.
  11. The invisible ones. For a square root \(f'(a) = 1\) exactly at \(a = 1/4\), so forgetting to multiply by \(f'(a)\) is undetectable there. For a cosine \(f'(0) = 0\), so at that anchor a sign flip, a doubled correction and a target-for-step slip are all identical to the truth. Neither point is 16 or \(60^\circ\), so this question is clean, but a reworded one might not be.

Question 7 is the one to study. At \(90^\circ\) the second derivative \(-\cos(90^\circ)\) is exactly zero, so the concavity test says nothing at all. The cubic term decides instead: \(\cos(90^\circ - h) = \sin h = h - h^3/6\), which is less than \(h\), so the estimate overshoots. Step the other way and the same estimate undershoots. An inflection is the only place in this whole catalog where the sign of \(dx\) decides the direction.

Score magnitude and direction separately. They are different failure modes needing different fixes. A wrong magnitude means you mis-differentiated or mis-anchored. A wrong direction means you reasoned from the sign of \(dx\), which is a five-minute fix. Eight of the twelve overshoot, so answering "over" every time scores 8 out of 12, which looks like competence and is not.

The place counts across the twelve run \(0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5\). Four of them reach four correct places. What separates those four is the discriminator from section 03.

Three things the category corrected about itself along the way, each one written down only because a measurement contradicted a draft.

"Nearest wins, always" is false

Stated on Q12.1, refuted on trigonometry by Q12.2, and refuted on square roots here. The surviving rule is: minimise \(|f''(a)|(dx)^2\).
