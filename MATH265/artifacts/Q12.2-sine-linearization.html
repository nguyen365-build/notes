Estimate a sine with linearization

"Use linearization to estimate the value of \(\sin(62^{\circ})\)." Q12.1 asked whether you can choose an anchor. This one asks whether you can choose a unit, and it is worth one more mark for exactly that reason.

01 · The thirty-second version

Five moves, one derivative, no calculator. The middle move is the one this question was set to test, and it is the one students skip.

1 · Name f

You are asked for a number that is \(f\) of something. \(\sin 62^{\circ}\) means \(f(x) = \sin x\).

2 · Choose the anchor

The nearest angle whose sine and cosine are exact. \(60^{\circ}\) is a 30-60-90 angle, so \(a = \dfrac{\pi}{3}\), \(f(a) = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\), \(f'(a) = \dfrac{1}{2}\).

3 · Convert the step

\(dx = 2^{\circ} = 2 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{90}\) radians. Write this line on its own, before differentiating.

4 · Build dy and add

\(dy = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{90} = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\), so \(\sin 62^{\circ} \approx \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \dfrac{\pi}{180}\).

Then say which way it leans. \(f''(x) = -\sin x < 0\) on \((0, \pi)\), so \(\sin\) is concave down there, so the tangent line sits above the curve, so the estimate is too big. It is, by \(0.00053\). There is also a free sanity check that no other question in this category offers: a sine can never exceed 1, so an answer of \(1.87\) is refuted before you have looked at anything else.

02 · The unit is the question

Every trigonometric derivative in this course is written for radians. \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x\) is true when \(x\) is in radians and false when it is in degrees, where the derivative picks up a factor of \(\dfrac{\pi}{180}\). So the step must be converted before it is multiplied by anything.

Panel A draws both candidate steps at true scale from the same anchor. The correct step, \(\pi/90\) radians, is two degrees wide and lands so close to the anchor that the two markers overlap. The blunder uses the bare number 2, which is 2 radians, and walks \(114.6^{\circ}\) along the tangent to land at a height of \(1.866\) - above the top of a sine's range, where the curve itself has already come back down to \(0.094\). Panel B plots the error itself, \(L(x) - \sin x\), because at any zoom that keeps both curves on screen the gap between them is thinner than the lines are drawn. Read three things off it: the error is zero at the anchor, it is positive on both sides so the overestimate does not depend on which way you step, and it grows quadratically so doubling the step quadruples the error.

The converted step, pi/90

  • \(dx = 0.0349\), about one thirtieth of a radian.
  • \(dy = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{90} = \dfrac{\pi}{180} = 0.01745\).
  • Estimate \(0.8834787\) against a true \(0.8829476\).
  • Error \(+5.31 \times 10^{-4}\), or \(0.060\) percent.

This is the answer, and \(dx^2/2 = 6.1\times10^{-4}\) is small precisely because the step is measured in radians.

The bare step, 2

  • \(dx = 2\), which is \(114.6^{\circ}\).
  • \(dy = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2 = 1\).
  • Estimate \(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 1 = 1.8660\).
  • Error \(+0.983\), or \(111\) percent.

1851 times the correct method's error, and impossible on sight. Worse, a smaller step makes it relatively worse: on a one-degree step the ratio is 3713, because this error is linear in the step while the method's is quadratic.

You may work in degrees, provided you differentiate the degree function. Put \(g(t) = \sin\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{180}\right)\) with \(t\) in degrees. Then \(g'(t) = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{180}\right)\), and \(g(62) \approx g(60) + 2g'(60) = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \dfrac{\pi}{180}\), bit for bit the same double. The \(\pi/180\) does not disappear. It moves from the step into the derivative, and it is still paid.

03 · Four routes to the same number

All four were evaluated and all four land on the identical floating-point value \(0.8834786963043819\). Choose by what the stem asks for and by how many lines you want to write.

Route 1 · differentials

\(dy = f'(a)\,dx = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi}{90} = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\), then add \(f(a)\).

$$\sin 62^{\circ} \approx \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$$

Four lines. The \(\cos(\pi/3) = \tfrac12\) halves the \(\pi/90\) into \(\pi/180\), so the correction is exactly "one degree in radians". Use this one under pressure.

Route 2 · the linearization

$$L(x) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\left(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$

Evaluate at \(x = \dfrac{31\pi}{90}\). Identical answer, but you now have to convert the target as well as the step, which doubles your exposure to the one error this question is built around.

Route 3 · the angle sum

\(\sin(60^{\circ} + 2^{\circ}) = \sin 60^{\circ}\cos 2^{\circ} + \cos 60^{\circ}\sin 2^{\circ}\), then take \(\cos 2^{\circ} \approx 1\) and \(\sin 2^{\circ} \approx \dfrac{\pi}{90}\).

This is not a different method. It is the linearization with its two approximations made visible, and it tells you exactly what you threw away: \(\cos 2^{\circ}\) is really \(0.99939\).

Route 4 · the degree function

\(g(t) = \sin\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{180}\right)\), \(g'(t) = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{180}\right)\), then \(g(62) \approx g(60) + 2g'(60)\).

The honest way to stay in degrees. Safe, but the \(\pi/180\) is now hiding inside \(g'\) where it is easier to drop.

The stem says "use linearization", so write the words \(L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)\) somewhere even if you compute by route 1. It costs one line and it is the phrase the marker is looking for.

04 · Nearest is almost always best, and it is worth knowing when it is not

Q12.1 established that the anchor must be chosen by measurement rather than by instinct. Nine legal exact angles were ranked here by the error each actually produces.

The bars are ordered by distance from \(62^{\circ}\), so a bar that is taller than the one to its right is an inversion of the obvious rule. There are two. The \(30^{\circ}\) anchor is four degrees further from the target than \(90^{\circ}\) and still costs 1.16 times less, and \(0^{\circ}\) is four degrees further than \(120^{\circ}\) and costs less again.

The reason is that the error is \(\dfrac{|f''(a)|}{2}dx^2 = \dfrac{|\sin a|}{2}dx^2\), so the second derivative gets a vote alongside the distance. \(\sin 30^{\circ} = \tfrac12\) against \(\sin 90^{\circ} = 1\), and that halving buys back the extra four degrees. At \(0^{\circ}\) the effect is total: \(\sin 0 = 0\), the quadratic term vanishes, and the error there is third order rather than second.

The working rule

  • Take the nearest exact angle: \(0, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 135, 150, 180\).
  • You need both \(\sin a\) and \(\cos a\), because one gives \(f(a)\) and the other \(f'(a)\).
  • Right almost always, and right here: \(60^{\circ}\) is both nearest and best.

Under exam pressure this is the rule to use. It takes two seconds.

The honest rule

  • Take the smallest \(|f''(a)|\,dx^2\).
  • Use it only to break a tie between two nearly equidistant anchors.
  • "Exact" alone is not the criterion: \(180^{\circ}\) is perfectly legal and 2215 times worse.
  • "Nearest" means nearest among the anchors that exist. A hypothetical exact angle at \(61^{\circ}\) would be four times better, so \(60^{\circ}\) is not magic, only best available.

The quadratic model also has limits: it predicts the 30-versus-90 comparison in the right direction but off by a third in size, because at a 30-degree step \(dx\) is no longer small.

05 · What the estimate is actually worth

Do not assert that the method works. Measure it.

The estimate rounds to \(0.8835\); the truth rounds to \(0.8829\). Three correct decimal places, not four. Across a twelve-entry trigonometric catalog, the correct-place counts are \(3, 3, 3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3, 3\), and not one of the twelve reaches four. Q12.1 found the same for roots, where only 5 of 21 managed it.

This settles what Q12.3 means. When Final Exam 1 item 2 says "estimate each of the values below to four decimal places", it can only mean write four decimal places. The marker's answer is the differential value, so \(0.4698\) is correct for \(\cos 62^{\circ}\) even though the true value is \(0.4695\). Say so on the page if you have room.

The two-second check

Is the answer inside \([-1, 1]\)? It is a sine. This one line alone kills the degrees blunder, and nothing else in category 12 offers a check this cheap.

The magnitude check

Is it a little above \(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 0.866\)? You stepped up from \(60^{\circ}\) by a little. Is the correction about \(0.017\)? That is \(\pi/180\), one degree in radians.

The bound, and where to take it

The remainder is \(\dfrac{|f''(\xi)|}{2}dx^2\) over the interval. The anchor bound \(5.2761\times10^{-4}\) is violated by the actual error \(5.3110\times10^{-4}\); the target bound \(5.3792\times10^{-4}\) holds. Take the maximum of \(|f''|\) over the whole interval, never at the anchor.

If you want it better

One more Taylor term, \(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot\dfrac{(\pi/90)^2}{2}\), gives \(0.8829511\) and is 152 times more accurate. It is not what this question asks for, but the extra accuracy costs one line.

And the direction flips for \(\tan\) and \(\sec\), both concave up at their anchors, so a student who learned "linearization overestimates" from three sine problems gets both wrong.

Family 2The argument is already in radians

Trigger: no degree sign. Tool: no conversion, and often a flat anchor where \(f'(a) = 0\), which makes the linearization a constant. \(\sin 1.6\) from \(\pi/2\) estimates as exactly \(1\); \(\cos 0.05\) from \(0\) estimates as exactly \(1\). That is not an error, it is what a horizontal tangent means - but it is also why those two are among the least accurate entries, since the whole error then sits in the quadratic term.

Family 3The anchor is zero, and the answer is a small-angle rule

Linearizing at \(0\) gives \(\sin x \approx x\), \(\tan x \approx x\), and \(\cos x \approx 1\). These are the small-angle formulas. Note carefully that \(\cos\) gives \(1\), not \(1 - x^2/2\): the familiar quadratic rule is a second-order Taylor result and is not what this method produces. \(\sin\) and \(\tan\) do unusually well here because \(f''(0) = 0\) makes their error cubic - shrink the step by 10 and the error falls by about 1000, not 100.

Family 4A trigonometric function inside a composite

Trigger: \(x\sin x\), \(\sin 2x\), \(\sin(x^2)\). Tool: product or chain rule for \(f'\), then the identical five moves. Worth staring at: \(\sin(2x)\) at \(x = 31^{\circ}\) is \(\sin 62^{\circ}\), and the method reproduces the same double from the other side, because the chain factor 2 doubles the correction while the halved step halves it back. Drop the chain factor and you halve the answer's correction, which is the error MT1 items 7, 8 and 9 all test in a row.

Family 5Inverse trigonometric functions

\(\arctan 1.02\) from \(1\) gives \(\dfrac{\pi}{4} + 0.01 = 0.7953982\), with \(f'(a) = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2} = \dfrac12\) - the same slope as the headline question by a completely unrelated route. \(\arcsin 0.51\) from \(0.5\) gives \(\dfrac{\pi}{6} + \dfrac{0.02}{\sqrt3} = 0.5351458\), and is an underestimate. The argument of an inverse trig function is a number, not an angle, so there is nothing to convert; the answer, however, comes out in radians.

Family 8How good is it

Trigger: "to four decimal places", "estimate the error in your estimate". Tool: \(\dfrac{\max|f''|}{2}dx^2\) over the interval, plus honesty about rounding. The place ordering is not simply by \(|f''|\), because rounding boundaries interfere: \(\sqrt{16.4} \approx 4.05\) agrees with the truth to \(3\times10^{-4}\) and yet reports zero correct decimal places, because \(4.05\) rounds to \(4.1\) where the truth rounds to \(4.0\). Quantize before claiming a place count; never infer it from the size of the error.

07 · The trap list, ranked

  1. Not converting degrees to radians. Produces \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + 1 = 1.8660\), an error of \(0.983\), which is 1851 times the correct method's and \(111\) percent in relative terms. It is the only error here that a free check catches, because no sine exceeds 1. It has essentially no hiding place: across the whole first quadrant it never coincides with the truth, except at \(90^{\circ}\) where \(\cos a = 0\) kills the term it corrupted.
  2. Spot-checking at the anchor. Of eight plausible wrong formulas, six agree with the truth exactly when \(dx = 0\), because they differ from it only in the \(dy\) term. Setting \(dx = 0\) to "check the setup" is a test that cannot fail. The two survivors are the two that corrupt the \(f(a)\) term instead.
  3. Dropping \(f'(a)\), or using the wrong slope. Each classic slip hides exactly where the factor it touches equals 1: forgetting \(f'(a)\) is invisible at \(a = 0\) where \(\cos a = 1\); using \(\sin a\) as the slope is invisible at \(45^{\circ}\) where \(\sin a = \cos a\); a wrong sign on \(dy\) is invisible at \(90^{\circ}\) where \(\cos a = 0\). At \(60^{\circ}\) with \(dx = 2^{\circ}\) none of the eight hides, so the exam's own numbers are a safe place to check.
  4. Evaluating \(f'\) at the target instead of the anchor. Nastier than the rest because it is asymptotically invisible: its error shrinks with \(dx\) exactly as the correct answer's does, so a smaller step cannot expose it. Pleasant consequence: the two choices bracket the true value and their average is within \(10^{-5}\).
  5. Reporting the true value instead of the estimate. If you check yourself with a calculator, resist "correcting" \(0.8835\) to \(0.8829\). The question asked for the linearization.
  6. Answering an error question with \(f(a) + dy\). In Family 6, "estimate the error" wants \(dy\) alone.
  7. Assuming linearization always overestimates. True for \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) near \(60^{\circ}\); false for \(\tan\), \(\sec\) and \(\arcsin\). Look at \(f''\) every time.
  8. Choosing an exact anchor that is not the nearest. \(180^{\circ}\) is perfectly legal and 2215 times worse than \(60^{\circ}\).
  9. Claiming four correct decimal places. Not one of the twelve trigonometric catalog entries delivers four.
  10. Using a bound taken only at the anchor. For this very question the anchor bound is \(5.2761\times10^{-4}\) and the actual error is \(5.3110\times10^{-4}\), so the bound is violated.

08 · Drill set

On paper, in radians, no calculator until the end. Score sign separately from magnitude. A wrong magnitude is an arithmetic slip and sends you back to section 03; a wrong sign means you did not look at \(f''\) and sends you back to Family 7. Different failures, different fixes.

Nine over and three under, so answering "overestimate" every time scores 9 out of 12 and teaches you nothing. The errors span five orders of magnitude between numbers 5 and 11, which is why "linearization is accurate" is not a statement that survives without a step size attached.
