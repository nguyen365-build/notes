Build the Integrand

Q19.1, Q19.2 and Q19.3 all handed you a function and asked you to integrate it. This one does not. Nothing here is a function until you build one out of a picture, and the picture's entire content is that a chain raised at one end has slices that travel different distances.

THE STEM
A chain lying on the ground is 10 m long and its mass is 80 kg. How much work is required to raise one end of the chain to a height of 6 m?
THE ANSWER
$144g = 1411.2\ \text{J}$
TYPE INTO MÖBIUS
1411.2

The picture is the question

Because 6 m is less than the chain's 10 m, only part of the chain leaves the ground. The final arrangement is 6 m hanging vertically and 4 m still lying down, which splits the mass 48 kg that moves and 32 kg that never does. Every slice of the hanging part rose a different distance: the slice that ends at height $y$ started on the ground, so it rose exactly $y$.

1Draw the final picture, choose a slicing variable, and say where the origin is. The integrand depends on that choice; the answer does not.
2Write the weight of one slice. Here $\lambda = 80/10 = 8$ kg/m, so a slice of thickness $dy$ weighs $8g\,dy = 78.4\,dy$ newtons.
3Read the rise of that slice off the picture. This is where the question is won or lost.
4Add them up. The work is the integral, over the range of that variable, of the weight of the slice times the rise of the slice.
$$W = \int_0^{6} 78.4\,y\,dy \;=\; 78.4\left[\frac{y^{2}}{2}\right]_0^{6} \;=\; 78.4 \times 18 \;=\; 1411.2\ \text{J}$$

The same work, two origins

On a modelling question the strongest available check is not to redo the arithmetic - it is to rebuild the integrand from a different origin. Slice by height above the ground and the rise is $y$ over $[0,6]$. Slice by arclength from the raised end and the rise is $\max(6-s,0)$ over the whole chain $[0,10]$. Different variable, different origin, different limits, and the two areas are the same number.

RouteVariable and originIntegralVerdict
A$y$, height above the ground $\int_0^6 78.4\,y\,dy$ use this one under pressure
B$s$, arclength from the raised end $\int_0^{10} 78.4\max(6-s,0)\,ds$ the independent check
C$u$, arclength from the grounded end $\int_4^{10} 78.4\,(u-4)\,du$ correct, rarely worth it
Dcentre of mass of the moving part $48 \times 9.8 \times 3$ an eight-second check, not the written method
Is the centre-of-mass shortcut valid here? Yes - but only on the part that moves. The hanging 6 m is uniform, so its centre of mass rises from $0$ to $3$ m and $48\times 9.8\times 3 = 1411.2$ J. It works because the rise is linear in the slicing variable, so the average rise equals the rise of the average position. Apply the same $3$ m to the full $80$ kg and you get $2352$ J, because $32$ kg never left the ground. Replace the rise $y$ with something non-linear such as $y^{2}/6$ and the shortcut returns $705.6$ J against a true $940.8$ J.

Weight or mass? The mark nobody sees themselves lose

This stem gives a mass, in kilograms, so $g$ belongs in the integrand. Q19.5, the next question in this category, is stated in foot-pounds, so inserting $g$ there is the same mistake running the other way. No numerical check can see either one.

$1411.2$ is exactly 30 percent of the trivial upper bound and exactly half of the tight one. Neither is a coincidence: the first is $H/(2L)$, and the second is the statement that a uniform chain's average rise is half its maximum rise. That makes the halving a real check rather than a numerological one.

Exactly one candidate hides. Measuring each slice's rise downward as $6-y$ rather than $y$ returns $1411.2$ J from a wrong picture, because $\int_0^6 y\,dy = \int_0^6 (6-y)\,dy = 18$ - the rise is symmetric about the interval's midpoint, so reversing it changes nothing. That symmetry breaks the moment the interval does not start at zero. Nine of the seventeen candidates sit on a value some other mistake also produces, so "two methods agreed" is weak evidence here unless the two methods used different origins.

So this page changes what you should reach for. On an area or net-change question the sign is the cheap discriminator. On a work question there is no sign to check, and the magnitude bracket is the only cheap check that discriminates.

Family 7 is the reason the two shapes matter. It produces a structurally identical integral from the opposite physical argument - the whole object travels the whole distance and it is the force that changes. Writing the right integral for the wrong reason is how a student passes this question and fails the next one.

The decision tree

Is a WORK question in front of you?
|
+-- Does the WHOLE object move the SAME distance?
|      YES -> W = (total weight) x (distance).  No integral.        [Family 2]
|      NO  -> continue
|
+-- Is it the FORCE that varies, one object on one path?
|      YES -> W = int F(x) dx over the path.        [Families 7, 9, 10]
|      NO  -> it is the DISTANCE that varies; slice it.
|
+-- SLICE: pick a variable, STATE the origin
|      chain raised at one end   -> y = height,  rise = y,   0..h
|      chain hanging, hauled up  -> d = depth,   rise = d,   0..L
|      liquid pumped out         -> y = layer,   rise = top - y
|
+-- WEIGHT of one slice
|      stem gave MASS (kg)      -> multiply by g
|      stem gave WEIGHT (N, lb) -> do NOT multiply by g
|
+-- W = int (weight of slice) x (rise of slice)
|
+-- CHECK
|      units    -> must come out in J (or ft-lb)
|      moving?  -> is it really ALL of the mass?
|      bracket  -> 0 < W < (moving weight) x (max rise)
|      halfway  -> a uniform chain gives exactly half that bound
|
+-- PREVIEW the Mobius box.  Number only, no unit suffix.

What was verified. The answer was derived from three different origins plus a centre-of-mass argument, all agreeing to the last bit of floating point, and evaluated by exact antiderivative, by exact rational arithmetic ($g=49/5$ gives $W = 7056/5$), by Gauss-Legendre, by tanh-sinh and by Simpson. The observed quadrature order on this integrand is unmeasurable - it is linear, so Simpson is exact and every error on the ladder is roundoff; exactness was asserted instead, and the instrument was demonstrated on a smooth control converging to 4.00 and a cusp control converging to $5/3$. Route B's integrand does have a corner, at $3/5$ of its interval, where Simpson's measured order is 2, not 4, while every $n$ divisible by 10 reports a spurious zero error. Units were checked as a separate dimensional gate with seeded rejections. Full working: notes\Q19.4 - Work, raising one end of a chain.md.
