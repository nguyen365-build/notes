The Layer Ledger

Five derivatives, every one of them a stack of layers. Nothing here needs a rule you did not already have after Q8.1. What it needs is somewhere to write four factors down, because the way this question takes marks is not a wrong rule, it is a lost factor.

The number to carry across, not rediscover

On a ten-digit calculator the centred-difference optimum is \(h = 10^{-3}\), which clears three significant figures on every function Q8.2 tried. \(h = 10^{-5}\) gives relative errors up to \(1.0\), meaning no correct figures at all on two of the five. Use \(h = 0.001\) here too, and do not go smaller.

Q8.3's own contribution is depth

Q8.1's trees were two levels. Q8.2's were two levels run twice. Here every part but one is three levels, and the middle level is often a different rule rather than a different function. So the new content is not mathematics, it is bookkeeping.

1 · The ledger

Number the layers, one line each, and the answer is the product down the column. The ledger also gives you a count, and the count is the cheapest possible check.

2 · A rule inside a rule

A quotient inside a chain, a chain inside a quotient, a chain inside a product. The sub-derivation is itself a multi-step job, so holding it in your head is where the factor goes missing.

3 · How to see a dropped factor

A derivative with one factor missing is still a plausible-looking expression: right shape, right functions, right arguments, wrong answer. The detector that is not obvious: a dropped factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1, so a numeric check at a badly chosen point cannot find it. Section 06 has the picture.

03The procedure, and the ledger

Step 3 is the step Q8.3 adds. Number the layers from the outside in. Give each one its own line, and on that line write only its derivative, with everything below it left completely untouched. Stop when a layer is a polynomial, a bare \(x\), or a constant.

Step 4. Any layer that needs a rule of its own gets its own separate ledger. Only its result goes back upstairs.

Step 5. Multiply down the right-hand column, then count. \(n\) layers must produce \(n\) factors.

04The five parts, as ledgers

(a)  \(y = \sin x\cdot\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)\)  ·  product at the top

Read the notation first. \(\sin x^2\) means \(\sin\left(x^2\right)\): the exponent is on the argument. \(\sin^2 x\) would be \((\sin x)^2\), and the two readings are genuinely different functions, giving \(-0.3180\) and \(-0.0872\) at \(x=1\).

The last operation is a multiplication, so the product rule, with \(u=\sin x\) and \(v=\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)\). Then \(v\) gets its own ledger.

Assemble with \(u'v + uv'\), which is symmetric so there is no order to get wrong:

$$y' = \cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$$

The second term has four factors and every one is doing work. The minus in front of it came from layer 1 and nowhere else, so if you cannot say which layer produced a sign, you have not finished the ledger.

Check. \(y'(0) = 1\) exactly, because the second term carries a factor of \(2x\) and vanishes. That is a good three-second check and it is blind to the second term, so it is not enough on its own.

(b)  \(y = \left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}\)  ·  a quotient inside a chain

Two layers, but layer 2 is a full quotient rule, so it gets its own ledger first.

   w = (1 + x^3) / (1 - x^2)          quotient rule, u'v - uv' over v^2

The exponent

\(\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23\), so the surviving power is \(w^{-2/3}\) and not \(w^{-1/3}\). Subtracting 1 from a fraction is where this goes wrong. Say it out loud rather than computing it.

Two other routes, both evaluated. Splitting the power gives \(y = \left(1+x^3\right)^{1/3}\left(1-x^2\right)^{-1/3}\) and a product rule, which avoids the quotient rule but produces two terms and a \(-\frac43\) exponent. Logarithmic differentiation is shortest of all and is the wrong choice on this paper: it needs \(\ln\), and it needs the base positive, which fails on \(x>1\). Both were verified identical to the main route over twelve points.

Check. \(x=\frac12\) is the rehearsal point, because everything is exact: \(w = \frac32\) and \(w' = 3\), so \(y' = \left(\frac23\right)^{2/3} \approx 0.7631428\) and \(y = \left(\frac32\right)^{1/3}\).

The 4 is a count, not a number

It is \(2\times2\), one 2 from each square root. Three roots give 8, and a root over a cube root gives \(2\times3=6\). Layer 3 contributes nothing here because the derivative of \(1+x\) is 1, which is exactly why part (c) breeds the habit of forgetting there is an innermost layer at all.

Route B is one line. Square both sides: \(y^2 = 1+\sqrt{1+x}\), so \(2y\,y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}}\) and \(y' = \frac{1}{4y\sqrt{1+x}}\), where \(y\) is the original function. Verified identical. It generalises: for any \(y=\sqrt{\text{stuff}}\), \(y' = \frac{(\text{stuff})'}{2y}\).

Check. \(x=8\) is the rehearsal point because every root closes: \(1+x=9\), \(\sqrt9=3\), \(1+3=4\), \(\sqrt4=2\). So \(y(8)=2\) and \(y'(8) = \frac{1}{4\cdot2\cdot3} = \frac1{24}\) exactly.

(d)  \(y = \frac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{x^2-2x-8}\)  ·  a chain inside a quotient

The trap

\(u' = 2x\)

The numerator is \(\left(x^2-1\right)^{1/2}\), not \(x^2-1\). This is Q8.1's trap 1 wearing a different hat.

The truth

\(u' = \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\)

The \(\frac12\) from the root and the \(2x\) from the quadratic cancel, so no coefficient survives.

With \(u=\sqrt{x^2-1}\), \(v=x^2-2x-8\), \(v'=2x-2\), the quotient rule gives the answer as written, and that line is worth full marks. Then comes the one simplification on this whole question that pays, and it is not tidiness: the raw answer has a fraction inside a fraction. Multiply top and bottom by \(\sqrt{x^2-1}\):

$$x\left(x^2-2x-8\right) - \left(x^2-1\right)(2x-2)$$

$$= x^3-2x^2-8x-2x^3+2x^2+2x-2 \;=\; -x^3-6x-2$$

$$y' = \frac{-\left(x^3+6x+2\right)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$$

The \(x^2\) terms cancel exactly, which is a free check on the expansion. If an \(x^2\) survives, the expansion slipped.

What the simplification buys you, beyond readability

\(x^2-2x-8 = (x-4)(x+2)\), so \(y\) has poles at \(4\) and \(-2\), both inside \(|x|\ge1\), and the domain is four pieces. And \(x^3+6x+2\) has exactly one real root, at \(x \approx -0.32748\), located by a sign sweep. That root lies inside \((-1,1)\) where \(\sqrt{x^2-1}\) is not real, so \(y'\) never vanishes and \(y\) has no critical point at all. You can only see that once you have done the simplification.

Check. At \(x=3\): \(x^2-1=8\), \(x^2-2x-8=-5\), numerator \(27+18+2=47\). So \(y(3) = -\frac{2\sqrt2}{5}\) and \(y'(3) = -\frac{47\sqrt2}{100} \approx -0.6646804\).

(e)  \(y = \sec^2\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)\)  ·  three layers, three rules

The last operation is the squaring: \(\sec^2 u\) means \((\sec u)^2\), so the outer function is the power and not the secant. The full ledger is the figure in section 03. Layer 3 comes out in one line from the linear-over-linear formula:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{ax+b}{cx+d} = \frac{ad-bc}{(cx+d)^2}, \qquad\text{here}\qquad ad-bc = -2-1 = -3$$

That formula was verified over 77 coefficient sets at four points each, so it is a family fact and not a lucky coincidence. The numerator is always a constant, because \(u'v-uv'\) is degree \(\le1\) and the \(x\) terms always cancel.

Where the -6 comes from

It is \(2\times(-3)\): the 2 from the power rule on the square, the \(-3\) from the quotient rule on the innermost layer. Two sources, and missing either gives you \(-3\) or \(+2\) and an answer that looks entirely reasonable.

Check. \(x=5\) is the rehearsal point because \(v(5) = \frac63 = 2\) exactly. \(y(5) = \sec^2 2 \approx 5.7743992\) and \(y'(5) = -\frac23\sec^2 2\tan 2 \approx 8.4115283\). The answer is positive, because \(\tan 2\) is negative, and that surprises people who read the \(-6\) and expect a negative number.

05How much to simplify

Q8.1's policy is that simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk, so the default is to stop the moment the derivative is written. Q8.2 added the case where \(f'\) is an input to another differentiation. Q8.3 sharpens the remaining exception, and it is narrower than "when a factor cancels".

Simplify when the raw answer contains a fraction inside a fraction. Not because it is untidy, but because a stacked fraction is the one form a reader cannot check and a later question cannot use.

06Verify before you commit

The method is Q8.1's and the \(h\) is Q8.2's measured one. What Q8.3 adds is the choice of \(x\), and it is not a detail.

The blind spot

A dropped factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1. So a check at a point where any layer's factor happens to be 1 cannot detect a drop in that layer, however many figures agree.

The practical rule that follows is short. Avoid \(x=\frac12\) when the inner derivative is \(2x\). Avoid \(x=0\) when a factor is \(\cos\left(x^2\right)\) or \(\cos x\). Avoid \(x=1\) when a factor is \(x^n\). When in doubt, check at two points, chosen so no factor is near 1 at both.

07Answer entry

Both exams are delivered through Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a right derivative with a bad keystroke scores zero. Q8.1 has the general rules and Q8.2 the exponent-position rules; these are the ones Q8.3 adds.

The most expensive typo

((1+x^3)/(1-x^2))^-2/3

That is the whole expression divided by 3. Bracket the fractional exponent: ^(-2/3). The preview shows the difference immediately.

The second most expensive

sec^2((x+1)/(x-2))

Most parsers refuse it or read it as something else. Put the exponent on the closing bracket: sec((x+1)/(x-2))^2. This is the second paper in a row where it appears.

The safety valve

Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence, not string match, so part (d) can be entered exactly as the quotient rule produced it, internal fraction and all, and part (c) as the raw two half-powers. When you are unsure of an algebraic step, enter the form you are sure of.

Row 5 is the trap in miniature. The first term \(\sin\left(x^2\right)\), from differentiating the bare \(x\), is the one that gets dropped. The product rule is symmetric, which means both halves are equally forgettable.

09The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

  1. Dropping the innermost layer's factor. Family 8 is the whole catalog. The number one mark-loser in the course, worst on a three-level tree because there are more places for it to hide, and the factor count catches it without any arithmetic.
  2. Getting the fractional exponent wrong in (b). \(\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23\). Say it out loud rather than computing it.
  3. Writing \(u'=2x\) in (d). The numerator is \(\left(x^2-1\right)^{1/2}\). Trap 1 wearing a different hat.
  4. A sign slip expanding \(-\left(x^2-1\right)(2x-2)\) in (d). Four terms, four signs, and the free check is that the \(x^2\) terms must cancel.
  5. Reading \(\sec^2 v\) as \(\sec\left(v^2\right)\). It changes which rule the top layer needs, so it is a wrong method rather than a notation slip.
  6. The coefficient in (e). \(-6\) is \(2\times(-3)\). Count the sources.
  7. The sign of \(v'\) in (e). \((x-2)-(x+1) = -3\), not \(+3\).
  8. A 2 where the 4 belongs in (c). The coefficient is a count of the roots, not a number to remember.
  9. The double negative in (b)'s \(w'\). \(-\left(1+x^3\right)(-2x) = +2x+2x^4\).
  10. Reading \(\sin x^2\) as \(\sin^2 x\) in (a). Different functions, \(-0.3180\) and \(-0.0872\) at \(x=1\).
  11. Forgetting the first term of the product rule in (a). The product rule is symmetric, so both halves are equally forgettable.
  12. Checking at a point where a factor equals 1. The check passes and the answer is wrong.
  13. Using \(h\) too small on the calculator check. Q8.2 measured it: \(h=10^{-5}\) can give zero correct figures, so a right answer looks wrong and you "fix" it.
  14. Simplifying (a), (b) or (e) at all. There is nothing there, so any algebra is pure risk.
  15. Missing brackets on the fractional exponent at entry. ^(-2/3) versus ^-2/3.

11The one-page decision tree

"Compute the derivative of y = <expression>."
|
+-- STEP 1.  If I substituted a number, what would I do LAST?
|      + or -            -> each term separately, then ask again
|      x between two     -> PRODUCT   u'v + uv'   (symmetric, no order risk)
|         x-factors
|      /                 -> QUOTIENT  (u'v - uv')/v^2   <- ORDER MATTERS
|      ( )^n             -> CHAIN     n( )^(n-1) * (inner)'
|      sin/cos/tan/sec/  -> CHAIN     outer'(inner) * (inner)'
|         csc/cot/sqrt
|      nothing left      -> POWER     n x^(n-1)   or   0
|
+-- STEP 2.  Write the rule with BLANKS first.  Then name u and v.
|
+-- STEP 3.  BUILD THE LEDGER.  Outside in, one layer per line.
|            Each line holds ONLY that layer's derivative, with
|            everything below it left completely UNTOUCHED.
|
|              layer 1  (outermost)  ->  ...
|              layer 2               ->  ...
|              layer 3  (innermost)  ->  ...
|
+-- STEP 4.  Any layer needing its OWN rule gets its OWN ledger,
|            on its own lines.  Only the RESULT goes back upstairs.
|              a quotient inside   -> u'v - uv' over v^2, three lines
|              linear over linear  -> (ad - bc)/(cx+d)^2, ONE line
|
+-- STEP 5.  Multiply down the right-hand column.  Then COUNT.
|            n layers  ->  n factors.  Look at your answer and count.
|
+-- STEP 6.  Simplify ONLY if:
|              a fraction is stacked INSIDE a fraction  -> yes, clear it
|              two coefficients can multiply             -> yes, one step
|              a factor cancels top and bottom           -> yes
|              a bracket would have to be EXPANDED       -> NO
|              a trig product would be DISTRIBUTED       -> NO
|
+-- STEP 7.  CHECK.  [f(x+0.001) - f(x-0.001)] / 0.002
             h = 0.001, NOT smaller.  (Q8.2 measured the valley.)
             PICK x so no layer's factor is near 1, or the check is BLIND.
               inner 2x     -> avoid x = 0.5
               inner cos    -> avoid x = 0
               inner x^n    -> avoid x = 1

      COUNT     n layers, n factors.  The missing one is the innermost.
      NAME IT   look for the innermost layer's signature string:
                (1-x^2)^2  /  sqrt(1+x)  /  sqrt(x^2-1)  /  (x-2)^2
      INNER     the inside of a bracket or trig function is UNCHANGED
      EXPONENT  n - 1, so 1/3 leaves -2/3.  Say it, do not compute it.
      SIGN      count the minus sources: cos, csc, cot, and ad - bc
      SUPERSCR  on the NAME -> power outer.  Inside the bracket -> named.
      RADIANS   before you evaluate anything with a pi in it
      TYPE IT   sec(...)^2 not sec^2(...).  ^(-2/3) not ^-2/3.
