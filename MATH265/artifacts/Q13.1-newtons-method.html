Three Steps, Four Digits

Newton's method is the Q12 tangent-line estimate fed back into itself. Iterating squares the error, so the digits you have correct roughly double every step. What does not double is the number of digits you are entitled to write down, and this question is built on the gap between the two.

01 The question

Use Newton's method to approximate the solution of the equation \(x^4 + 2x - 5 = 0\) in the interval \([1, 2]\). (5 points)

Five points, printed inline at the end of the stem in the Final Exam 1 style. The paper's fourteen items sum to 124, so this is 4.0323 percent of it. Item 7 carries no hint, no "show your work", and no precision demand at all - which is the first thing that separates it from its neighbour, item 2, where "to four decimal places" is the whole difficulty.

So the first decision is yours: how many digits to report. The rest of this page is about earning them.

WHAT IS GIVEN

An equation, and a bracket. The bracket is not decoration - it is an existence proof, and it is also a guarantee that nothing can go wrong.

WHAT IS ASKED

A number. There is no method mark for elegance here and no printed marking scheme anywhere in the course files.

WHAT IS TESTED

Whether you can differentiate, iterate without rounding, and stop at the right place.

02 The iteration, derived rather than recalled

Do not memorise the formula. Derive it, because the derivation is also the reason the method works. The tangent line to \(f\) at \(x_n\) is

$$y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)\,(x - x_n)$$

Set \(y = 0\) and solve for \(x\). That \(x\) is the next iterate:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$
That single line is the whole relationship to category 12. Q12 built a tangent line and evaluated it. Q13 builds a tangent line and finds its root. Solving a linearization for zero is exactly one Newton step, so the entire linear-approximation category is Newton's method stopped after one iteration.

Here \(f(x) = x^4 + 2x - 5\) and \(f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2\). Clearing the fraction once, before starting, turns two evaluations and a subtraction into a single quotient:

$$x_{n+1} \;=\; \frac{3x_n^4 + 5}{4x_n^3 + 2}$$

THE BRACKET IS AN EXISTENCE PROOF

\(f(1) = -2\) and \(f(2) = 15\). The signs differ and \(f\) is continuous, so the Intermediate Value Theorem puts a root strictly inside \((1,2)\). Two arithmetic evaluations, no calculus.

The examiner has already done the hardest part of a real root-finding problem for you: locating the root roughly.

AND A SAFETY GUARANTEE

\(f''(x) = 12x^2 \ge 0\) everywhere, so \(f\) is convex on the whole real line. And \(f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2\) is at least \(6\) on \([1,2]\).

A steep, convex, increasing function on the given interval. Section 06 shows that every way Newton can fail needs one of those three to break.

04 What "four decimal places" actually costs

The root is \(1.25593754\ldots\), and the four-decimal rounding tie point nearest above it is \(1.25595\). The distance between them is 1.2452e-05.

So an iterate approaching from above has to beat 1.2452e-05, not the 5e-05 that "four decimal places" appears to ask for. The two differ by a factor of four, and one iterate lands squarely between them.

THE ITERATE

\(x_3\) from \(x_0 = 1\) is \(1.2559647487\), with an error of 2.7201e-05.

THE OBVIOUS TEST

2.72e-05 is comfortably inside 5e-05. By the error criterion this iterate is good to four decimal places.

THE TRUTH

It rounds to 1.2560. The true root rounds to 1.2559. The iterate is on the wrong side of the tie point and the answer is wrong.

Never infer a digit count from the size of an error. Q12.3 found this from the other direction, where \(\sqrt{16.4}\) agreed with its estimate to 3e-04 and scored zero correct places. The same fact, arriving here as a live trap rather than a curiosity.

Rounding the intermediate iterates destroys the method

This is the most expensive habit in the topic and it looks like carefulness. Newton can only square an error it is actually given, so rounding each iterate caps the accuracy the method can ever reach, no matter how many steps you take.

05 The convergence order, measured

"Newton's method is second order" is a claim, and a claim can be measured. Taking \(\log e_{n+1} / \log e_n\) along the run from \(x_0 = 1\):

from\(e_2, e_1\)\(e_3, e_2\)\(e_4, e_3\)\(e_5, e_4\)\(e_6, e_5\)\(e_7, e_6\)\(e_8, e_7\)
measured order2.04402.00992.00452.00232.00112.00062.0003

The order is 2, approached from above, halving its excess every step. Two cautions come out of the measurement itself.

THE LADDER MUST BE BOUNDED

Run at 220 digits, the ninth ratio reads 1.4925 and the tenth reads 1.0000, because the errors have hit the arithmetic's precision floor and stopped being errors. An unbounded ladder reports that Newton's method is first order.

THE CONSTANT MATTERS TOO

\(e_{n+1}/e_n^2\) converges to 0.9536409406443394, which is \(f''(r)/2f'(r)\) computed independently. Because it is close to 1, the digit count simply doubles. A large \(f''\) would delay the doubling by several steps.

AND IT GIVES YOU AN ERROR BAR

Once inside the regime, \(\text{err}(x_{n+1}) \approx C\,|x_{n+1} - x_n|^2\). Measured accuracy: 99.79 percent at \(x_3\), 99.9996 at \(x_4\), 100.0000 at \(x_5\). At \(x_1\) it is only 80 percent, so the estimate needs one good step first.

What the step size does and does not tell you

After computing \(x_{n+1}\), the quantity \(|x_{n+1} - x_n|\) is an excellent estimate of the error in \(x_n\) - 99.81 percent accurate at \(x_2\), 99.9996 at \(x_3\). It is a terrible estimate of the error in \(x_{n+1}\):

after\(x_2\)\(x_3\)\(x_4\)\(x_5\)
the step over-states err\((x_n)\) by22.5x529x280,593x7.87e10

That is a safe bound and a useless one. Use the step to grade the previous iterate; use \(|f(x_n)|/|f'(x_n)|\), the size of the step you are about to take, to grade the current one.

06 The four ways Newton fails, and why none of them can happen here

Category 12 has no analogue for this section. A single linearization is always defined; an iteration can run away, land on the wrong answer, or never settle at all.

MODE 1A flat \(f'(x_n)\) throws the iterate away

The step is \(f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\), so a small denominator means a large jump, and the damage scales as \(1/f'\).

\(x_0\)\(f'(x_0)\)\(x_1\)

Why it cannot happen here. \(f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2\) has exactly one zero, at \(x_c = -\sqrt[3]{1/2} = -0.7937005259840998\). On \([1,2]\) the derivative never falls below 6.

MODE 2Convergence to a different root

\(f\) has a second real root at \(-1.7027063709099214\). Scanning starting points across \([-20, 20]\) finds exactly one basin boundary, and it is \(x_c\) itself: every start to the right reaches 1.2559, every start to the left reaches -1.7027. That is unusually clean, and it is a consequence of \(f\) being convex everywhere with only two real roots. Panel 1 above is that split. \(x_0 = -0.8\) looks harmless and lands on the wrong root.

MODE 3Oscillation between two points

The famous failure is \(f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2\) from \(x_0 = 0\), which cycles \(0, 1, 0, 1, \ldots\) exactly and never approaches its real root at \(-1.7693\).

For \(x^4 + 2x - 5\) oscillation is impossible, and that is proved rather than merely unobserved. Setting \(N(N(x)) = x\) and clearing denominators gives a polynomial of degree 16. Every root of \(f\) is a fixed point of \(N\), so \(f\) divides it exactly, leaving a cofactor of degree 12 - and all twelve of its roots are complex, in six conjugate pairs. Run on \(x^3 - 2x + 2\), the same machinery returns a degree-6 cofactor with four real roots, which are two genuine 2-cycles: the famous \(\{0, 1\}\) and a second at \(\{0.133346,\; 1.024967\}\). A census that finds two cycles where cycles exist, and none here, is a measurement. One that always returned none would look identical.

MODE 4Outright divergence

Not available for this \(f\) either. The standard exhibit is \(f(x) = x^{1/3}\), where the iteration collapses to \(N(x) = -2x\), so the iterate doubles and flips sign for ever: 0.1, -0.2, 0.4, -0.8, 1.6, -3.2, 6.4.

The positive statement. For a convex increasing function, starting anywhere with \(f(x_0) > 0\) gives monotone convergence, guaranteed. \(f(2) = 15 > 0\), so \(x_0 = 2\) is the provably safe start and its iterates do descend to the root without crossing it. \(f(1) = -2 < 0\), so \(x_0 = 1\) fails that condition and overshoots to \(4/3\) on its first step - after which it is monotone. Both converge; the theorem is what lets you promise it in advance.

07 The blind spot: checking your answer cannot catch a wrong formula

The natural self-check is to put the answer back into the iteration and see whether it moves. It does not work here, and the reason is structural.

corruption of Newton's formuladoes it fix the true root?
\(x + f/f'\)   sign flippedyes
\(x - f/f''\)   wrong derivative belowyes
\(f' = 4x^3\)   lost the \(+2\)yes
\(f' = 4x^4+2\)   power not droppedyes
\(x - f\cdot f'\)   multipliedyes
\(x - f(x)\)   forgot to divideyes
\(x - f'/f\)   invertedno
\(f/f'\)   dropped the \(x\) termno

Six of the eight leave the true root exactly where it is. The mechanism: \(f(r) = 0\), so any correction term that multiplies \(f\) vanishes at the root whatever else is wrong with it. The two survivors are precisely the two that do not multiply \(f\). So substitute into \(f\), never into the iteration.

This is category 13's version of Q12's zero-increment blind spot and Q11's zero-rate blind spot, and it is worse than both: those hide at a degenerate input you can avoid, while this one hides at the answer, the one place you would naturally look. One consolation - at \(x_0 = 1.5\) and at \(x_0 = 2\) none of the eight corruptions agrees with the truth, so either is a safe place to sanity-check the formula itself. Only \(x = 1\) is poisoned, because \(x^4 = x^3\) there.

08 The variation catalog

Every family below is the same three moves - write \(f = 0\), differentiate, iterate. What changes is only where \(f\) comes from and what can go wrong.

FAMILY 0A polynomial root inside a stated bracket

The headline shape. Six worked instances, and not one needs more than four steps from the bracket midpoint - four of the six need three. If you are on step six, you have made an arithmetic error, not a bad choice of \(x_0\).

FAMILY 1Choosing \(x_0\)

Steps to 1.2559 by starting point: \(x_0=1\) needs 4, \(x_0=1.2\) needs 2, \(x_0=1.25\) needs 2, \(x_0=1.3\) needs 2, \(x_0=1.5\) needs 3, \(x_0=2\) needs 5. The spread is three whole steps on a question that only needs three, and the payoff saturates - getting inside \([1.2, 1.3]\) is worth two steps and getting closer is worth nothing. With no bracket given, evaluate \(f\) at consecutive integers until the sign flips.

FAMILY 2A transcendental equation

Subtract to make \(f\), then proceed unchanged. \(\cos x = x\) gives 0.7391; \(xe^x = 1\) gives 0.5671; \(\ln x = 1/x\) gives 1.7632; \(e^x = 3x\) gives 0.6191; \(\sin x = x/2\) gives 1.8955; \(x = \cos^2 x\) gives 0.6417. Every one reaches four decimals within four steps.

Two traps live only here. \(\sin x = x/2\) also has \(x = 0\) as a root, and Newton from 0.5 collapses onto it, so rewriting as \(f = 0\) can hand you a root the question did not want. And \(e^x = 3x\) has a second intersection at 1.5121, so the bracket is not optional even when it looks like decoration.

FAMILY 4A reciprocal with no division

\(f(x) = 1/x - a\) gives \(x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - a x_n)\), which contains no division at all - this is how computers actually divide. From \(x_0 = 0.1\), \(1/7\) runs 0.13, 0.1417, 0.14284777, 0.1428571422, and the run of correct digits doubles visibly. This family has a convergence window the others do not: it converges only for \(0 < x_0 < 2/a\), diverges immediately from 0.5 when \(a = 7\), and maps \(x_0 = 2/a\) to zero, where it sticks.

FAMILY 5The intersection of two curves

Identical to Family 2 - subtract, then find a root. \(x^3\) meets \(x+1\) at 1.3247; \(e^x\) meets \(3x\) at 0.6191; \(\cos x\) meets \(x\) at 0.7391; \(x^2\) meets \(\cos x\) at 0.8241. The only new thing is remembering to report the \(y\) value too if the question asks for the point.

FAMILY 6"Carry out exactly \(N\) iterations"

The answer is \(x_2\), not the root. From \(x_0 = 1\), \(x_2 = 391/310 = 1.2612903226\); from 1.5, \(x_2 = 1.2579188022\); from 2, \(x_2 = 1.3243105219\). Those three disagree in the second decimal place, so "give \(x_2\)" has no answer without the stem's \(x_0\), and a remembered iterate from a different start is a guaranteed zero.

FAMILY 7Diagnose a failure

Name which of section 06's four modes it is. The answer is a sentence, not a number.

FAMILY 8A multiple root, where quadratic convergence dies

When \(f\) and \(f'\) vanish together, convergence drops from quadratic to linear, at rate exactly \(1 - 1/m\). Measured on \((x-1)^m(x+3)\): ratio 0.50000000 at \(m=2\), 0.66666667 at \(m=3\), 0.75000000 at \(m=4\), 0.80000000 at \(m=5\). Four decimal places then takes about fourteen steps instead of three.

This is the sharpest caveat in the topic. "Correct digits double each step" holds only when \(f'(r) \ne 0\). Here \(f'(r) = 9.92\), comfortably nonzero, which is why the headline question behaves. Say that when you claim quadratic convergence and the claim becomes a statement instead of a slogan.

FAMILY 9How many steps for a stated accuracy

Count digits, not iterations. Find how many correct digits \(x_0\) has, then double until you pass the target. From 0.61 digits, four decimals needs three steps and six needs four. The doubling is the constant \(C = 0.9536\) being close to 1; when \(C\) is large the doubling starts later, and when \(f'(r) = 0\) it never starts.

09 The trap list

  1. Rounding the intermediate iterates. The single most expensive habit here, and it looks like carefulness. Rounding to 2 decimals caps the answer at an error of 4.06e-03 for ever, against 7.06e-10 at the same step in full precision.
  2. Stopping at the wrong iterate. \(x_3\) from \(x_0 = 1\) is inside 5e-05 and still rounds to 1.2560. The root sits 1.2452e-05 below its own tie point, so that is the number to beat.
  3. Reporting more digits than the iteration has earned. \(x_2\) from the midpoint prints as 1.2579188021959602 and exactly two of those digits are correct. Report only the digits two successive iterates agree on.
  4. Getting \(f'\) wrong. Every iterate inherits it, so a slip is not a small error - it is a different method converging somewhere else. Check \(f'\) once, before iterating.
  5. Trusting the "iterate again and it does not move" check. Six of eight corrupted formulas fix the true root. Substitute into \(f\), not into \(N\).
  6. Choosing \(x_0\) carelessly. Costs up to two steps here, and the whole question in Family 7.
  7. Answering the wrong question. "Two iterations" means report \(x_2\). "Approximate the solution" means report the root.
  8. Checking at \(x = 1\). Writing \(f' = 4x^4+2\) instead of \(4x^3+2\) is bit-identical to the truth there, because \(x^4 = x^3\). Check at 1.5 or 2 instead, where none of the eight corruptions hides.
