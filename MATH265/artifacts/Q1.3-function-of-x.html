Athabasca MATH 265 · Midterm 2 · Q1 · 4 points

Which equations define a function of x

This looks like a definition recital, and it is really a counting question. Fix an \(x\), count the \(y\) values that solve the equation, and everything follows. The one asymmetry that decides the marks: zero outputs is harmless, two outputs is fatal. Part (a) is on the paper to see whether you know the difference.

The answers
a  /  \(\dfrac{2y}{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}=1\)

Yes — a function

$$y=\frac{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$$

Domain \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{-3,0,3\}\), because the denominator \(\lvert x\rvert(\lvert x\rvert-3)\) vanishes there and those \(x\) get no \(y\) at all.

b  /  \(y^2+x^2=10\)

No — not a function

$$y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^{2}}$$

Witness: \(x=1\) gives \(y^2=9\), so both \(y=3\) and \(y=-3\) satisfy the equation. One input, two outputs.

The instruction that carries the marks

The paper prints (4 points) and the single word "Explain." There is no method restriction, so nothing is off limits, but a bare "a is, b is not" is an incomplete answer. A "no" needs one named witness pair. A "yes" needs a uniqueness argument and the domain.

One correction to your own review sheet

MATH265_Review.md line 641 answers part (a) with "Solve, one \(y\) per \(x\), function." The verdict and the reason are both right, and it silently drops the domain: the equation as written is undefined at \(x=0,3,-3\). That omission is worth a mark, and it is not a reason to change the verdict. Section 4 explains why.

The habit

Do not confuse "no output" with "two outputs". Only the second one breaks a function.

01

What this question is really testing

4 of the 94 points on midterm 2, and the cheapest question on the paper in minutes per mark. Five reflexes, all of which reappear later.

ReflexWhere it comes back
Solve an implicit equation for \(y\)Implicit differentiation, MT2 Q8, \(x^3y+xy^2=4xy+7\), which only makes sense because \(y\) is assumed to be a function of \(x\) locally
A vanishing denominator removes an \(x\)Q1.2 composite domains, and the vertical asymptote of \(\frac{x^2-3x-4}{x^2-16}\) in MT2 Q6
\(\lvert x\rvert\) splits into cases at a cornerQ2.2, graphing \(-3\lvert x-2\rvert\), and "not differentiable at \(x=-2\)" in MT2 Q10

The deeper point is the one the rest of calculus rests on. Limits, continuity and derivatives are all statements about a function, so before any of them apply you need to know that "\(y\)" is a single determined number once \(x\) is fixed. Implicit differentiation is the sharpest example: it differentiates \(y\) as though it were a function of \(x\), and that step is only legitimate on a piece of the curve where the answer to this question is yes.

The circle in part (b) is the standard illustration. You cannot call \(y\) a function of \(x\) on the whole circle, but you can on the top half, and that is why implicit differentiation of a circle still produces a meaningful \(\frac{dy}{dx}\).

02

The things you must have cold

2.1   The definition, stated so it can be tested

\(y\) is a function of \(x\) if, for every \(x\) in the domain, the equation determines exactly one value of \(y\).

"Well defined" in the question wording is not an extra condition. It is the same condition said with emphasis: the rule must define one output, with no ambiguity about which one.

The definition does NOT requireWhy part (a) is still a function
That every real \(x\) works\(x=0,\pm3\) produce no \(y\) at all, so they leave the domain and the question of uniqueness never arises for them
That the formula be one algebraic expression\(y=(x^2-3\lvert x\rvert)/2\) is really two formulas glued at \(x=0\), and a piecewise rule is a perfectly good function

2.2   Read the count table as one asymmetry

Students lose this mark in both directions, and the two mistakes are mirror images.

The mistakeWhat it confuses
Saying (a) is not a function because it blows up at \(x=0\)The domain with well-definedness
Saying (b) is a function because you can write \(y=\sqrt{10-x^2}\)Choosing a root with being given one

2.3   The degree of \(y\) is a cue, not a proof

Look at the highest power of \(y\). Fastest read available, right most of the time, and you must still confirm it.

power of yUsual verdictThe exception that makes it a cue
1functionWhere the coefficient of \(y\) is zero you get either no \(y\) or every \(y\)
evennot\(y^2+x^2=0\) has the single solution \(y=0\), a function on the one-point domain \(\{0\}\)
oddfunction\(y^3-3y=x\) is odd degree and not a function, because \(y^3-3y\) is not monotone
The honest version of the rule

It is about monotonicity in \(y\), not degree. If the \(y\)-side is strictly increasing or strictly decreasing, it crosses any level exactly once, so there is exactly one \(y\). Odd powers happen to be monotone; \(y^3-3y\) is not, which is why the cue fails there.

2.4   The three ways uniqueness actually dies

Every "not a function" instance in this course is one of three shapes. Learn the shape and you stop needing to think.

ShapeThe mechanismCanonical instance
Even power of \(y\)Undoing \(y^{2n}\) introduces \(\pm\)y²+x²=10 (part b)
\(\lvert y\rvert\)\(\lvert y\rvert=c\) with \(c>0\) has two solutions|y| = x
Periodic in \(y\)\(\sin y=c\) repeats foreversin y = x

There is a fourth, rarer shape: the coefficient of \(y\) collapses. In \(xy=0\), the value \(x=0\) leaves \(0\cdot y=0\), which every \(y\) satisfies. That is infinitely many outputs, and it is the exact opposite failure from part (a), where the collapsing coefficient left no \(y\). Family 10 is built around that pair.

2.5   Where the \(\lvert x\rvert\) in part (a) fits

It does nothing to the verdict. A decoration on the \(x\) side cannot threaten uniqueness, because uniqueness is a question about \(y\) only. Its only job is the domain, and here it does that in a way worth noticing:

$$x^2-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)$$

using \(x^2=\lvert x\rvert^2\). That factorisation beats splitting into cases: the product is zero exactly when \(\lvert x\rvert=0\) or \(\lvert x\rvert=3\), which is \(x\in\{0,3,-3\}\). The \(\lvert x\rvert\) is a decoy, and spotting a decoy quickly is worth as much as any technique.

03

The universal procedure

1. Isolate \(y\) as far as the algebra allows. Multiply out denominators, but write down what you multiplied by, because that is where the domain lives.

2. Count the \(y\)-values for a general \(x\). Highest power of \(y\) is the cue; monotonicity in \(y\) is the proof.

3. If the count is ever 2 or more, stop. Produce one specific \(x\) with two specific \(y\)-values. That single witness is a complete proof.

4. If the count is always 1, say so, then find where it is 0. Set every denominator to zero, every even radicand negative, every log argument non-positive.

5. State the verdict, the reason, and the domain. The word Explain is in the question.

04

Q1.3 worked four ways

Route A  /  solve for \(y\), then hunt the domain use this one

Part (a). The equation only says anything at all when the denominator is nonzero, so record that condition first, before touching the algebra:

$$x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\neq0 \iff \lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)\neq0 \iff x\neq0,\ x\neq3,\ x\neq-3.$$

Now multiply by that nonzero denominator and divide by \(2\):

$$2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y=\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}.$$

The right side is a single number for each admissible \(x\), so there is exactly one \(y\). Yes, a well defined function of \(x\), with domain \((-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)\).

Why the domain remark is not optional

At \(x=0\) the original equation reads \(\frac{2y}{0}=1\), which no \(y\) satisfies. So \(x=0\) contributes zero outputs, not two, and by the count test the verdict stands.

Here is the subtlety that makes this a 4-point question. The cleared equation \(2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\) is not the same relation as the original. At \(x=0\) the cleared version says \(2y=0\), which has the perfectly good unique solution \(y=0\), whereas the original says nothing at all. Multiplying through by a denominator added three points to the graph that were not there before. Both relations are functions, but with different domains, and the one the question asked about is the one with the three holes. Writing the domain down is how you show the marker you noticed.

Sanity checks. \(y\) is even, since \(x^2\) and \(\lvert x\rvert\) both are, so \(y(-x)=y(x)\). Spot values \(y(1)=-1\), \(y(2)=-1\), \(y(4)=2\), \(y(5)=5\). Substituting \(x=4\), \(y=2\) into the original gives \(\frac{4}{16-12}=1\), so the solved form really does satisfy the equation it came from.

The piecewise form, if you want it, is two upward parabola pieces that mirror each other across the \(y\)-axis, each with a hole punched at its own zero:

$$y=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2-3x}{2}, & x>0,\ x\neq3,\\[6pt]\dfrac{x^2+3x}{2}, & x<0,\ x\neq-3.\end{cases}$$

Part (b). \(y^2=10-x^2\), so \(y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^2}\). The \(\pm\) is the answer: for any \(x\) with \(x^2<10\) both roots are real and different. Do not stop there, because Explain wants the witness:

$$x=1:\quad y^2=10-1=9\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y=3\ \text{ and }\ y=-3,$$

and both \((1,3)\) and \((1,-3)\) satisfy \(y^2+x^2=10\). One input, two outputs. \(x=3\) works just as nicely and gives \(y=\pm1\).

Two remarks that show you understand the boundary, worth including if you have room. At \(x=\pm\sqrt{10}\) there is exactly one \(y\), namely \(0\), and for \(\lvert x\rvert>\sqrt{10}\) there is no real \(y\) at all. Neither rescues the relation, because a single bad \(x\) is already fatal.

Route D  /  geometric recognition

For (b), \(y^2+x^2=10\) is a circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\approx3.162\) centred at the origin, and the vertical line \(x=1\) cuts it twice. Fastest route if you recognise the conic, and the most persuasive single sentence available. For (a), \(y=\frac{\lvert x\rvert^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\) is the parabola \(\frac{t^2-3t}{2}\) in \(t=\lvert x\rvert\geq0\), made even in \(x\), with holes at its roots. Its limitation: it only works on curves you recognise, and \(y^3-3y=x\) has no name you know.

Under pressure

Use Route B to pick the side within seconds, then write it up as Route A for (a) and Route C for (b). That is the combination that gets all four marks. Route D is a free extra sentence on (b), and naming the curve reads as understanding rather than manipulation.

05

The exam-ready write-up

What you actually put on the page. Short on purpose: four points, two minutes.

  • a  —  yes, a well defined function of x

    The equation requires \(x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\neq0\). Since \(x^2=\lvert x\rvert^2\), we have \(x^2-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert(\lvert x\rvert-3)\), which is zero exactly when \(x=0\), \(x=3\) or \(x=-3\). For every other \(x\) we may multiply by the denominator to get \(2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\), so \(y=\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\).

    The equation is linear in \(y\) with nonzero coefficient, so each admissible \(x\) determines exactly one \(y\). Hence \(y\) is a function of \(x\) with domain \((-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)\). The three excluded values give no \(y\) at all, which removes them from the domain but does not affect well-definedness.

  • b  —  no, not a well defined function of x

    Solving gives \(y^2=10-x^2\), so \(y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^2}\), and the two signs give different values whenever \(x^2<10\). For example \(x=1\) gives \(y^2=9\), so both \(y=3\) and \(y=-3\) satisfy the equation. The single input \(x=1\) produces two different outputs, so \(y\) is not a function of \(x\).

    Geometrically the graph is the circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\) centred at the origin, and the vertical line \(x=1\) meets it twice, so it fails the vertical line test.

The three sentences carrying the marks

SentenceWhy it scores
"linear in \(y\) with nonzero coefficient, so exactly one \(y\)"This is the reason for (a), not the computation. Without it you have shown a formula, not argued uniqueness.
"both \(y=3\) and \(y=-3\) satisfy the equation"This is the whole proof for (b). A named witness beats any amount of talk about \(\pm\).
"the excluded values give no \(y\), which does not affect well-definedness"Shows you know zero and two are different failures. The difference between a complete and a partial answer on (a).

Answer-entry rules

The expensive failure mode here is not the parser

The paper says Explain, and 4 points split across two parts, so a machine-marked version almost certainly pairs a verdict widget with a free-text or upload explanation, or is a "select all that are functions" item. The way to lose marks is answering the verdict and leaving the explanation blank.

The single most expensive typo on this question is x^2-3*abs(x)/2, which parses as \(x^2-\frac{3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\) rather than \(\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\). Bracket the whole numerator before dividing.

Absolute value in Möbius is abs(x). The vertical-bar notation is not a reliable input in either grading engine, so do not use it in an answer box even though it is standard on paper.

The domain, if it is asked for

Not fully verified — settle it on the sample exam

My platform notes cover expression entry thoroughly but do not document Möbius interval-notation syntax. Treat this as a strategy rather than a guaranteed string.

Three exclusions means the interval answer has four pieces:

(-infinity,-3)U(-3,0)U(0,3)U(3,infinity)

If the box takes an inequality instead, the three conditions are x <> 0, x <> 3, x <> -3, where some engines want != instead. Write exact values, never decimals.

Always click preview

An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics. Preview is the only proof that the parser agrees with you; reading your own answer back is not. On a four-piece union this matters more than usual, because a mis-parsed union still looks tidy in the input box.

The variation catalog

Organised by the tool the variation forces, not by how the question looks. Two equations that look nothing alike but force the same tool are the same question.

Read these as structure, not as facts

Across all fourteen families there are only three reasons a relation fails: an even power of \(y\), an \(\lvert y\rvert\), or a periodic function of \(y\). Everything else on the "not a function" side is one of those three wearing a costume, plus the one structural failure where the coefficient of \(y\) collapses to zero. On the "is a function" side there is only one reason: the equation pins \(y\) down uniquely, by being linear in \(y\) or monotone in \(y\).

Trigger   an odd power of \(y\) plus a lower \(y\) term of opposite sign, like \(y^3-3y\).

09

The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

  1. Forgetting the domain on part (a). The verdict "function" is correct without it, but the question says Explain, and the three excluded values are the only non-trivial content in part (a). The most likely place to drop a mark while writing the right verdict.
  2. Excluding only \(x=0\) and \(x=3\), forgetting \(x=-3\). \(\lvert x\rvert=3\) has two solutions. The parity check catches this in three seconds.
  3. Deciding (a) is not a function because it is undefined somewhere. Zero outputs removes an \(x\) from the domain. Two outputs kills the function. The whole design of part (a) is to see whether you know the difference.
  4. Deciding (b) is a function by taking only the positive root. Writing \(y=\sqrt{10-x^2}\) is choosing an answer the equation did not authorise.
  5. Answering (b) with "\(\pm\), therefore not a function" and no witness. Right reason, but Explain wants the specific pair. Four extra words convert a partial mark into a full one.
  6. Multiplying through by the denominator and forgetting what you multiplied by. \(2y=x^2-3\lvert x\rvert\) is a different relation: it is defined at \(0,\pm3\) and the original is not. Write the exclusion down before you clear the fraction.
  7. Trusting the degree-in-\(y\) cue as a proof. Correct on both parts of the real question, but \(y^3-3y=x\) is odd-degree and not a function, and \(x^2+y^2=0\) is even-degree and is one.
  8. Bracket error entering \(\frac{x^2-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\). x^2-3*abs(x)/2 divides only the last term.
  9. Confusing "cannot solve for \(y\)" with "not a function". \(y^3+y=x\) has no elementary solution and is a perfectly good function.
  10. Confusing well-definedness with continuity on a piecewise variation. A rule with a jump can be well defined. A rule whose branches disagree at a shared endpoint cannot.
