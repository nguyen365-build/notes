MATH 265 · Midterm 2 · Question 1 · 4 points

Which equations define a function of x

This looks like an algebra question and is really a counting question. Fix an \(x\), count how many \(y\) satisfy the equation. Exactly one every time, and it is a function. Two or more for even a single \(x\), and it is not.

The question, and the two verdicts
Part a one y per x · function $$\frac{2y}{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}=1$$

\(y\) appears once, to the first power. Solving offers no choice, so there is always exactly one \(y\). The denominator removes three inputs.

Solved $$y=\frac{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$$ Domain $$(-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)$$ Type into Möbius (x^2-3*abs(x))/2 (-infinity,-3)U(-3,0)U(0,3)U(3,infinity)

Three removed points, so four intervals. The one people drop is −3.

Part b two y per x · not a function $$y^{2}+x^{2}=10$$

\(y\) appears squared. Freeing it needs an even root, and an even root brings a sign choice. One counterexample settles it.

The counterexample $$x=1:\quad y^{2}=9,\quad y=3 \ \text{ and } \ y=-3$$ Both check in the original $$3^{2}+1^{2}=10,\qquad (-3)^{2}+1^{2}=10$$ Type into Möbius, if the branches are asked for sqrt(10-x^2) -sqrt(10-x^2)

The circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623\) about the origin.

The one sentence that decides every question of this type

An equation defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\) when every \(x\) in the domain produces exactly one \(y\). An \(x\) that produces no \(y\) does not break the function; it is simply not in the domain. An \(x\) that produces two does break it, and one such \(x\) is enough.

SECTION 01

What this question is really testing

Question 1 is worth 4 of the 94 points on Midterm 2, and it is the cheapest four points on the paper once you know what is being counted. It is also the first question, which means the examiner is checking a definition before spending the rest of the paper assuming you have it.

The instruction is "Explain", and the two halves need opposite evidence

Part a claims something is ALWAYS true

So the explanation must be a general argument. Solve for \(y\) and point out that no sign choice ever appeared. The formula is the explanation.

Part b claims something FAILS

So the explanation must be one specific counterexample. A general argument is not needed and wastes time. Two integers are a complete proof.

Getting that asymmetry right is worth more than any algebra on this question. A student who writes "b is a circle" has described the picture without demonstrating the failure. A student who writes \(y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^{2}}\) and stops has done the algebra without saying what it proves.

Format warning

The sample paper prints "(4 points)" and reads like a written exam. The live delivery is Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, where the verdict is usually a tick box and the explanation becomes a separate box asking for the solved form, the domain, or the counterexample values. Prepare for both.

Note also that Midterm 2 prints each point value on the line above its question, the opposite convention to Midterm 1. Read the boundary rather than assuming the marker trails.

SECTION 02

The five things you must have cold

2.1  The definition, and the asymmetry inside it

Two clauses do all the work, and students usually remember only one. The "exactly one" clause is what part b fails. The "in the domain" clause is what part a survives.

At a given \(x\) the equation hasEffect on the verdict
exactly one \(y\)fine, that \(x\) is in the domain
no \(y\) at allfine, that \(x\) is not in the domain
two or more \(y\)fatal, it is not a function
every \(y\)fatal, it is not a function

The graphical name for the same statement is the vertical line test: no vertical line may meet the curve more than once. A vertical line that misses the curve entirely is allowed, and is exactly what "not in the domain" looks like.

The even root is dangerous only when you are the one who has to take it.

2.3  The identity this question is built on: \(x^{2}=\lvert x\rvert^{2}\)

Part a's denominator is written with an absolute value on purpose.

$$x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)$$ $$\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)=0\iff\lvert x\rvert=0\ \text{ or }\ \lvert x\rvert=3 \iff x=0,\ x=3,\ x=-3$$
The slip that costs the question

Treat the \(\lvert x\rvert\) as an \(x\), factor \(x^{2}-3x=x(x-3)\), and report \(x=0\) and \(x=3\). Substituting \(x=-3\) into that gives \(9-(-9)=18\), comfortably nonzero, so nothing warns you. Two exclusions, three intervals, and a confident wrong answer.

Two routes that cannot slip

Factor \(\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)\) using \(x^{2}=\lvert x\rvert^{2}\). Or avoid the bars entirely: set \(x^{2}=3\lvert x\rvert\), square both sides (safe, both sides are already non-negative) to get \(x^{4}=9x^{2}\), so \(x^{2}\left(x^{2}-9\right)=0\) and \(x=0,\pm3\).

A third route splits by sign, which you need anyway to graph it.

2.4  The restriction triggers

Four things, and only four things, remove points from a domain in this course. For part a only the first row fires, and it fires three times.

SECTION 03

The universal procedure

This works on every version of this question in the catalog.

  1. Find \(y\) and only \(y\). Ignore the \(x\) side entirely for a moment. Ask: to what power does \(y\) appear, and is it inside an absolute value, a root, or a trig function?
  2. Read the triage table and predict the count. You now already know the verdict. Everything after this is producing evidence for it.
  3. If the prediction is "one", solve for \(y\) and show that no choice appeared. Write the formula. That formula is the explanation.
  4. If the prediction is "two or more", stop solving and find one concrete counterexample. Pick the \(x\) that makes the arithmetic cleanest, produce two explicit \(y\), and check both in the original.
  5. Only if the answer was "yes": get the domain, from the ORIGINAL equation. Apply the trigger table and count the intervals.
  6. Write the one sentence. "Yes, because solving gives \(y=\ldots\), one value for each \(x\) with \(\ldots\)", or "No, because \(x=\ldots\) gives both \(y=\ldots\) and \(y=\ldots\)".
Where the wasted time goes

Step 4. Once you know it fails, a general formula is worth nothing extra. Two integers are a complete proof, and they take five seconds.

SECTION 04

Q1.3 worked, four ways

Part a  ·  \(\dfrac{2y}{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}=1\)

Route A: solve for \(y\)

Multiply both sides by the denominator, which is legal precisely because we are about to record that the denominator is nonzero:

$$2y=x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y=\frac{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}$$

Nowhere in that line did a choice appear. No square root was taken, no \(\pm\) was introduced, no branch was picked. Every \(x\) that survives the denominator produces one and only one \(y\).

The domain, from the original

$$x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=0,\ 3,\ -3$$ $$\operatorname{dom}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:x\neq0,\pm3\right\}=(-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)$$

Route B: count the roots

Write the equation as \(F(x,y)=0\) with

$$F(x,y)=\frac{2}{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}\,y-1=A(x)\,y-1$$

This is a linear equation in \(y\). A linear equation \(Ay=B\) has exactly one root when \(A\neq0\), no root when \(A=0\) and \(B\neq0\), and every \(y\) when \(A=B=0\). Here \(A(x)\) is a nonzero number divided by something, so it is never zero anywhere it is defined. Exactly one root, always. This route generalises to Families 4, 5 and 10, where there is no clean solved form to point at.

Route C: the graph

For \(x>0\) the branch is \(y=\dfrac{x(x-3)}{2}\), an upward parabola through \((0,0)\) and \((3,0)\) with its minimum at \(x=\dfrac32\), where \(y=-\dfrac98=-1.125\). For \(x<0\) it is the mirror image.

The detail that makes this question memorable

The three excluded \(x\)-values are \(0\), \(3\) and \(-3\), and those are exactly the three places where the solved formula would have given \(y=0\). So the picture is a smooth pair of parabolic arcs with three holes punched in it, all three sitting on the \(x\)-axis, at \((-3,0)\), \((0,0)\) and \((3,0)\). See the figure in Section 2.

Route D: the sign of \(\partial F/\partial y\)

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{2y}{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}-1\right)=\frac{2}{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}$$

Never zero, so for fixed \(x\) the left side is strictly monotone in \(y\) and can cross zero at most once. Overkill here, but it is the only route that works on Family 4, where no formula for \(y\) exists.

Part b  ·  \(y^{2}+x^{2}=10\)

Route A: solve for \(y\)

$$y^{2}=10-x^{2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^{2}}$$

That \(\pm\) is the whole answer. Because you had to take an even root to free \(y\), both signs are genuine solutions whenever \(10-x^{2}>0\).

Route B: the counterexample, which is what you should actually write

$$x=1:\qquad y^{2}+1^{2}=10\;\Longrightarrow\;y^{2}=9\;\Longrightarrow\;y=3\ \text{ and }\ y=-3$$

Check both in the original: \(3^{2}+1^{2}=10\) and \((-3)^{2}+1^{2}=10\). Two different \(y\) for the same \(x\), so it is not a function. Done, in one line.

\(x=1\) is the right choice because \(10-1=9\) is a perfect square. \(x=3\) works just as well, giving \(y=\pm1\). Avoid \(x=0\), which gives the correct but ugly \(y=\pm\sqrt{10}\).

Route C: the discriminant

Read \(y^{2}+\left(x^{2}-10\right)=0\) as a quadratic in \(y\) with \(a=1\), \(b=0\), \(c=x^{2}-10\), so \(\Delta=b^{2}-4ac=40-4x^{2}\).

Route D: the graph

The circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\approx3.1623\) about the origin. The vertical line \(x=1\) enters at \((1,3)\) and leaves at \((1,-3)\), so the vertical line test fails immediately. The only two places a vertical line touches once are \(x=\pm\sqrt{10}\), where it is tangent and \(y=0\), and those two exceptions rescue nothing: the definition demands every \(x\) give one \(y\), and the interior \(x\) all give two.

The standard follow-up, worth having ready

"Restrict it so that it does become a function." Take the upper half: \(y=\sqrt{10-x^{2}}\) with domain \(\left[-\sqrt{10},\sqrt{10}\right]\) and range \(\left[0,\sqrt{10}\right]\). The lower half \(y=-\sqrt{10-x^{2}}\) is equally valid. Brackets are closed at \(\pm\sqrt{10}\), because \(\sqrt{0}=0\) exists.

SECTION 05

The exam-ready write-up

If the paper you sit asks for method, these are the sentences that earn the marks. Part a needs four sentences and part b needs one.

  1. a. Multiplying both sides by \(x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert\) gives \(2y=x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert\), so \(y=\dfrac{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\).
  2. a. No even root or absolute value was applied to \(y\), so no sign choice arises and each admissible \(x\) determines exactly one \(y\).
  3. a. The original equation has \(x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert=\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)\) in a denominator, which vanishes at \(x=0\) and \(x=\pm3\), so those three values are excluded.
  4. a. Therefore the equation does define \(y\) as a function of \(x\), with domain \((-\infty,-3)\cup(-3,0)\cup(0,3)\cup(3,\infty)\).
  5. b. At \(x=1\) the equation becomes \(y^{2}=9\), which is satisfied by both \(y=3\) and \(y=-3\), so a single \(x\) has two outputs and the equation does not define \(y\) as a function of \(x\). (It is the circle of radius \(\sqrt{10}\) about the origin, which fails the vertical line test.)

The single most expensive typo on this question

x^2-3*abs(x)/2 parses as \(x^{2}-\dfrac{3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\), because division binds tighter than subtraction. At \(x=4\) that gives \(16-6=10\) where the true value is \(2\); at \(x=5\) it gives \(25-7.5=17.5\) where the true value is \(5\). Nothing about either output looks like a typo, which is why it costs the whole mark. Bracket the entire numerator before you divide, every time.

The second most expensive: a three-interval domain

(-infinity,0)U(0,3)U(3,infinity) is well-formed, parseable, and confidently wrong. It is what you get by factoring \(x^{2}-3x\) instead of \(x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert\). Count the pieces against "three removed points, four intervals" before you submit.

Always click the preview icon. An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics. For part a these are all accepted as equivalent, so pick the one you can type without a slip: (x^2-3*abs(x))/2, (abs(x)^2-3*abs(x))/2, abs(x)*(abs(x)-3)/2.

SECTION 09

The trap list

Ranked by how often they actually cost marks.

  1. Factoring \(x^{2}-3x\) instead of \(x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert\), and missing \(x=-3\). The number one error and the reason the absolute value is in the question at all. Use \(x^{2}=\lvert x\rvert^{2}\) and factor \(\lvert x\rvert\left(\lvert x\rvert-3\right)\), or square \(x^{2}=3\lvert x\rvert\) and solve \(x^{2}\left(x^{2}-9\right)=0\). Then count: three points, four intervals.
  2. Reading part a's domain off the solved formula. \(y=\dfrac{x^{2}-3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\) is defined everywhere, including at \(0\) and \(\pm3\) where it returns \(0\). The exclusions live in the original equation's denominator and are erased the moment you multiply through.
  3. Concluding that part a is "not a function" because it is undefined at three points. A function is not required to be defined everywhere. Missing inputs shrink the domain; duplicate outputs break the function. Only the second disqualifies.
  4. Writing a general formula for part b instead of a counterexample. \(y=\pm\sqrt{10-x^{2}}\) is correct but is not yet an explanation. The mark is for naming a specific \(x\) with two specific \(y\).
  5. Assuming any square root means \(\pm\). \(\sqrt{y}=x\) is a function; \(y^{2}=x\) is not. The \(\pm\) appears only when you apply the even root to free \(y\), never when the root already sits on \(y\).
  6. Assuming any odd power is safe. \(y^{3}=x\) passes, but \(y^{3}-3y=x\) fails, because the second doubles back. Check monotonicity, not just the leading power.
  7. Missing a hidden quadratic. \(y^{2}+y=x\) has no visible \(\pm\), and its two roots at \(x=6\) are \(2\) and \(-3\), not a matched pair. Anything quadratic in \(y\) fails.
  8. Giving a three-interval domain. Three removed points must produce four intervals. (-infinity,0)U(0,3)U(3,infinity) is exactly what trap 1 produces, and it parses perfectly.
  9. Not bracketing the numerator on entry. x^2-3*abs(x)/2 parses as \(x^{2}-\dfrac{3\lvert x\rvert}{2}\) and gives \(10\) at \(x=4\) where the answer is \(2\).
  10. Treating "every \(y\) works" as harmless. In \(xy=0\) and \(x^{2}y=x\), the point \(x=0\) admits every \(y\), which is a failure, not an exclusion. Distinguish \(0=0\) from \(5=0\) at any collapse point.
  11. Cancelling a factor before checking the collapse point. \(x^{2}y=x\) becomes \(y=\dfrac1x\), and the cancellation converts a fatal "every \(y\)" into a benign "undefined".
  12. Forgetting that repeated outputs are legal. \(\{(1,7),(2,7),(3,7)\}\) is a function. Only repeated inputs with different outputs break it.
  13. Answering part b's follow-up with the wrong brackets. The restricted semicircle \(y=\sqrt{10-x^{2}}\) has domain \(\left[-\sqrt{10},\sqrt{10}\right]\), closed at both ends, because \(\sqrt{0}\) exists.

How to score yourself

Score 1: the verdict

If verdicts are right and evidence is thin, you know the concept and are losing the explanation marks. The fix is Section 5.

If the verdicts themselves are wrong, you are pattern-matching on surface features. The fix is the triage table in 2.2, read as "where does \(y\) sit" rather than "what does the equation look like".

Score 2: the evidence

A "yes" needs the solved formula plus the sentence about no choice appearing. A "no" needs one \(x\) and two \(y\), both checked in the original.

Score 3: the domains, on 1, 5, 6, 9 and 11

A right verdict with a wrong domain is a specific, fixable error, and it comes from the trigger table in 2.4, not from anything about functions. On question 1 in particular, check that you produced four intervals and not three.
