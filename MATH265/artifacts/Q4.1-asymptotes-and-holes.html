MATH 265 · Midterm 2 · Question 6 · 10 points

Asymptote or hole

The denominator \(x^{2}-16\) has two roots. One of them is a vertical asymptote and the other is not. Telling them apart is the whole question, and it is worth ten points because the fast answer gets it wrong.

\(f(x)=\dfrac{x^{2}-3x-4}{x^{2}-16}\)  ·  the three things to report
Vertical blows up $$x = -4 \ \text{ only}$$

The reduced denominator \(x+4\) vanishes there and the numerator does not: it equals \(-3\). So the quotient is \(-3/0\) and the graph runs off the page.

The two sides disagree $$\lim_{x\to-4^{-}}f=+\infty,\qquad \lim_{x\to-4^{+}}f=-\infty$$ Type into Möbius x=-4

Check: f(-4.1) = 31 and f(-3.9) = -29.

Horizontal settles $$y = 1 \ \text{ in both directions}$$

Equal degrees, leading coefficients \(1\) and \(1\). The rule reads the leading terms only, so the \(-3x-4\) and the \(-16\) play no part in it.

And it is never reached $$f(x)=1-\frac{3}{x+4}\ne 1 \ \text{ for any } x$$ Type into Möbius y=1

Check: f(100) = 101/104 and f(-100) = 99/96.

At \(x=4\) stays finite $$\text{a hole at } \left(4,\ \tfrac58\right)$$

The factor \((x-4)\) cancels top and bottom, so the limit there is finite. A finite limit is never an asymptote. This is the observation the question was built around.

The cancellation $$\frac{(x-4)(x+1)}{(x-4)(x+4)}=\frac{x+1}{x+4}$$ Type into Möbius (4,5/8)

Check: f(3.999) = 0.6249, which is not large.

The one sentence that decides this question

A zero of the denominator is a vertical asymptote only if it survives cancellation. Factor both halves first, cancel what cancels, and only then read the asymptotes off what is left. Whatever you cancelled is a hole.

SECTION 00

The 30-second version

The examiner wrote a numerator that shares a factor with the denominator. That is the entire question. Everything else is the two rules you already know.

Find the vertical and horizontal asymptotes of f(x) = (x^2-3x-4)/(x^2-16).

STEP 1  FACTOR BOTH.  Never solve "denominator = 0" before you factor.

        x = 4 CANCELLED  ->  a HOLE at (4, 5/8),  NOT an asymptote

        y = 1       and it is the same limit at +inf and at -inf

CHECK   f(-4.1) = +31  and  f(-3.9) = -29      blows up, opposite signs

        f(100) = 101/104   and  f(-100) = 99/96     settling on 1 from both sides

If you factored, you got it. If you set \(x^{2}-16=0\) and wrote "\(x=4\) and \(x=-4\)", you got half of it, and the half you lost is the half the examiner was aiming at.

Why the number 10 matters

On a paper where the transformation question is 6 points and the tangent-line question is 4, an asymptote question at 10 is not being asked casually. The neighbouring six-part limit question is 24, so each limit is worth 4. This question is worth two and a half limits, which is the examiner telling you there are more than two things to say.

There are, and they are: the horizontal asymptote, the vertical asymptote, the hole, and the reason the second candidate is not an asymptote.

Format warning

The sample paper prints "(10 points)" and reads like a written exam. The live delivery is Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, where the ten points typically split across several boxes: one for the vertical asymptote, one for the horizontal, often one for the hole or the domain, sometimes one for the one-sided limits. A student who writes only "\(x=-4\), \(y=1\)" has the right mathematics and may still be leaving boxes empty.

Note also that Midterm 2 prints each point value on the line above its question, the opposite convention to Midterm 1. Read the boundary rather than assuming the marker trails.

SECTION 02

The five things you must have cold

2.1  Both asymptotes are limit statements, and nothing else

Everything in this topic is a definition about limits wearing geometric clothes.

KindThe definitionWhat you compute
vertical at \(x=a\)\(\lim_{x\to a^{-}}f=\pm\infty\) or \(\lim_{x\to a^{+}}f=\pm\infty\)a one-sided limit at a finite point
horizontal \(y=L\)\(\lim_{x\to+\infty}f=L\) or \(\lim_{x\to-\infty}f=L\)a limit at infinity
slant \(y=mx+b\)\(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\bigl[f(x)-(mx+b)\bigr]=0\)the same limit, after subtracting a line
hole at \(x=a\)\(\lim_{x\to a}f\) exists and is finite, but \(f(a)\) is undefineda two-sided finite limit

Three consequences of reading the definitions literally, which is where the marks are.

One side is enough for a vertical asymptote

The word "or" is in the definition. \(\ln x\) has a vertical asymptote at \(x=0\) even though it is not defined at all on the left.

Both ends are separate questions for a horizontal one

A function can have one, or two different ones, or none. \(\arctan x\) has two. Rational functions always agree at both ends, which is a convenience specific to them.

A horizontal asymptote is not a fence

It is an end-behaviour statement. The graph is allowed to cross it, and many graphs do. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers, and that is because the function is not defined there.

2.2  The degree rule, all four cases

Let \(f=\dfrac{N}{D}\) with \(n=\deg N\) and \(d=\deg D\), after cancelling.

CaseHorizontalSlantWhy
n < d\(y=0\)nonethe bottom outgrows the top
n = d\(y=\dfrac{\text{lead }N}{\text{lead }D}\)nonethe two growths match, so the ratio settles
n = d + 1none\(y=mx+b\), by long divisionthe quotient is a line
n > d + 1nonenoneit runs off faster than any line

The rule reads leading terms only. For this question that means comparing \(x^{2}\) with \(x^{2}\), giving \(1/1=1\); the \(-3x-4\) and the \(-16\) have nothing to do with it. Here is the algebra it is short for, worth doing once so that you trust it. Divide top and bottom by the highest power in the denominator.

$$\frac{x^{2}-3x-4}{x^{2}-16}=\frac{1-\dfrac{3}{x}-\dfrac{4}{x^{2}}}{1-\dfrac{16}{x^{2}}} \xrightarrow[\ x\to\pm\infty\ ]{}\frac{1-0-0}{1-0}=1$$

Every term with an \(x\) underneath dies, and the sign of \(x\) never enters, which is exactly why a rational function has the same horizontal asymptote at both ends.

2.3  Factoring the two quadratics in five seconds each

You cannot do this question without factoring, and factoring is where the clock goes. For \(x^{2}-3x-4\), find two numbers with sum \(+3\) and product \(-4\): opposite signs, because the product is negative, so \(4\) and \(-1\), giving \((x-4)(x+1)\). For \(x^{2}-16\), a square minus a square, so \((x-4)(x+4)\).

PatternFactoredRecognise it by
\(x^{2}-16\)\((x-4)(x+4)\)a square minus a square, no middle term
\(x^{2}-3x-4\)\((x-4)(x+1)\)sum \(3\), product \(-4\)
\(x^{2}+3x-4\)\((x+4)(x-1)\)the same numbers, signs swapped
\(x^{2}-5x+4\)\((x-4)(x-1)\)product positive, sum positive, both roots positive
\(x^{2}+16\)does not factor over the realsa sum of squares has no real root

That last row is a real exam trap in its own right. \(x^{2}+16\) is never zero, so a function with it in the denominator has no vertical asymptote at all.

The five-second sanity check on any factoring

Multiply the two constants and compare with the constant term you started with: \((-4)(+1)=-4\), which matches. Then add them: \(-4+1=-3\), which matches the middle coefficient.

2.4  Hole against vertical asymptote: it is a multiplicity comparison

Write both halves factored and look at one root at a time. Let \((x-a)\) appear to power \(p\) upstairs and power \(q\) downstairs.

The row that catches people

"A factor cancelled, so it is a hole" is wrong when the denominator had more copies than the numerator. In \(\dfrac{x-1}{(x-1)^{2}}\) a factor does cancel and \(x=1\) is still an asymptote, because one copy is left downstairs.

The height of a hole is whatever the reduced function gives on substitution. Here the reduced function is \(\dfrac{x+1}{x+4}\), and at \(x=4\) that is \(\dfrac58\). This is exactly a \(0/0\) limit computed by cancelling, which is Q3.1a and Q3.2a all over again. The topic heading changed; the mathematics did not.

2.5  The sign test at a vertical asymptote

Once you know \(x=a\) is an asymptote, saying which infinity is separate work, and on this paper it is often a separate box. Substitute \(a\) into the numerator and keep its sign, then ask what sign the denominator takes on each side, and divide.

sidenumerator \(x+1\)denominator \(x+4\)quotientlimit
x = -4.1-3.1, negative-0.1, negativepositive\(+\infty\)
x = -3.9-2.9, negative+0.1, positivenegative\(-\infty\)

\(f(-4.1)=31\) exactly and \(f(-3.9)=-29\) exactly, which is a check you can do in your head.

The parity shortcut

If the surviving factor has odd multiplicity, the two sides have opposite signs. If it has even multiplicity, they have the same sign. Here the multiplicity is \(1\), so the signs are opposite, so \(\lim_{x\to-4}f(x)\) does not exist and writing "\(=+\infty\)" is wrong. Contrast \(\dfrac{1}{(x-2)^{2}}\), where the multiplicity is \(2\) and the limit genuinely is \(+\infty\) on both sides.

SECTION 03

The universal procedure

This works on every version of this question in Section 08.

  1. Factor the numerator and the denominator completely. Before anything else. Every error in this topic that is not an arithmetic slip comes from skipping this step.
  2. Cancel every common factor, and write down what you cancelled. The cancelled roots are your candidate holes and you will need them again in step 5.
  3. Vertical asymptotes: set the REDUCED denominator to zero. Each real root is an asymptote. Confirm the reduced numerator is nonzero there.
  4. Horizontal: compare the degrees of the REDUCED fraction. Use the four-case table in 2.2. If the gap is exactly one, long-divide and report a slant instead.
  5. Holes: substitute each cancelled root into the reduced function. That value is the height. Compare multiplicities first, per 2.4, because a partial cancellation is still an asymptote.
  6. Sign test at each vertical asymptote, if the question or the box asks for behaviour. Odd multiplicity means the sides disagree and the limit does not exist.
  7. State the domain if asked. Every root of the ORIGINAL denominator is excluded, holes included.
Step 7 is the one people get backwards

Cancelling changes the formula, not the function. \(x=4\) is excluded from the domain of \(f\) even though \(\dfrac{x+1}{x+4}\) is perfectly happy there.

Horizontal. Reduced degrees are \(1\) over \(1\), leading coefficients \(1\) and \(1\), so \(y=1\). Comparing the original degrees, \(2\) over \(2\), gives the same answer, because cancelling a common factor cannot change the leading ratio.

Route B  ·  solve "denominator = 0" without factoring

$$x^{2}-16=0 \implies x=4 \ \text{ or } \ x=-4$$

"Therefore there are vertical asymptotes at \(x=4\) and \(x=-4\)."

This is the answer the question was built to catch

It is fast, it looks finished, and it gets the horizontal asymptote right, so half the answer is correct and nothing warns you about the other half. The warning you should have generated yourself is one substitution: put \(x=4\) into the numerator and get \(16-12-4=0\). A vertical asymptote needs a nonzero numerator. When both vanish you have \(0/0\), which is the signature of a cancellation, and \(0/0\) is a limit to be computed, not an asymptote to be declared.

Route B is not useless. It is Route A with step 1 skipped, and it is exactly right on every function whose numerator shares no root, which is most of them. The fix is that one substitution.

Route C  ·  straight from the limit definitions

If you distrust the shortcuts, or the question says "using limits", compute the four limits.

$$\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{x^{2}-3x-4}{x^{2}-16} =\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{1-\frac{3}{x}-\frac{4}{x^{2}}}{1-\frac{16}{x^{2}}}=\frac{1-0-0}{1-0}=1$$ $$\lim_{x\to4}\frac{(x-4)(x+1)}{(x-4)(x+4)}=\lim_{x\to4}\frac{x+1}{x+4}=\frac58 \quad\text{finite, so a hole and not an asymptote}$$ $$\lim_{x\to-4^{-}}\frac{x+1}{x+4}=\frac{-3}{0^{-}}=+\infty,\qquad \lim_{x\to-4^{+}}\frac{x+1}{x+4}=\frac{-3}{0^{+}}=-\infty$$

This route produces the most complete answer, because the one-sided limits come out of it for free. It is also the route the marker wants if the wording is "justify".

$$\frac{x+1}{x+4}=\frac{(x+4)-3}{x+4}=1-\frac{3}{x+4}$$

"Justify" or "using limits" means Route C. "And sketch" means Route D. Route B is never a method on its own; it is Route A with its first step missing.

SECTION 05

The exam-ready write-up

If the paper you sit asks for method, these are the sentences that earn the marks. Ten points wants roughly this much.

Model answer

Factoring gives \(f(x)=\dfrac{(x-4)(x+1)}{(x-4)(x+4)}\), so for \(x\neq4\) we have \(f(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{x+4}\).

Vertical. The reduced denominator vanishes only at \(x=-4\), where the numerator equals \(-3\neq0\). Hence \(\lim_{x\to-4^{-}}f=+\infty\) and \(\lim_{x\to-4^{+}}f=-\infty\), and \(x=-4\) is the only vertical asymptote. The two-sided limit at \(-4\) therefore does not exist.

The other root of the denominator is not an asymptote. The factor \((x-4)\) cancels, and \(\lim_{x\to4}f(x)=\dfrac58\), which is finite. So \(f\) has a removable discontinuity (a hole) at \(\left(4,\tfrac58\right)\), not a vertical asymptote, although \(x=4\) is still excluded from the domain.

Horizontal. The numerator and denominator have equal degree with leading coefficients \(1\) and \(1\), so \(\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f(x)=1\) and \(y=1\) is a horizontal asymptote in both directions.

Four things a marker looks for that students routinely skip

Saying why x = -4 survives

By checking the numerator is nonzero there. Without that sentence you have asserted an asymptote rather than shown one.

Naming x = 4 and disposing of it

An answer that lists only \(x=-4\) reads like a student who never noticed the second root. Say it: the factor cancels, the limit is finite, it is a hole.

Separating "not an asymptote" from "in the domain"

\(x=4\) is not in the domain of \(f\). Cancelling produced a different function that happens to agree with \(f\) everywhere else.

Giving both one-sided limits

This is the difference between "\(\lim=\infty\)", which is wrong, and "the limit does not exist; \(+\infty\) from the left and \(-\infty\) from the right", which is right.

The single most expensive answer on this question

x=4, x=-4. It is well formed, it parses, it is the answer to a question the examiner deliberately did not ask, and it is what Route B produces. Before you submit any vertical asymptote, substitute its \(x\) into the numerator and confirm you do not get zero.

The second most expensive

x+1/x+4. Without brackets that parses as \(x+\dfrac1x+4\), which at \(x=4\) gives \(8.25\) instead of \(\dfrac58\). Bracket the whole numerator and the whole denominator, every time.

Do not overcorrect

Two vertical asymptotes is a legitimate answer to other versions. \(\dfrac{2x^{2}+5}{x^{2}-9}\) genuinely has asymptotes at \(x=3\) and \(x=-3\), because \(2x^{2}+5\) is never zero. The rule is "check the numerator", not "expect a hole".

And the domain still excludes the hole. If a box asks for the domain, write three intervals, not two. The point people drop is \(4\), exactly the one they just decided was "only a hole".

SECTION 09

The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

  1. Solving \(x^{2}-16=0\) and declaring both roots to be vertical asymptotes. This is the error the question was built to catch. Factor the numerator too, or at minimum substitute each root of the denominator into the numerator and confirm you do not get zero. At \(x=4\) the numerator is \(16-12-4=0\), which is the whole story.
  2. Calling \(x=4\) a hole and then also putting it back in the domain. Cancelling changes the formula, not the function. The domain is \((-\infty,-4)\cup(-4,4)\cup(4,\infty)\), three intervals, not two.
  3. Writing \(\lim_{x\to-4}f(x)=+\infty\). The two sides disagree in sign, so no single infinite value is correct. The limit does not exist; quote \(+\infty\) from the left and \(-\infty\) from the right.
  4. Assuming that because something cancelled, there is no asymptote there. In \(\dfrac{x-1}{(x-1)^{2}}\) a factor cancels and \(x=1\) is still an asymptote. Compare multiplicities; do not just look for a cancellation.
  5. Using the constant terms in the degree rule. The rule reads leading terms only. Here that is \(x^{2}\) against \(x^{2}\), so \(y=1\); the \(-3x-4\) and the \(-16\) are irrelevant to it.
  6. Reporting "no asymptote" when the degrees differ by one. There is one, it is slanted, and you get it from long division. \(\dfrac{x^{2}-3x-4}{x+4}\) is this question one edit away from it.
  7. Assuming both ends give the same horizontal asymptote. They do for every rational function, and they do not once a square root, an absolute value or an exponential appears. \(\dfrac{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}{x}\) has two.
  8. Thinking a graph cannot cross a horizontal asymptote. It often does. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers.
  9. Not doing the sign test at all. Once you have \(-3/0\) the sign work is the remaining question, and on a 10-point item it is usually a separate box.
  10. Getting the factoring signs backwards. \(x^{2}-3x-4=(x-4)(x+1)\), not \((x+4)(x-1)\), which is \(x^{2}+3x-4\). Multiply back and check both the constant and the middle coefficient.
  11. Treating a sum of squares as factorable. \(x^{2}+16\) has no real root, so \(\dfrac{x^{2}-3x-4}{x^{2}+16}\) has no vertical asymptote at all.
  12. Treating a jump as an asymptote. \(\dfrac{x^{2}-4}{\lvert x-2\rvert}\) has finite one-sided limits \(-4\) and \(4\) at \(x=2\). Nothing blows up, so nothing is an asymptote.
  13. Forgetting the hole entirely. On a 10-point question that asks only for "asymptotes", the hole is still the discriminating observation, and the marker is looking for the sentence that disposes of \(x=4\).

Mark yourself on three separate scores

Score 1 · the vertical asymptotes

Wrong here means you are skipping the factoring step. The fix is Section 03 step 1 and the substitution test in trap 1. Questions 1, 3, 7, 8 and 10 are the discriminating ones, and 7 catches people who overcorrected after learning about holes.

Score 2 · the horizontal or slant

Wrong here means you are misapplying the degree rule. The fix is the four-case table in 2.2, and in particular noticing that 4 and 8 have a degree gap of one. On 8 you must cancel first, or you divide the wrong pair.

Score 3 · the holes

A missing hole is not the same error as a wrong asymptote. It means you found the cancellation and then forgot to report it. The fix is Section 03 step 2: write down what you cancelled as you cancel it, and revisit the list before you submit.

Reading the three scores together

Good on 1 and 2 but bad on 3, and you know the mathematics and are losing presentation marks. Bad on 1, and you are still answering Route B's question rather than the examiner's.
