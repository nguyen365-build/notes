MATH 265 · Midterm 1 · Item 10 · 5 points

Four Conditions, One Sketch

Four sentences in limit notation, four features on a picture. This question has no unique answer and no algebra at all - it checks whether you can read notation and draw what it means.

The one sentence that decides it

The four conditions live at three different places on the \(x\)-axis, so they cannot fight each other. Draw them one at a time, at \(x=-2\), at \(x=0\), and far to the right. Then join up whatever is left over however you like.

QThe question

Sketch the graph of a single function \(f(x)\) that satisfies all of the conditions listed below.

Condition 1
\(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)\) does not exist
→ break the graph at 0
Condition 2
\(f(0) = 0\)
→ a solid dot at the origin
Condition 3
\(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = -1\)
→ horizontal asymptote, right end
Condition 4
\(f\) is not differentiable at \(x=-2\)
→ a corner

Conditions 1 and 2 are the only two that land on the same \(x\), and they are the whole difficulty. One says the graph must break at zero. The other says the graph must have a value there. Both are true at once, and the picture that says so is one vertical line carrying three marks.

01The answer, drawn

Any function with these four features earns full marks. This one is chosen because every feature is visible and every number is round.

$$f(x)=\begin{cases} \lvert x+2 \rvert + 1, & x < 0\\[3pt] 0, & x = 0\\[3pt] -1 + \dfrac{3}{x+1}, & x > 0 \end{cases}$$

Read the vertical line at \(x=0\): three marks, top to bottom, open at 3, open at 2, solid at 0. That single column answers two of the four conditions, and it is the part a marker looks at first.

04The limit and the value are independent

This is the idea the first two conditions are built to test, and the one students most often do not believe. \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\) is about what happens near \(a\). \(f(a)\) is about what happens at \(a\). The definition of a limit deliberately excludes the point itself, so the two can be anything relative to each other.

The most common wrong answer

A graph drawn smoothly through the origin. It satisfies \(f(0)=0\) perfectly and destroys condition one, because a curve you can draw through \((0,0)\) without lifting the pen has \(\lim_{x\to0}f = 0\), which exists. Read the height just left of 0 and just right of 0. If they match, start again.

05The three ways a limit fails, and the four ways differentiability fails

“Does not exist” is not one thing, and neither is “not differentiable”. Knowing every mechanism is what lets you pick the cheapest one to draw.

A false claim in the course notes, recorded not smoothed over

The course notes say “since \(f(0)=0\) is defined, a vertical asymptote is ruled out”. That is wrong. Defining a value places one dot; it says nothing about behaviour near the point, which is all a limit looks at. \(f(x) = -\lvert x+2\rvert/x\) with \(f(0)=0\) has a pole at zero, has \(f(0)=0\) defined, and satisfies all four conditions. It was verified numerically. The notes' advice to use a jump is still right, for a different reason: a jump is easier to draw, not more legal.

“Not differentiable” carries no information about continuity. Three of the four are continuous, so answering “no” to “is \(f\) continuous at \(-2\)?” is a straight loss. A corner has two different finite slopes; a cusp has two infinite ones. The course review file blurs those two into one phrase “sharp corners (cusps)”; a later passage in the same file separates them correctly. Use the four-way list.

06The universal procedure

  1. Write the \(x\)-value beside every condition. Anything at \(\pm\infty\) goes in a separate tail pile.
  2. Sort them, and check whether any two sit at the same place. Conditions at different places are independent. Conditions at the same place must be reconciled first, and that is where the difficulty always is.
  3. Translate each condition into a feature using the dictionary. Write “corner at \(-2\)”, not “not differentiable at \(-2\)”.
  4. Check for a contradiction before drawing. If two conditions demand incompatible things, the answer is “no such function exists” plus the reason.
  5. Draw the frame only: axes, dashed asymptotes, a tick at every constrained \(x\). No curves yet.
  6. Place the dots. Solid where a value exists, open where a limit is approached but not attained.
  7. Join up with the simplest curve that hits everything. Straight lines and V shapes are allowed and usually best.
  8. Label, then re-read the conditions one at a time against the picture.

Step 2 is where the marks are. In Q5.1 the constrained places are \(-2\), \(0\) and the right tail, and only \(x=0\) carries two conditions.

A trap in the checking itself

If you test differentiability with a centred difference \(\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}\), it reports that \(\lvert x+2\rvert\) is differentiable at \(-2\) with derivative \(0\). It is exactly zero there, for every \(h\), because the two arms rise by the same amount on both sides and cancel. A symmetric difference is blind to corners. Differentiability has to be tested one side at a time. The automated harness behind this page made exactly this mistake and had to be rewritten.

10The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

  1. Drawing a smooth curve through the origin. The error the question was built to catch. \(f(0)=0\) says the point is on the graph; it does not say the graph is unbroken there.
  2. Omitting the open circles. Without them your picture assigns three values to \(x=0\) and is not the graph of a function at all.
  3. Answering “no” to “is \(f\) continuous at \(-2\)?”. A corner is continuous. “Not differentiable” says nothing about continuity.
  4. Calling a cusp a corner, or the reverse. A corner has two different finite one-sided slopes; a cusp has two infinite ones.
  5. Forcing the left tail onto \(y=-1\) too. Only \(x\to+\infty\) was constrained. Extra work here earns nothing and adds risk.
  6. Writing \(\lim = \infty\) when the two sides disagree in sign. Then the limit does not exist, full stop.
  7. Putting the corner at \(x=2\). \(\lvert x+2\rvert\) has its corner at \(-2\). The same inside-the-bars sign trap as Q2.2.
  8. Drawing a pole at \(0\) and then leaving \(f(0)\) undefined. The blow-up route is legal, but the solid dot still has to be there.
  9. Reaching for a single smooth formula. Nothing requires the answer to be one formula, continuous, or smooth.
  10. Believing the graph cannot touch or cross \(y=-1\). It may do both. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers.
  11. Leaving it unlabelled. On a five-point picture the marker is looking for four features.
  12. Checking differentiability with a centred difference. It is exactly \(0\) at a corner and will tell you the function is differentiable there.
  13. Failing the vertical line test. A stray solid dot left over from a first attempt turns a correct answer into a non-function.
