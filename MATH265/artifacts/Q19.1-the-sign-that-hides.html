The Sign That Hides

Find the area between $y = x$ and $y = 2 - x^2$. The integration is two lines of a polynomial. The mark is in the setup, and one particular mistake here keeps the right number.

THE ANSWER

$\dfrac{9}{2} = 4.5000$

TYPE INTO MÖBIUS

9/2

VERIFIED

5 routes agree to 2.1e-14

The whole method, in four lines

1. SET THE CURVES EQUAL      x = 2 - x^2  ->  x^2 + x - 2 = 0  ->  x = -2, x = 1
2. PICK A POINT BETWEEN      at x = -1/2: parabola 1.7500, line -0.5000 -> parabola on top
3. INTEGRATE (TOP - BOTTOM)  A = int from -2 to 1 of ( (2 - x^2) - x ) dx
4. EVALUATE                  [ 2x - x^2/2 - x^3/3 ] from -2 to 1 = 7/6 - (-10/3) = 9/2

Step 2 is the one everybody skips, and it is the only step that decides the sign.

The region

FIGURE 1 · THE PARABOLA IS THE UPPER CURVE ACROSS THE WHOLE SPAN, SO ONE INTEGRAL DOES IT

The two curves meet at $(-2,-2)$ and $(1,1)$, and nowhere else. Between them the parabola is above the line at every point, so a vertical strip has height $(2 - x^2) - x$ and the region needs no split. The dashed parallelogram stands on the chord joining the two intersections, and its height is the largest vertical gap $2.2500$; the parabola is tangent to its top side at $x = -1/2$. A parabolic segment always fills exactly two-thirds of that parallelogram, which is where the twenty-second magnitude check comes from. It has to be the sheared shape, not an axis-aligned rectangle: a rectangle of the same width and height would not contain the region at all.

The procedure, and it is the foundation for eight more questions

Q19.1 opens the applications-of-integration block, which is the largest category in the bank at nine questions. Q19.2 is the same shape with a cosine; Q19.3, Q19.8 and Q19.9 integrate a rate; Q19.4 and Q19.5 build the integrand from a physical description; Q19.6 and Q19.7 divide by the interval length. The six steps below are what all nine share.

  1. Sketch, roughly. Not for marks. You are counting how many pieces the region has.
  2. Find the intersections exactly. Set the expressions equal and solve algebraically. Never read them off the sketch.
  3. Decide which curve is on top by evaluating both at one point strictly between consecutive intersections. Write the comparison down.
  4. Write the definite integral before evaluating it. $A = \int_a^b (\text{top} - \text{bottom})\,dx$.
  5. Antidifferentiate and evaluate, upper limit minus lower limit.
  6. Check the sign and the magnitude. A negative answer means step 3 went the wrong way.

Why it is top minus bottom, and when the absolute value actually matters

A vertical strip has height $\text{top}(x) - \text{bottom}(x)$. A height is a length, so it has to come out positive, and that is the entire reason the order matters. Textbooks write the formula as $A = \int_a^b \lvert f - g\rvert\,dx$, which is correct and unusable, because nothing antidifferentiates an absolute value. What it is really telling you is: find where $f - g$ changes sign, and split there.

On this question there is nothing to split. The difference $D(x) = -(x+2)(x-1)$ has its only two zeros at the interval's own endpoints, and it was checked to be strictly positive at 20,000 interior points. So the absolute value is invisible here, on the very question that introduces it. The split test is one question: does anything cross strictly inside the interval?

Q19.2 is where it fires. Its curves cross at $\pm\pi/4$, both strictly inside the given $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. Integrating straight through gives -0.2214414691, which is negative and therefore not an area at all. Splitting at both crossings gives 0.8284271247, exactly $2\sqrt2 - 2$. Learn the rule here, where it is quiet, so it is a reflex there.

The same idea returns in physics clothing on Q19.8: displacement is $\int v$, distance travelled is $\int \lvert v\rvert$. That is this paragraph again, with velocity in place of a gap.

Route A, checked with route C. The two-thirds law says the area of a parabolic segment is $\tfrac23 \times \text{span} \times \text{largest gap}$, here $\tfrac23 \times 3 \times 2.2500$, the parallelogram in figure 1. It costs twenty seconds and it catches a wrong interval, a wrong orientation and a dropped term at once, because all three move the answer away from two-thirds of the box.

Route B has a trap that is invisible on this question. The leading coefficient of the difference is $-1$, so $\lvert a\rvert = 1$ and a student who forgets the factor entirely still gets $9/2$. On $y = x$ against $y = 4 - 2x^2$ the same omission is wrong by a factor of exactly two.

Route D was worked in full anyway, because the mirror-image question exists: $x = y^2$ against $x = y+2$ is one integral in $y$ and two in $x$. The rule is not about taste. Count the pieces each variable needs, and take the smaller count. Here that is one to two in favour of $x$; there it is one to two in favour of $y$.

Three different mistakes all land on exactly $-9/2$: reversing the orientation, dropping the absolute value in the shortcut, and evaluating $F(a) - F(b)$ instead of $F(b) - F(a)$. Every one of them becomes the correct $4.5$ the instant the student writes "area is positive" and drops the minus. A sign error on this question is undetectable by any arithmetic check. The only defence is the one-line orientation statement in step 3, which fixes the order before the integration rather than after it.

A fourth candidate matches even in sign: splitting at $x = 0$ for no reason and adding the pieces gives $9/2$ exactly, because there is no sign change at $0$ to get wrong. An unnecessary split is harmless; a missing necessary one is not.

Of the 14 caught mistakes the closest miss is off by 0.7287638337, which is 16 percent of the answer. So the partition is clean: anything that really changes the region is loud, and every quiet mistake is a sign. That tells you exactly where to spend your checking time.

The traps, ranked by what they actually cost

  1. Reading the intersections off the sketch. Not because a nearly-right endpoint is costly. It is nearly free: integrating from $-1.97$ instead of $-2$ costs 0.0298 percent, because $dA/da = -D(a)$ and $D$ vanishes at a true intersection, so the area is stationary there. The habit is what fails, and it fails loudly the moment the intersections are not integers.
  2. Getting the orientation backwards and then hiding it. Three versions of this all give $-9/2$, and all three become $4.5$ when the minus is dropped as obvious. Decide the orientation by evaluating, in writing, first.
  3. Using the parabola's own roots as the interval. $2 - x^2 = 0$ gives $\pm\sqrt2$, which is where the parabola meets the axis, not the line. That is 0.7287638337 too much.
  4. Not splitting when the curves cross inside a given interval. Latent here, fatal on Q19.2.
  5. Sign errors at the negative limit. $F(-2) = -4 - 2 + \tfrac83 = -\tfrac{10}{3}$. The $-x^3/3$ term turns positive at a negative $x$, and that is where most arithmetic marks go on this question.
  6. Dropping the $\lvert a\rvert$ in the shortcut. Invisible here, a factor of two elsewhere.
  7. Reporting a negative number. Area is a magnitude. If the arithmetic gives $-9/2$, the area is $9/2$ and your setup was backwards.

On paper, three lines should appear before the number: the intersection work, the orientation sentence, and the definite integral written out before it is evaluated. How every number here was checked

  • The question. Final Exam 1 item 10, 8 points, parsed from the paper file rather than typed. Final Exam 1 prints 14 values summing to 124, so this item is 6.45 percent of that paper. That block carries no "no credit" clause at all.
  • The area, five ways. Exact rational antiderivative; tanh-sinh quadrature; horizontal strips in $y$; the $\lvert a\rvert s^3/6$ shortcut; Archimedes' two-thirds law. All five agree to 2.1e-14.
  • The intersections. Proved exactly in rational arithmetic, then confirmed by a sign-change census that was itself controlled in both directions: it returns 0, 2, 3 and 4 roots on cases having those counts. It returns zero on a tangency, which is its honest failure, and whether it finds a touch at all depends on the parity of the grid. A census is not a root-finder at a tangency; the algebra is.
  • The orientation. Decided by evaluating both curves at $x = -1/2$ and confirmed at 20,000 interior points, never by looking at the figure.
  • The quadrature's precision. A digit-lock inherits the precision of the method behind it, so the order was measured rather than assumed. On this integrand the measurement is degenerate and that is worth saying: it is a quadratic, Simpson is exact on cubics, the error is 0.000e+00 at every $n$, and no convergence order can be measured on this question at all. Order was therefore measured on a curved control, $\sin(t^2)$ on $[0,1]$: 4.0030, 4.0008, 4.0002, 4.0001, 4.0006. The same routine on a cusp integrand measures 1.6663, 1.6666, 1.6667, 1.6667, 1.6667, which proves it can report something other than 4.
  • A last measured detail. Integrating $\lvert D\rvert$ straight through the kink at $x = 1$ is wrong by 4.790e-07; splitting at the kink first brings that to 1.243e-14. Even the numerics want you to split at the crossing.
