Exact value of a cosine

Question 1 of the midterm looks like a warm-up and is actually a gate. It checks three reflexes that reappear all over the paper: parity, decomposing an awkward angle into standard ones, and giving an answer in radicals instead of decimals. This is the method, every variation of it, and the eight-second check.

The question and the answer
Asked

Give the exact value of \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)\).

Exact value $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$$

Also accepted: sqrt(6)/4+sqrt(2)/4, cos(pi/12), sqrt(2+sqrt(3))/2

The whole method in four lines

If you remember one thing, remember this chain. It runs in about twenty seconds with no calculator.

cos(-pi/12)
  -> cos is EVEN, so drop the minus sign      cos(pi/12)
  -> pi/12 is 15 deg, and 15 = 45 - 30        cos(pi/4 - pi/6)
  -> cos(A-B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB        (r2/2)(r3/2) + (r2/2)(1/2)
  -> combine over 4                          (r6 + r2)/4
The one sign that ruins it

The cosine sum-and-difference formula has the opposite sign to the bracket. So \(\cos(A-B)\) has a plus in the middle. Say "cosine flips" out loud every time you write it. This single sign is the most common way to lose this question.

Hook for the sine column: it climbs \(\tfrac12,\ \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2},\ \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\) as the angle grows, and cosine is that same list read backwards. The two highlighted rows are the ones this question uses.

Parity, and quadrant signs

Even: drop the minusOdd: pull the minus out front
\(\cos(-x)=\cos x\)  ·  \(\sec(-x)=\sec x\) \(\sin(-x)=-\sin x\)  ·  \(\tan(-x)=-\tan x\)  ·  \(\csc,\cot\) too

Two even, four odd. Cosine and its reciprocal are the even pair; everything else is odd. For signs, ASTC: quadrant I All positive, II Sine only, III Tangent only, IV Cosine only. Reduce to the reference angle first, take the magnitude, then attach the sign. The reference angle is measured to the nearest part of the \(x\)-axis, never the \(y\)-axis.

The two formula families

$$\cos(A\mp B)=\cos A\cos B \pm \sin A\sin B \qquad \sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$ $$\tan(A\pm B)=\frac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B} \qquad \cos\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}} \qquad \sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}}$$

On the half-angle formulas the \(\pm\) is not decoration. You pick it from the quadrant of \(\theta/2\), the angle you are computing, and not from \(\theta\).

03

The procedure for any exact-value question

Four moves. Learn the moves rather than the answers, and the whole family becomes one question.

  1. Strip. Kill the minus sign with parity. Reduce anything past \(2\pi\) by subtracting full turns. Note the quadrant and hold that sign aside.
  2. Classify. Look at the denominator of the angle. That one number picks your tool, per the table below.
  3. Substitute. Write the formula out in full symbols first, then substitute. Never substitute into a half-remembered formula.
  4. Simplify and check. Put it over one denominator, then run the eight-second check in section 06 before you commit.
The decisive table

This is the part worth memorising. The denominator of the angle tells you the method, and picking the method in ten seconds is the actual exam skill.

For this question: step 1 gives \(\cos(\pi/12)\), quadrant I so positive. Step 2 sees a denominator of 12 and picks the difference formula. Done deciding, in under ten seconds.

04

Three routes, and which one to use

All three are legitimate and all three land on the same surd. They are not equally good under pressure.

Route A — parity first, then the difference formula

Cosine is even, so \(\cos\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{12}\). Then split the angle, checking the split before using it: \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{3\pi-2\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{12}\). Good.

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\sin\frac{\pi}{6} =\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} =\frac{\sqrt6}{4}+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$$

Route B — the same formula without using parity

Keep the minus inside and split \(-\tfrac{\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{6}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), which gives \(\tfrac{\sqrt6}{4}+\tfrac{\sqrt2}{4}\) again. That is the point: cosine does not care about the order of the subtraction, because it is even. A third valid split is \(\tfrac{\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), also verified to give 0.9659258263. Useful as a cross-check, but it is one more thing to get right.

Route C — half angle from \(\pi/6\)

\(\tfrac{\pi}{12}\) is half of \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\), and it sits in quadrant I, so take the plus:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/6)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2}} =\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}$$

Correct, and numerically identical, but it is a nested radical. It does equal the clean form, \(\tfrac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}=\tfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\), and denesting that by hand is real work you did not have to do.

Route A · use this

4 lines. One risk: the middle sign. Output is already the clean surd. This is the exam answer.

Route B · cross-check

4 lines. Two risks: the middle sign, and the direction of the split. Same clean output.

Route C · hold in reserve

5 lines plus denesting. Two risks: choosing the \(\pm\), and the nested form. This is the right tool for \(\pi/8\), not for \(\pi/12\).

The decision, stated

Half-angle is correct for \(\pi/8\) angles, where no sum of standard angles can reach the target. It is the wrong tool for \(\pi/12\) angles, where a difference of standard angles lands on it exactly. Matching tool to denominator is the entire skill.

05

The write-up that earns method marks

If the paper asks for procedure, these four sentences are the marks. Copy the shape, not just the numbers.

  1. Since cosine is an even function, \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)\).
  2. Writing \(\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) expresses the angle as a difference of two standard angles.
  3. By the cosine difference identity \(\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B\), \(\ \cos\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12\).
  4. Therefore \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\).

Three things markers look for that students skip: naming the parity property, naming the identity, and presenting the decomposition as a deliberate choice rather than letting \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) appear from nowhere. Those are free marks.

06

Check it in eight seconds

\(\sqrt6\approx2.449\) and \(\sqrt2\approx1.414\), so the answer is about \(3.863/4\approx0.966\). Is that plausible? \(-\pi/12\) is only \(-15^\circ\), and \(\cos 0=1\), so the value must be positive and a hair under 1. It fits.

The nested radical is the expected answer form here. Do not try to denest it. Möbius accepts sqrt(2+sqrt(2))/2.

Family 3Reciprocal functionsflip, then conjugate

Do the cosine or sine first, then flip and rationalise. There is no "secant difference formula", so do not go looking for one.

$$\sec\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\frac{4}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2} =\frac{4(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)}{6-2}=\sqrt6-\sqrt2\approx1.0353$$

The conjugate step is the whole content of this variation. The difference of squares collapses the denominator to \(6-2=4\), which cancels the 4 on top, which is why these come out so clean: \(\csc\frac{\pi}{12}=\sqrt6+\sqrt2\approx3.8637\) and \(\cot\frac{\pi}{12}=2+\sqrt3\approx3.7321\).

Family 4Tangent of a 15° angledivide, or tan formula
$$\tan\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}=\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1} =\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{2}=\frac{4-2\sqrt3}{2}=2-\sqrt3\approx0.2679$$

That first form is exact but unsimplified; rationalising by \(\sqrt3-1\) finishes it. Faster alternative: if you already have the sine and the cosine, just divide them, \(\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}\), and rationalise to the same \(2-\sqrt3\). Dividing beats learning a third formula.

Family 5Inverse trig compositionreference triangle

\(\sin(\arccos\frac35)\) and relatives. There is no identity for these. Draw the triangle. \(\arccos\frac35\) means "the angle whose cosine is \(\frac35\)", so label adjacent 3 and hypotenuse 5; Pythagoras gives opposite 4. Then read off what the outer function wants: \(\sin(\arccos\frac35)=\frac45\).

$$\cos\left(\arcsin\frac13\right)=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\approx0.9428 \qquad \tan\left(\arcsin\frac13\right)=\frac{\sqrt2}{4} \qquad \tan\left(\arccos\left(-\frac23\right)\right)=-\frac{\sqrt5}{2}$$
Where this one bites

Get the magnitude from the triangle and the sign from the inverse function's range. \(\arccos\) has range \([0,\pi]\), so a negative cosine puts the angle in quadrant II, where tangent is negative. \(\arcsin\) and \(\arctan\) land in \([-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]\).

The algebraic version is the same method: \(\tan(\arcsin x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\), from a triangle with opposite \(x\) and hypotenuse 1.

Family 6Double-angle compositionpick the right identity form

\(\cos(2\arcsin\frac13)\) looks worse than it is. Set \(\theta=\arcsin\frac13\), so \(\sin\theta=\frac13\) by definition, then choose the form of the double-angle identity whose input you already have:

$$\cos\left(2\arcsin\frac13\right)=1-2\sin^2\theta=1-\frac29=\frac79\approx0.7778$$

You never compute \(\cos\theta\) at all. That is the whole trick. Same move elsewhere: \(\sin(2\arccos\frac35)=2\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac35=\frac{24}{25}\), \(\tan(2\arctan\frac12)=\frac43\), and \(\cos(2\arctan\frac34)=\frac{7}{25}=0.28\).

Family 7The identity read backwardsrecognise, do not grind

Sometimes the question hands you the right-hand side of an identity and the value is one line.

$$\sin75^\circ\cos15^\circ+\cos75^\circ\sin15^\circ=\sin90^\circ=1 \qquad \cos^2\frac{\pi}{8}-\sin^2\frac{\pi}{8}=\cos\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$ $$2\sin\frac{\pi}{12}\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=\sin\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac12 \qquad \cos\frac{\pi}{12}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+\sin\frac{\pi}{12}\sin\frac{\pi}{4}=\cos\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$

The recognition cue: two products added or subtracted, with the same two angles in both terms, is always a sum or difference formula collapsing. A squared cosine minus a squared sine of the same angle is always a double angle collapsing.

Family 8Deeper nestinghalf angle, twice

\(\pi/24\) is \(7.5^\circ\), half of \(15^\circ\), so apply half-angle to the answer you already have:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{24}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/12)}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}}{2}\approx0.9914$$

Doubly nested radicals are the correct final form. This is unlikely on the midterm, but knowing the pattern continues tells you the method is general rather than a bag of memorised special cases.

09

Traps, ranked by what they actually cost

  1. The middle sign of \(\cos(A\pm B)\). The formula's sign is the opposite of the bracket's, so \(\cos(A-B)\) has a plus. Number one error on this question.
  2. Bracketing the numerator on entry. sqrt(6)+sqrt(2)/4 is a different number from (sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4.
  3. Giving a decimal. "Exact value" forbids it, at any precision.
  4. Applying odd-function parity to cosine. \(\cos(-x)=+\cos x\). Writing \(-\cos x\) flips the answer's sign for free.
  5. Choosing the half-angle \(\pm\) from the wrong quadrant. The sign comes from \(\theta/2\), the angle you are computing.
  6. Degrees versus radians. \(\pi/12\) is \(15^\circ\), not \(12^\circ\). A calculator left in degrees makes every numerical check lie to you silently.
  7. Forgetting to reduce past \(2\pi\). \(\frac{25\pi}{12}\) is coterminal with \(\frac{\pi}{12}\).
  8. Not rationalising a reciprocal. \(\frac{4}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}\) is exact. Möbius accepts it; a human marker may call it unsimplified.
  9. Reaching for a formula in Family 0. Denominator 2, 3, 4 or 6 means recall is the method.
  10. Trying to denest a \(\pi/8\) answer. The nested radical is the answer there.
