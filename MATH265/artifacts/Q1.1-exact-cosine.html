Athabasca MATH 265 · Midterm 1 · Q1 · 5 points

Exact value of a cosine

Question 1 of the midterm looks like a warm-up and is actually a gate. It checks three reflexes that reappear all over the paper: parity, decomposing an awkward angle into standard ones, and giving an answer in radicals instead of decimals. This is the method, every variation of it, and the eight-second check.

The question and the answer
Asked

Give the exact value of \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)\).

Exact value $$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$$

The whole method in four lines

If you remember one thing, remember this chain. It runs in about twenty seconds with no calculator.

cos(-pi/12)
  -> cos is EVEN, so drop the minus sign      cos(pi/12)
  -> pi/12 is 15 deg, and 15 = 45 - 30        cos(pi/4 - pi/6)
  -> cos(A-B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB        (r2/2)(r3/2) + (r2/2)(1/2)
  -> combine over 4                          (r6 + r2)/4
The one sign that ruins it

The cosine sum-and-difference formula has the opposite sign to the bracket. So \(\cos(A-B)\) has a plus in the middle. Say "cosine flips" out loud every time you write it. This single sign is the most common way to lose this question.

02

What has to be recall, not derivation

You cannot do this question "fast" by rebuilding these on the spot.

The angle table: every angle that has an exact value

Every multiple of \(15^\circ\) from 0 to 360, plus every odd multiple of \(22.5^\circ\). That is 33 rows, and every one of them has an exact value. That is the point of this row set: these 33 angles are, near enough, the complete list of angles an "exact value" question can use.

An angle that is not here, such as \(5^\circ\), \(10^\circ\) or \(20^\circ\), has no expression in square roots at all. It needs cube roots, because a regular 72-sided polygon is not constructible. So when a question hands you an angle, the first check is whether it is in this table. If it is not, either you have misread it or it needs reducing first.

The table also picks your tool

Which family a row belongs to tells you which of the three tools to reach for, before you write anything down.

17 rows · multiples of 30 or 45 · straight recall 8 rows · ends in 15 or 75 · sum or difference, 03A 8 rows · odd multiples of 22.5 · half angle, 03B
How to read the two tables

All six trig functions are here, three per table, and both tables repeat the degree and radian columns so each one is complete on its own. That split is deliberate: five columns fit an iPhone held sideways, nine do not, and the angle you are looking up stays the leftmost thing on the line. The first table holds the three functions a question asks for directly; the second holds their reciprocals, which you would otherwise have to flip and rationalise by hand.

Two of the six carry an even chip: \(\cos(-x)=\cos x\) and \(\sec(-x)=\sec x\), so for those two a minus sign inside the bracket evaporates and the row you want is the one for the positive angle. The other four are odd and pull the minus out in front instead. That is the first move of Q1.1.

\(\csc x = 1/\sin x\) and \(\sec x = 1/\cos x\), so those two columns are sine and cosine turned upside down, sign and all. Their size is never below 1, which is a free sanity check. Once rationalised they stop looking like their parents: \(4/(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)\) is \(\sqrt6+\sqrt2\), so the reciprocal columns carry the same surds without the quarter.

\(\cot x = 1/\tan x = \cos x/\sin x\), so tan and cot are reciprocals and each is undefined exactly where the other is zero. Tan and sec are undefined at \(90^\circ\) and \(270^\circ\), where cosine is zero; cot and csc at \(0^\circ\), \(180^\circ\) and \(360^\circ\), where sine is zero.

Notice how few distinct numbers there actually are. The whole 15-degree family uses only \(\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\) and \(\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\) for its sines and cosines, and only \(2+\sqrt3\) and \(2-\sqrt3\) for its tangents. The whole 22.5-degree family uses only \(\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\) and \(\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}\), and only \(\sqrt2+1\) and \(\sqrt2-1\). Everything else across those 16 rows is a sign.

Never memorise 33 rows. Reduce your angle to a reference angle in \([0,90]\), read the magnitude off the top of the table, then attach the sign from ASTC. Two symmetries make good checks: sine mirrors about \(90^\circ\) so rows 15 and 165 share a sine, and the whole table repeats with both signs flipped after \(180^\circ\) so row 195 carries row 15's surds negated. Tangent and cotangent, by contrast, repeat every \(180^\circ\) with no sign change, which is why rows 15 and 195 have the identical tangent \(2-\sqrt3\).

For an angle outside \([0,360]\), subtract full turns until it lands in the table: \(\frac{25\pi}{12}=375^\circ\) is coterminal with \(15^\circ\). For a negative angle, use parity first.

Parity, and quadrant signs

Even: drop the minusOdd: pull the minus out front
\(\cos(-x)=\cos x\)  ·  \(\sec(-x)=\sec x\) \(\sin(-x)=-\sin x\)  ·  \(\tan(-x)=-\tan x\)  ·  \(\csc,\cot\) too

Two even, four odd. Cosine and its reciprocal are the even pair; everything else is odd. For signs, ASTC: quadrant I All positive, II Sine only, III Tangent only, IV Cosine only. Reduce to the reference angle first, take the magnitude, then attach the sign. The reference angle is measured to the nearest part of the \(x\)-axis, never the \(y\)-axis.

The two formula families

$$\cos(A\mp B)=\cos A\cos B \pm \sin A\sin B \qquad \sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$ $$\tan(A\pm B)=\frac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B} \qquad \cos\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}} \qquad \sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}}$$

On the half-angle formulas the \(\pm\) is not decoration. You pick it from the quadrant of \(\theta/2\), the angle you are computing, and not from \(\theta\).

03

The procedure for any exact-value question

Four moves. Learn the moves rather than the answers, and the whole family becomes one question.

  1. Strip. Kill the minus sign with parity. Reduce anything past \(2\pi\) by subtracting full turns. Note the quadrant and hold that sign aside.
  2. Classify. Look at the denominator of the angle. That one number picks your tool, per the table below.
  3. Substitute. Write the formula out in full symbols first, then substitute. Never substitute into a half-remembered formula.
  4. Simplify and check. Put it over one denominator, then run the eight-second check in section 06 before you commit.
The decisive table

This is the part worth memorising. The denominator of the angle tells you the method, and picking the method in ten seconds is the actual exam skill.

03A

Tool 1 · the sum and difference formulas

Use it when the angle is an odd multiple of \(\pi/12\), which in degrees means it ends in 15 or 75: 15, 75, 105, 165, 195, 255, 285, 345.

What the tool is for

The unit circle only hands you a small set of angles for free: multiples of 30, 45, 60 and 90. A sum or difference formula is the bridge from that set to an angle just outside it. The whole idea is one question: if I cannot reach this angle directly, can I reach it as a sum or a difference of two angles I already know? For \(15^\circ\) the answer is yes, because \(15=45-30\) and both of those are free.

The formulas

$$\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B \qquad \cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B$$ $$\sin(A+B)=\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B \qquad \sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B$$ $$\tan(A\pm B)=\frac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B}$$
The sign discipline, said as a slogan

Cosine flips. Sine follows. Tangent flips downstairs.

Cosine's middle sign is the opposite of the bracket's, so \(\cos(A-B)\) has a plus. Sine's middle sign matches the bracket. For tangent the numerator matches and the denominator is the opposite.

There is also a structural tell that catches a misremembered formula instantly. In the cosine expansion the two terms are like with like, \(\cos\cos\) and \(\sin\sin\). In the sine expansion they are mixed, \(\sin\cos\) and \(\cos\sin\). If you have written \(\cos(A-B)=\cos A\sin B+\ldots\) you are already wrong, whatever the sign says.

Where the formulas come from

You are not asked to prove these, but knowing the shape means you can rebuild one if you blank. Think of \(\cos(A-B)\) as the cosine of the angle between two unit vectors, one at angle \(A\) and one at angle \(B\). Those vectors are \((\cos A,\sin A)\) and \((\cos B,\sin B)\), and the cosine of the angle between two unit vectors is just their dot product:

$$\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B$$

That is the difference formula in one line, and it explains why the cosine version pairs like with like: a dot product multiplies first components together and second components together. Every other formula in the family follows by replacing \(B\) with \(-B\) and using parity.

How to find the decomposition

Do not hunt. Work backwards from the target: convert to degrees, write the free list (every multiple of 30 and every multiple of 45, so 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 135, 150, 180, 210, 225, 240, 270, 300, 315, 330), find a pair whose sum or difference is your target, then verify the split in fractions before using it. That last line prevents the worst error available here, which is a correct formula applied to a wrong split.

Read the last column, then do the least work

Only two of the eight targets ever need this formula. For \(15^\circ\) and \(75^\circ\) you have no choice, because they are their own reference angles and there is nothing to reduce. For the other six, reducing to a reference angle of \(15^\circ\) or \(75^\circ\) and taking the sign from ASTC is strictly less work than expanding a sum formula: two moves instead of a four-term expansion with a sign trap in the middle.

The real procedure for this family

Reduce to a reference angle first. If it comes out as 15 or 75, get the magnitude and attach the sign from ASTC. Only expand a sum or difference formula when you are actually sitting on \(15^\circ\) or \(75^\circ\).

Worked both ways on \(\cos 165^\circ\), so the difference is visible. Via a split: \(165=120+45\), so

$$\cos120\cos45-\sin120\sin45=\left(-\tfrac12\right)\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} =-\frac{\sqrt2+\sqrt6}{4}$$

Four terms, two of them signed, one sign trap. Via a reference angle: \(165=180-15\), so the magnitude is \(\cos15^\circ=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\) and quadrant II makes it negative, giving \(-\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\). Two moves. Same answer, verified numerically as \(-0.9659\).

What this tool cannot reach, and why

The arithmetic is closed against you

No sum or difference of free angles will ever give \(22.5^\circ\), \(67.5^\circ\) or any of that family. Every angle on the free list is a multiple of 15, and adding or subtracting multiples of 15 can only ever produce another multiple of 15. \(22.5\) is not one.

That is not a gap in your memory, it is a fact about the tool, and it is exactly why section 03B exists.

Two more boundaries. For a target outside \([0,360]\), reduce by full turns first: \(375^\circ\) is coterminal with \(15^\circ\). For a negative target, apply parity first, which is precisely the first move of Q1.1.

Worked example 1 · \(\sin\dfrac{5\pi}{12}\)

Denominator 12, so this family. \(\dfrac{5\pi}{12}\) is \(75^\circ=45+30\), and the split checks out: \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}+\dfrac{\pi}{6}=\dfrac{3\pi+2\pi}{12}=\dfrac{5\pi}{12}\). Sine follows, so a plus stays a plus, and the terms are mixed:

$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12 =\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.9659$$

Check: \(75^\circ\) is near \(90^\circ\) where sine is 1, so a value just under 1 is right.

Worked example 2 · \(\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{12}\), where the answer is negative

\(105^\circ\) is past \(90^\circ\), so no difference of two free angles under 90 reaches it. Use \(105=60+45\). Cosine flips, so a plus bracket gives a minus in the middle, like with like:

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2} =\frac{\sqrt2-\sqrt6}{4}\approx-0.2588$$

It must be negative, because \(105^\circ\) is in quadrant II. The sign fell out of the algebra and it agrees with ASTC, which is the check actually working rather than just restating itself.

Worked example 3 · \(\tan\dfrac{\pi}{12}\), and why you might skip the formula

Tangent flips downstairs, so a minus bracket puts a plus in the denominator:

$$\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}} =\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}=\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{2}=\frac{4-2\sqrt3}{2}=2-\sqrt3$$

The middle form is exact but unsimplified; a surd left in a denominator is not finished. Faster alternative if you already have the sine and cosine: divide them, \(\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}\), and rationalise to the same \(2-\sqrt3\approx0.2679\). Dividing reuses work you have done and needs one formula fewer.

03B

Tool 2 · the half-angle formula

Use it when the angle is an odd multiple of \(\pi/8\): 22.5, 67.5, 112.5, 157.5 degrees.

What the tool is for, and why Tool 1 cannot do this

Tool 1 fails here for a concrete reason. Every standard angle is a multiple of \(15^\circ\), and adding or subtracting multiples of 15 can only ever give another multiple of 15. \(22.5\) is not one. So you need a tool that halves rather than adds, and \(22.5\) is exactly half of \(45\).

The classification rule, in one line

Denominator 12 means add or subtract. Denominator 8 means halve.

Where the formula comes from

Derive it rather than memorise it, because the derivation is three lines and the formula carries a \(\pm\) that memory tends to drop. Start from the two double-angle forms that each contain only one function:

$$\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta-1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \cos 2\theta = 1-2\sin^2\theta$$

Solve each for the squared term:

$$\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2} \qquad\qquad \sin^2\theta=\frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}$$

Now rename, letting \(\theta=\dfrac{\varphi}{2}\) so that \(2\theta=\varphi\), and take the root:

$$\cos\frac{\varphi}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}} \qquad\qquad \sin\frac{\varphi}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\varphi}{2}}$$

Two examinable facts fall out of that. The \(\pm\) is the price of the square root: squaring destroyed the sign, so taking the root cannot recover it and you must supply it from outside the algebra. And cosine gets the plus, sine gets the minus inside the fraction. If you mix those up, substitute \(\varphi=0\): cosine gives \(\sqrt{(1+1)/2}=1\) and sine gives \(\sqrt{0}=0\), both correct, which settles it in five seconds.

Choosing the sign, which is the whole difficulty

The number one half-angle error

The sign comes from the quadrant of the half angle \(\varphi/2\), the angle you are computing. It does not come from \(\varphi\).

Make it a separate written step: "the half angle is \(X^\circ\), quadrant \(Q\), so the sign is \(\pm\)". Then do the algebra.

The answer form: leave the nested radical alone

A half-angle answer is a nested radical, a root inside a root, like \(\dfrac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\). That is the correct and expected final form for this family. Do not try to flatten it; it usually cannot be flattened, and the attempt is where time disappears. Hold the contrast: the \(\pi/12\) family produces clean surds, the \(\pi/8\) family produces nested ones. Staring at a nested radical for a \(\pi/12\) angle means you picked the wrong tool.

Worked example 1 · \(\cos\dfrac{\pi}{8}\)

\(\dfrac{\pi}{8}=22.5^\circ\) is half of \(45^\circ\), so \(\varphi=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\). Sign first: the half angle \(22.5^\circ\) is in quadrant I, cosine positive, take the plus.

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{8}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}} =\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx0.9239$$

Name that last step, because it is the one people skip: \(\sqrt{a/4}=\dfrac{\sqrt a}{2}\), because \(\sqrt4=2\). Pulling the 4 out of the root is what puts the answer in standard form.

Worked example 2 · \(\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{8}\), and a double negative

\(67.5^\circ\) is half of \(135^\circ\), so \(\varphi=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\) and \(\cos\varphi=-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\). The half angle is in quadrant I, so sine is positive.

$$\sin\frac{3\pi}{8}=+\sqrt{\frac{1-\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}} =\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx0.9239$$

Watch the first step: the formula subtracts \(\cos\varphi\), and \(\cos\varphi\) is itself negative, so the two minuses make a plus. That is the second most common error in this tool. Sanity check on the result: \(\sin 67.5^\circ\) must equal \(\cos 22.5^\circ\) because the angles are complements, and it does.

Worked example 3 · \(\cos\dfrac{5\pi}{8}\), the case where the sign matters

\(112.5^\circ\) is half of \(225^\circ\), so \(\varphi=\dfrac{5\pi}{4}\) and \(\cos\varphi=-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\). Sign first, and this is the point of the example: the half angle \(112.5^\circ\) is in quadrant II, where cosine is negative.

$$\cos\frac{5\pi}{8}=-\sqrt{\frac{1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}}=-\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}\approx-0.3827$$

Had you instead reasoned from \(\varphi=225^\circ\) being in quadrant III, you would have got the right sign here by luck. Reason from the half angle every time and it is never luck.

The tangent half-angle, which comes out clean

There is a neater route for tangent that avoids nested radicals entirely, and it carries its own sign so there is no \(\pm\) to choose:

$$\tan\frac{\varphi}{2}=\frac{\sin\varphi}{1+\cos\varphi} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tan\frac{\pi}{8}=\frac{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2+\sqrt2}=\sqrt2-1\approx0.4142$$

This is the exception to "the \(\pi/8\) family gives nested radicals": the tangents are clean.

03C

Tool 3 · the reference triangle

Use it when an inverse trig function appears inside, such as \(\sin\!\left(\arccos\frac35\right)\) or \(\tan(\arcsin x)\).

What the tool is for

There is no identity for these, and looking for one is the trap. The tool is a picture.

The unlock is realising what an inverse trig function is. \(\arccos\frac35\) is not an operation waiting to happen. It is a number: an angle. Read it aloud as "the angle whose cosine is \(\frac35\)". So the expression \(\sin\!\left(\arccos\frac35\right)\) is really asking:

What the question actually says

There is some angle. I am telling you its cosine is \(\frac35\). What is its sine?

Written that way it stops being an inverse-function problem and becomes a right-triangle problem, which you have been able to do for years. The inverse function hands you one of the three ratios; the triangle plus Pythagoras gives you the other two.

The procedure

  1. Name it. Let \(\theta\) be the inner angle and write the defining equation. \(\theta=\arccos\frac35\) means \(\cos\theta=\frac35\).
  2. Draw a right triangle and label the two sides the ratio gives you, using SOH CAH TOA. Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse, so adj = 3 and hyp = 5.
  3. Pythagoras for the missing side.
  4. Read off the ratio the outer function asks for.
  5. Fix the sign from the RANGE of the inverse function, not from the drawing. Side lengths in a picture are always positive; the answer may not be.

Step 5 is the only step that is not mechanical, and it is where the marks are.

Step 5 in detail: the range decides the sign

An inverse function must return exactly one angle, so its output is restricted to one band. Those bands differ per function, and that is what fixes your sign.

functionrangequadrantsconsequence
\(\arcsin\)\(\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]\)IV and Ioutput cosine is always \(\ge 0\)
\(\arctan\)\(\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\)IV and Ioutput cosine is always \(> 0\)
\(\arccos\)\(\left[0,\pi\right]\)I and IIoutput sine is always \(\ge 0\)

The consequence column does most of the work. Inside an \(\arcsin\) or \(\arctan\), cosine and secant come out positive whatever the input's sign. Inside an \(\arccos\), sine and cosecant come out positive. And a negative input pushes \(\arcsin\) and \(\arctan\) into quadrant IV, but pushes \(\arccos\) into quadrant II. Get the magnitude from the triangle and the sign from the range: two separate steps, never mixed.

Worked example 1 · \(\sin\!\left(\arccos\dfrac35\right)\)

Let \(\theta=\arccos\dfrac35\), so \(\cos\theta=\dfrac35\). The input is positive and \(\arccos\) lands in \([0,\pi]\), so \(\theta\) is in quadrant I and everything is positive. Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse:

Worked example 2 · \(\tan\!\left(\arccos\!\left(-\dfrac23\right)\right)\), the sign case

Range first. \(\arccos\) lands in \([0,\pi]\) and the input is negative, so \(\theta\) is in quadrant II, where tangent is negative. Expect a negative answer before doing any algebra. Now the magnitudes: adj \(=2\), hyp \(=3\), so opp \(=\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5\).

$$\tan\left(\arccos\left(-\frac23\right)\right)=-\frac{\sqrt5}{2}\approx-1.1180$$

Note what did not flip: \(\sin\theta=+\dfrac{\sqrt5}{3}\) stays positive, because the range of \(\arccos\) guarantees a non-negative sine. Keep drawn lengths positive and handle the sign separately; carrying \(-2\) into the picture happens to work here but breaks as soon as you need a sine or a squared quantity.

Worked example 3 · \(\sec(\arctan 2)\)

\(\tan\theta=2=\dfrac21\), so opp \(=2\) and adj \(=1\), and \(\arctan\) of a positive number sits in quadrant I. Then hyp \(=\sqrt{4+1}=\sqrt5\), and secant is hypotenuse over adjacent:

$$\sec(\arctan 2)=\sqrt5\approx2.2361$$
Pythagoras runs the other way here

With tangent you are given the two legs, so Pythagoras adds. With sine or cosine you are given one leg and the hypotenuse, so it subtracts. Getting that backwards gives \(\sqrt{4-1}\) and a wrong answer that still looks plausible.

Worked example 4 · the algebraic version, \(\tan(\arcsin x)\)

Identical method with a letter instead of a number. Let \(\theta=\arcsin x\), so \(\sin\theta=\dfrac{x}{1}\): put opp \(=x\) and hyp \(=1\), giving adj \(=\sqrt{1-x^2}\).

$$\tan(\arcsin x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$$

04

Three routes, and which one to use

All three are legitimate and all three land on the same surd. They are not equally good under pressure.

Route A — parity first, then the difference formula

Cosine is even, so \(\cos\!\left(-\tfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\tfrac{\pi}{12}\). Then split the angle, checking the split before using it: \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}=\tfrac{3\pi-2\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{12}\). Good.

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\sin\frac{\pi}{6} =\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} =\frac{\sqrt6}{4}+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$$

Route B — the same formula without using parity

Keep the minus inside and split \(-\tfrac{\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{6}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), which gives \(\tfrac{\sqrt6}{4}+\tfrac{\sqrt2}{4}\) again. That is the point: cosine does not care about the order of the subtraction, because it is even. A third valid split is \(\tfrac{\pi}{12}=\tfrac{\pi}{3}-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\), also verified to give 0.9659258263. Useful as a cross-check, but it is one more thing to get right.

Route C — half angle from \(\pi/6\)

\(\tfrac{\pi}{12}\) is half of \(\tfrac{\pi}{6}\), and it sits in quadrant I, so take the plus:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/6)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2}} =\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}$$

Correct, and numerically identical, but it is a nested radical. It does equal the clean form, \(\tfrac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}=\tfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\), and denesting that by hand is real work you did not have to do.

Route A · use this

4 lines. One risk: the middle sign. Output is already the clean surd. This is the exam answer.

Route B · cross-check

4 lines. Two risks: the middle sign, and the direction of the split. Same clean output.

Route C · hold in reserve

5 lines plus denesting. Two risks: choosing the \(\pm\), and the nested form. This is the right tool for \(\pi/8\), not for \(\pi/12\).

The decision, stated

Half-angle is correct for \(\pi/8\) angles, where no sum of standard angles can reach the target. It is the wrong tool for \(\pi/12\) angles, where a difference of standard angles lands on it exactly. Matching tool to denominator is the entire skill.

05

The write-up that earns method marks

If the paper asks for procedure, these four sentences are the marks. Copy the shape, not just the numbers.

  1. Since cosine is an even function, \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)\).
  2. Writing \(\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}\) expresses the angle as a difference of two standard angles.
  3. By the cosine difference identity \(\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B\), \(\ \cos\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12\).
  4. Therefore \(\cos\!\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\).

Three things markers look for that students skip: naming the parity property, naming the identity, and presenting the decomposition as a deliberate choice rather than letting \(\tfrac{\pi}{4}-\tfrac{\pi}{6}\) appear from nowhere. Those are free marks.

Traps, ranked by what they actually cost

  1. The middle sign of \(\cos(A\pm B)\). The formula's sign is the opposite of the bracket's, so \(\cos(A-B)\) has a plus. Number one error on this question.
  2. Bracketing the numerator on entry. sqrt(6)+sqrt(2)/4 is a different number from (sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4.
  3. Giving a decimal. "Exact value" forbids it, at any precision.
  4. Applying odd-function parity to cosine. \(\cos(-x)=+\cos x\). Writing \(-\cos x\) flips the answer's sign for free.
  5. Choosing the half-angle \(\pm\) from the wrong quadrant. The sign comes from \(\theta/2\), the angle you are computing.
  6. Degrees versus radians. \(\pi/12\) is \(15^\circ\), not \(12^\circ\). A calculator left in degrees makes every numerical check lie to you silently.
  7. Forgetting to reduce past \(2\pi\). \(\frac{25\pi}{12}\) is coterminal with \(\frac{\pi}{12}\).
  8. Not rationalising a reciprocal. \(\frac{4}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}\) is exact. Möbius accepts it; a human marker may call it unsimplified.
  9. Reaching for a formula in Family 0. Denominator 2, 3, 4 or 6 means recall is the method.
  10. Trying to denest a \(\pi/8\) answer. The nested radical is the answer there.
