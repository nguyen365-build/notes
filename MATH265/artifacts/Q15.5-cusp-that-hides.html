MATH 265 · FINAL EXAM 2 · ITEM 7(a) · SIX POINTS, SHARED WITH Q17.3

The Cusp That Hides

Every closed-interval question in this course runs the same four lines: differentiate, solve \(f'=0\), add the endpoints, compare. This one adds a fifth kind of candidate, a point where \(f'\) does not exist at all, and then arranges the interval so that missing it costs you nothing. Both reported values come out right. The habit is what is being tested, and the answer cannot tell you whether you have it. This page is about the point with no slope, and about why this particular interval lets you get away with ignoring it.

02 The step that produces the answer

Write \(u = x^2+2x\), so \(u' = 2(x+1)\) and the chain rule gives

$$f'(x) \;=\; \tfrac{2}{3}u^{-1/3}u' \;=\; \frac{4(x+1)}{3\sqrt[3]{x^2+2x}}$$

Now write the next line, which is the one almost nobody writes:

$$\operatorname{dom}(f') \;=\; \mathbb{R}\setminus\{-2,\,0\}$$

The domain of \(f\) is all of \(\mathbb{R}\). The domain of \(f'\) is not, because the cube root in the denominator is zero exactly where \(x(x+2)\) is. Those two points are candidates, and setting \(f'(x)=0\) will never produce them.

Why a point with no slope is still a candidate

FERMAT NEEDS \(f'\) TO EXIST

Fermat's theorem says an interior extremum forces \(f'(c)=0\) only if \(f'(c)\) exists. Where it does not exist, the theorem says nothing at all, so the point cannot be ruled out. It is not a technicality bolted on for completeness: here the absolute minimum is attained at exactly such a point.

Why solving \(f'=0\) is not a slightly incomplete method

IT CANNOT SEE THE ANSWER

The candidate \(x=0\) is where the minimum lives, and it is invisible to every algebraic manipulation of the equation \(4(x+1)=0\). A student who never writes the derivative's domain down has not made a small omission; the answer is simply not in the set they searched.

The discriminator, and it is worth memorising. For \(g(x)^{p}\) with \(g(c)=0\) and \(g'(c)\ne 0\): if \(p < 1\) then \(f'\) blows up at \(c\) and \(c\) is a critical point of the undefined kind; if \(p \ge 1\) then \(f'\) is finite there and usually zero. Here \(p = 2/3\). This is measured rather than asserted: the harness probes the six exponents \(1/3, 2/3, 1, 4/3, 5/3, 2\) and the one-sided slope blows up on exactly the two below \(1\). Had the examiner written \(4/3\), solving \(f'=0\) alone would have been enough.

03 The function, and the two points that tie

The base factors twice over. As \(x(x+2)\) it shows the two zeros; as \((x+1)^2-1\) it shows that \(f\) depends on \(x\) only through \((x+1)^2\), so \(f\) is symmetric about the vertical line \(x=-1\) and the reflection \(x\mapsto -2-x\) leaves it unchanged. That single observation explains the whole picture: the arch on \([-2,0]\) is a mirror image about its peak at \(x=-1\), the two cusps are each other's reflection, and outside \([-2,0]\) the function grows without bound in both directions.

The interval \([-2,3]\) is not symmetric about \(x=-1\); its midpoint is \(x=\tfrac12\). So the right tail runs four units from the centre and the left tail runs one, which is why the maximum is at \(x=3\) and not at the other end.

04 Corner, cusp, vertical tangent

All three are points where \(f'\) fails to exist, all three are critical points, and only one of them is a cusp. Settle it from the two one-sided limits of \(f'\), never from a picture and never from \(f''\).

The classification here, computed

Near \(x=0\) the numerator \(4(x+1)\) tends to \(4\), a fixed positive number, while \(3\sqrt[3]{x(x+2)}\) approaches zero through positive values from the right and through negative values from the left, because the base changes sign at \(0\). Therefore

$$\lim_{x\to 0^-}f'(x) = -\infty, \qquad \lim_{x\to 0^+}f'(x) = +\infty$$

Opposite signs, both infinite. It is a cusp. Numerically \(f'(-10^{-9}) \approx -1058.27\) and \(f'(+10^{-9}) \approx +1058.27\), and both grow without bound as the step shrinks. The same computation at \(x=-2\) also gives a cusp on the natural domain; inside \([-2,3]\) only its right branch exists, and it is on the candidate list anyway because it is an endpoint.

The reflex check - take a centred difference quotient and see whether it is near zero - gives the wrong answer with total confidence. At \(h=10^{-6}\) it reads \(5.29\times10^{-5}\) while the two one-sided quotients are \(+158.740158\) and \(-158.740052\), an understatement by a factor above a million, because the two one-sided errors are near mirror images and cancel in the subtraction. On a symmetric corner the same quotient returns exactly \(0\); on an asymmetric corner it returns the average of the two slopes. Three behaviours, one method, none of them the derivative. Test one-sided slopes separately, every time.

The one-sided quotient diverges like \(h^{-1/3}\), so cutting \(h\) by a thousand multiplies it by exactly ten. Its closed form is \((h^2+2h)^{2/3}/h \to 2^{2/3}h^{-1/3}\), and \(2^{2/3} = 1.5874010520\), which is where the digits \(15874\) come from. That rate is also a fingerprint: a slope ladder that refuses to converge at order one is telling you it is standing on a fractional power.

Read the base as \(\left(x^2+2x\right)^{2/3} = \left(\left(x^2+2x\right)^2\right)^{1/3}\). The base is strictly negative on \((-2,0)\), which is most of the left half of the interval, and an even numerator kills the sign. That is why \(f \ge 0\) everywhere and why the minimum can be exactly zero. Reading \((-1)^{2/3}\) as \(-1\) would put the minimum at \(-1\), which contradicts the range - and note that on Q15.1(a), where the exponent's numerator is odd, the same misread gave \(+3\) and hid completely.

The best single check on this page is to cube the answer. \(6.0822019956^3 = 225\) exactly, because \(\left(15^{2/3}\right)^3 = 15^2\). And \(15^{2/3}\) has to sit between \(8^{2/3}=4\) and \(27^{2/3}=9\), much nearer the bottom, so about six. The two wrong exponents are far away: \(15^{1/3}=2.4662120743\) and \(15^{3/2}=58.09\).

06 The mistake that hides

Fifteen mutilations of the candidate list were scanned, with nothing excluded as degenerate, and each scored on whether the reported pair of values changes. The result is 10 HIDE, 5 SHOW.

The defining mistake

HIDES

Drop the interior cusp and the candidate list becomes \(\{-2,-1,3\}\), whose values are \(0\), \(1\) and \(15^{2/3}\). Minimum \(0\), maximum \(15^{2/3}\). Both correct. Drop both cusps as a class and nothing changes either, because \(x=-2\) is an endpoint and endpoints are on every list ever written.

Where it is visible

IN THE POINTS

The true argmin list has two entries; the mutilated one has a single entry, \(x=-2\). So the only box on this exam that can catch the error is a box asking where the minimum occurs. Report value and location as two separate answers and the error surfaces; report only the value and it never does.

Whose fault the hiding is

THE INTERVAL

Move the left endpoint from \(-2\) to \(-1.5\) and the cusp loses its twin. Dropping it then reports \(0.8254818122\) instead of \(0\), and the whole minimum is gone. The hiding belongs to this interval, not to the method, which is the argument for fixing the habit rather than trusting the instance.

Three method notes worth keeping. Every critical point here is at a rational fraction of the interval, namely \(0\), \(\tfrac15\) and \(\tfrac25\), so a naive sign-change census returns \(0\) on any grid whose subinterval count is divisible by five and \(2\) on any grid that is not; a census that steps over exact zeros returns \(2\) at every resolution tried. A sign-change census also counts zeros and cusps together and cannot tell them apart, which is why a fourth arm hunts the base's zeros separately. And a dense sweep's tolerance has to be derived from the function's own variation across one grid step, because \(f\) is not Lipschitz at a cusp: the sweep's value error there is \(O(\text{step}^{2/3})\), and a Lipschitz-derived tolerance produced nine false failures before it was replaced by a measured one.

A digit-lock inherits the precision of the method it locks. The part-(b) integrals were first computed with plain Simpson and locked to ten decimals; Simpson's measured order on this integrand is \(5/3\), not \(4\), because the integrand has a cusp, so the tenth decimal was wrong while two independent resolutions agreed with each other. The substitution \(x=s^3\) removes the singularity and the corrected values live in block q155c. Self-consistency is not accuracy.
