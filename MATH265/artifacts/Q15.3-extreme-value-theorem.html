MATH 265 · FINAL EXAM 2 · ITEM 3a · THE THEOREM IS NAMED ONCE IN THE COURSE

Existence Is Not Location

Q15.3 names the Extreme Value Theorem, and Q15.1 does not. That is the whole difference between them. Q15.1 asked you to run the closed-interval method; Q15.3 asks you to justify it, and the justification carries marks the answer does not. The theorem proves that the two extremes exist. It does not say where they are, how many there are, or how to find them. This page is about that gap.

02 The theorem, and the theorem it is usually confused with

Most students meet the EVT as a recipe: find the critical points, add the endpoints, compare. That recipe is two theorems stapled together, and only one of them is the EVT.

Theorem 1, the EVT

EXISTENCE

If \(f\) is continuous on a closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\), then there exist points \(c\) and \(d\) in \([a,b]\) with \(f(d) \le f(x) \le f(c)\) for every \(x\) in \([a,b]\). Three hypotheses, one conclusion, and the conclusion is that such points exist. It names none of them.

Theorem 2, Fermat

LOCATION

If \(f\) has a local extremum at an interior point \(c\) and \(f'(c)\) exists, then \(f'(c)=0\). Read the contrapositive, which is the useful direction: an interior point where \(f'\) exists and is not zero cannot be an extremum. That is what shrinks the search to a finite list.

So the candidate list comes from Fermat, not from the EVT. And notice what Fermat says about an endpoint: nothing, because an endpoint is not interior. On this question \(f'(-1) = 1/2\) and \(f'(1) = -1/2\), both plainly nonzero, and both of those points are minimisers.

No derivative condition flags an endpoint. You write them into the list by hand, or you lose them.

03 What each hypothesis is actually holding up

The three same-function rows are worth memorising as a set, because between them they show that the two extremes fail independently. Remove the ends and you lose the one that lived there. Puncture the middle and you lose the one that lived there. The hypotheses are not a ritual; each one is holding up a specific part of the conclusion.

And a dropped hypothesis does not force failure

This is the half that gets stated backwards in exams. The hypotheses are sufficient, not necessary. When one fails, the theorem is silent, not negative.

Open domain

STILL ATTAINS ITS MINIMUM

\(x^2\) on the open \((-1,1)\) reaches \(0\) at \(x=0\). The minimum is fine; only the maximum is lost.

Discontinuous

STILL ATTAINS BOTH

A step function on \([-1,1]\) attains \(0\) at \(x=-1\) and \(1\) at \(x=0\). A jump is not automatically fatal.

Unbounded domain

STILL ATTAINS ITS MAXIMUM

\(f\) itself on \([0,\infty)\) reaches \(1\) at \(x=0\). Losing one hypothesis does not lose both extremes.

So "the domain is open, therefore there is no maximum" is a wrong sentence. The right one is: "the domain is open, so the EVT does not guarantee one, and here there is not."

04 The candidate table, and the tie inside it

\(f\) is a quotient of polynomials whose denominator never vanishes, so it is continuous on all of \(\mathbb{R}\). Differentiate once, by the chain rule on \((x^2+1)^{-1}\) or by the quotient rule, which agree:

$$f'(x) = \frac{-2x}{(x^2+1)^2}, \qquad f''(x) = \frac{6x^2-2}{(x^2+1)^3}$$

The denominator of \(f'\) is at least \(1\), so \(f'\) exists everywhere and vanishes exactly when \(-2x = 0\), that is \(x=0\), which lies inside \([-1,1]\). Three candidates, and one of them is a tie.

Route B is worth knowing: the denominator of \(f'\) is positive, so the sign of \(f'\) is the sign of \(-2x\), which is positive on \([-1,0)\) and negative on \((0,1]\). So \(f\) increases then decreases, its maximum is the turning point, and its minimum is whichever end is lower. Both ends are equal, so both are minimisers.

Route C is the eight-second sanity check. \(x^2+1\) grows with \(|x|\), and taking a reciprocal reverses order on positive quantities, so \(f\) is decreasing in \(|x|\). Biggest where \(|x|\) is smallest, smallest where \(|x|\) is biggest. No calculus.

Use route A under exam pressure. It is the only one that generalises to every function the examiner can put in this slot, it is what "by the Extreme Value Theorem" is asking for, and its table documents itself for a marker.

05 The write-up a marker expects

Six sentences. Four things in them earn credit that a bare answer does not.

\(f(x) = 1/(x^2+1)\).

Since \(x^2+1 \ge 1 > 0\) for every real \(x\), the denominator never vanishes, so \(f\) is continuous on all of \(\mathbb{R}\) and in particular on the closed, bounded interval \([-1,1]\).

By the Extreme Value Theorem, \(f\) attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum on \([-1,1]\).

\(f'(x) = -2x/(x^2+1)^2\), which is defined for all \(x\) and vanishes only at \(x=0\), so the candidates are \(x=-1,0,1\).

\(f(-1) = 1/2\), \(f(0) = 1\), \(f(1) = 1/2\).

The absolute maximum value is \(1\), at \(x=0\); the absolute minimum value is \(1/2\), attained at both \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\).

  1. The reason \(f\) is continuous, not just the claim. "The denominator never vanishes" is the whole argument and it is nine words.
  2. The words "closed" and "bounded" attached to the interval. The stem named a theorem; citing its hypotheses as satisfied is the citation.
  3. "is defined for all \(x\)" about \(f'\). That is the empty box being reported as empty, which shows you looked for a cusp and found none.
  4. "attained at both". The minimum happens twice and the marker is looking for whether you noticed.

One thing that earns nothing here: a second-derivative test. It would work, since \(f''(0) = -2 < 0\), but the closed-interval comparison has already settled the question and the test cannot see the endpoints at all.

So Q15.3 is the question where the closed-interval method's most important step cannot be checked by the answer at all. Dropping the critical point does show, so the maximum's location is verifiable while the minimum's is not. Write both endpoints down every time as a habit, because on this question the answer will not tell you when you did not.

Two hiding mechanisms, live at once

Mechanism 1

AN ENDPOINT WITH A TWIN

Each endpoint carries the same value, so omitting either leaves the other to report it. Shift the peak to \(x=1\) and put the interval at \([-1,2]\) and the tie breaks: \(f(-1)=1/5\), \(f(2)=1/2\), and the same omission shows immediately. The hiding belongs to the symmetry of this one interval, not to the method.

Mechanism 2

A SYMMETRY FIXED POINT

\(x=0\) is the fixed point of \(x \mapsto -x\), and \(f\) is even. Any error that preserves that evenness leaves the maximiser sitting exactly where it belongs. The sign error and the un-squared denominator both do, which is why two obviously wrong derivatives still give the right answer.

And the value cannot check the point

The maximum is a smooth peak, so \(f\) is flat there. Measured: a point \(5\times 10^{-5}\) away from \(x=0\) has \(f\) within \(2.5\times 10^{-9}\) of \(1\). A nearly-right maximiser therefore produces a maximum that is right to nine decimal places.

Never validate the maximiser using the maximum. The verification behind this page root-finds on \(f'\) instead: that located \(x=0\) to within \(5\times 10^{-61}\), while a value comparison in the same arithmetic cannot resolve the position better than \(\sqrt{2\varepsilon f^{*}/|f''|} = 1.49\times 10^{-8}\). The gap is 52 orders of magnitude, and it belongs entirely to the comparison rather than to the arithmetic.

07 The other half of the item

Part (b) of item 3 asks for an estimate of \(\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{dx}{x^2+1}\) from the properties of the definite integral. The relevant property is the comparison bound:

$$m \le f(x) \le M \text{ on } [a,b] \quad \Longrightarrow \quad m(b-a) \;\le\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\le\; M(b-a)$$

Part (a) just produced \(m = 1/2\) and \(M = 1\), and \(b-a = 2\), so the bound is \([1,2]\). The true value is \(2\arctan 1 = \pi/2 = 1.5707963268\), comfortably inside. That is why the examiner chose this function and this interval, and it means part (b) is a free check on part (a).

09 The traps, ranked

  1. Reporting the location instead of the value. The stem says extreme values. Those are \(1\) and \(1/2\); the locations are \(0\) and \(\pm 1\). No magnitude check catches this, because \(0\) is a perfectly plausible input for this function.
  2. Naming only one endpoint for the minimum. It happens at \(x=-1\) and \(x=1\).
  3. Skipping the hypothesis check when the stem names the theorem. "By the Extreme Value Theorem" is an instruction, not a label. Two sentences is the cheapest credit on the item.
  4. Not testing the denominator against the interval. The reworded version puts a pole inside \([a,b]\) and the answer becomes a sentence. Solve denominator \(=0\) first, every time.
  5. Dropping the inner \(2x\). You get \(f' = -1/(x^2+1)^2\), which never vanishes, and land on max \(=\) min \(= 1/2\). That output is self-evidently broken: a maximum equal to a minimum says the function is constant.
  6. Setting the denominator of \(f'\) to zero rather than the numerator. Same broken answer, different route.
  7. Reaching for the second-derivative test. It happens to work here, but it cannot see the endpoints, and on the very similar \(1/(x^4+1)\) it goes silent.
  8. Reading a negative instance's maximum as the largest magnitude. Family 2's sign trap.
  9. Assuming a dropped hypothesis means the extreme is lost. The theorem is silent, not negative.
  10. Forgetting the \((b-a)\) factor in part (b). \([1/2,1]\) excludes the true \(\pi/2\).

Four checks, in the order they cost you time.

Magnitude · 3 seconds

0 < f <= 1

\(f\) is one over something at least \(1\). Any reported extreme outside \((0,1]\) is wrong on sight, which kills a reported minimum of \(-1/2\) and a maximum of \(2\).

Order

SMALLEST |x| WINS

\(f\) decreases in \(|x|\), so the candidate with the smallest \(|x|\) must carry the largest \(f\). Here \(|x|\) runs \(0,1,1\), so \(f\) must run largest, tied, tied.

Symmetry

f IS EVEN

The table must be symmetric about \(x=0\). Two endpoint values that differ on a symmetric interval are an arithmetic slip.

Part (b) · free

1 <= pi/2 <= 2

If the true integral falls outside \([m(b-a),\,M(b-a)]\), one of \(m\), \(M\) or \((b-a)\) is wrong. This is the only check that catches a wrong \(m\) or \(M\).

The single most expensive typo is answering the wrong question. Report the value and the point as two separate answers, always.
