# Q19.6 - Average temperature of a metal rod

**The question (Final Exam 1, item 14, 3 points).**
"The temperature of a metal rod, $6\text{ m}$ long, is $3x$ (in degrees centigrade) at a distance $x$ meters from one end of the rod.
What is the average temperature of the rod?"

**The answer.**
$$T_{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{1}{6-0}\int_0^6 3x\,dx \;=\; \frac{1}{6}\Big[\tfrac{3}{2}x^2\Big]_0^6 \;=\; \frac{54}{6} \;=\; 9$$
that is, **exactly $9$ degrees centigrade**.

## 0. The 30-second version

```
average value of f on [a,b]
  = (the total)  /  (the width)
  = (1/(b-a)) * INTEGRAL from a to b of f(x) dx

  rod:  a = 0,  b = 6,  f(x) = 3x
  INTEGRAL 3x dx  = (3/2)x^2  ->  (3/2)(36) - 0 = 54     [degC * m]
  divide by width 6                              = 9     [degC]

check: 0 <= 9 <= 18   (m and M are the endpoint temperatures)
check: units are degC, not degC*m   <- the forgotten division shows up HERE
```

That is the whole method.
Four lines, no lookup, and the two checks at the bottom take about six seconds each.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2.1 The formula, and the sentence that generates it

> **Average value.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then
> $$f_{\text{avg}} \;=\; \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.$$

Do not memorise it as a formula.
Memorise the picture, because the picture regenerates the formula and also tells you which mistakes are impossible.

```
        M = 18 +                                        /
                |                                     /
                |                                   /
                |                                 /
   f_avg = 9 +--+---------------------------- * -----------+
                |                         /  |             |   the rectangle
                |                       /    |  9 tall     |   and the triangle
                |                     /      |  6 wide     |   have the SAME area, 54
                |                   /        |             |
        m = 0   +-----------------*-----------------------+
                0                 3                       6
                                  ^
                              c = 3, where f actually equals 9
```

The area under $f$ is 54.
A rectangle 6 wide with the same area must be $54/6 = 9$ tall.
That is where the division comes from, and it is why the division is not optional: without it you are quoting an **area**, not a **height**.

**Why the rectangle works, in one picture.**
Lay the rectangle over the triangle and look at what is left over.
Two wedges: one inside the rectangle but above the line, running from $x=0$ to $x=3$, and one inside the triangle but above the rectangle, running from $x=3$ to $x=6$.
Both are right triangles with legs 3 and 9, so both have area 13.5, and each is exactly a quarter of the whole triangle.
Equal leftovers is precisely what "same area" means, so the congruence is the proof, not a decoration.

**The part of that which generalises, and the part which does not.**
The two leftover regions always have **equal area**, for any continuous $f$: that is just the average-value formula rearranged, since $\int_a^b (f_{\text{avg}} - f) = 0$.
They are **congruent shapes** only when $f$ is affine, because only then does the crossing land at the midpoint.
Verified: the crossing sits at 3.4641016173 for $x^2$ on $[0,6]$ against a midpoint of 3, at 3.9999991193 for $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,9]$ against 4.5, and at 1.1614393624 for $e^x$ on $[0,2]$ against 1, while the two areas agree to $10^{-5}$ in every case.

### 2.2 Why you divide by the interval length, said three ways

| Reading | The statement | What it rules out |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Geometric** | $f_{\text{avg}}$ is the height of the equal-area rectangle | Reporting 54, which is an area |
| **Dimensional** | $\int f\,dx$ carries (units of $f$) times (units of $x$); dividing by a length gives back the units of $f$ | Any answer in $^\circ\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$ |
| **Discrete limit** | The mean of $n$ equally spaced samples is $\frac{1}{n}\sum f(x_i) = \frac{1}{b-a}\sum f(x_i)\Delta x \to \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ | Any answer that is not a weighted mean of the values of $f$ |

The third reading is the one worth carrying, because it is the only one that explains why $\frac{1}{b-a}$ rather than $\frac{1}{n}$ or $\frac{1}{b}$.
The $\frac{1}{n}$ becomes $\frac{\Delta x}{b-a}$ when you write $\Delta x = \frac{b-a}{n}$, and that $(b-a)$ in the denominator is the only place it can come from.
This was verified directly: the mean of $n$ midpoint samples of $3x$ on $[0,6]$ returns 9 at $n = 10, 100, 1000, 10000$ and $100000$, and the one-sided sample means bracket 9 from both sides with the error shrinking by a measured factor of exactly 10 per tenfold refinement, which is the $O(1/n)$ of a one-sided rule.

### 2.3 The average of $f$ is NOT the average of the endpoint values

> **The trap in one line.** $\dfrac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}$ is the average of two numbers.
> $f_{\text{avg}}$ is the average of infinitely many.
> They agree **exactly when $f$ is affine** on $[a,b]$, and disagree otherwise.

**Check whether they coincide here.** They do.
$f(0) = 0$, $f(6) = 18$, and $\frac{0+18}{2} = 9 = f_{\text{avg}}$.
So on this exam question the endpoint-average mistake is **invisible**, and this is the single most important fact in this document.

Here is the coincidence breaking on four non-affine functions, all computed:

| $f$ and interval | true $f_{\text{avg}}$ | endpoint mean | midpoint value |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $3x$ on $[0,6]$ (**this question**) | $9$ | $9$ | $9$ |
| $x^2$ on $[0,6]$ | $12$ | $18$ | $9$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,6]$ | $1.6329931619$ | $1.2247448714$ | $1.7320508076$ |
| $e^x$ on $[0,6]$ | $67.0714655821$ | $202.2143967464$ | $20.0855369232$ |
| $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ | $0.6366197724$ | $0$ | $1$ |

Read the table as **one fact with four witnesses**, not as five rows.
The fact is: the top row is the only one where all three columns agree, and it is the only affine row.
Note also that the endpoint mean is too **high** for a convex $f$ ($x^2$, $e^x$) and too **low** for a concave one ($\sqrt{x}$, $\sin$ on $[0,\pi]$), because the chord sits above a convex curve and below a concave one.
That gives you a free sign on the error whenever you are tempted to use it.

**When the two coincide.** Exactly when $f$ is affine, $f(x) = \alpha x + \beta$.
Then $\int_a^b (\alpha x + \beta)\,dx = \frac{\alpha}{2}(b^2-a^2) + \beta(b-a)$, and dividing by $(b-a)$ gives $\frac{\alpha}{2}(a+b) + \beta$, which is both $f\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)$ and $\frac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}$.
Verified on five intervals, including one with $a < b < 0$, and on three different intercepts.
This is the **same theorem** as Q19.5's average-force shortcut and Q19.4's centre-of-mass shortcut, in a third disguise: a linear-average shortcut is exact if and only if the varying factor is affine in the slicing variable.

### 2.4 The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals

> **MVT for Integrals.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ there exists at least one $c$ in $[a,b]$ with
> $$f(c) \;=\; \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;=\; f(c)\,(b-a).$$

In words: the function really does hit its own average somewhere.
The rectangle in the picture of 2.1 is not a fiction; its top edge crosses the curve.

**Find it here.** Solve $3c = 9$, so $c = 3$ metres.
The temperature 3 m from the end really is $9\,^\circ\text{C}$.

**Is it the midpoint?** Yes, $c = 3$ and the midpoint of $[0,6]$ is also 3, and that is another degeneracy of this stem rather than a general fact.
The MVT point equals the midpoint precisely when $f$ is affine (or symmetric about the midpoint).
For $f(x) = x^p$ on $[0,6]$ the average is $\frac{6^p}{p+1}$ and the MVT point is $c = 6/(p+1)^{1/p}$, computed:

| $p$ | $f_{\text{avg}}$ on $[0,6]$ | MVT point $c$ | midpoint |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1$ | $3$ | $3.0000000000$ | $3$ |
| $2$ | $12$ | $3.4641016151$ | $3$ |
| $3$ | $54$ | $3.7797631497$ | $3$ |
| $4$ | $259.2$ | $4.0124418299$ | $3$ |

For $p=1$ the MVT point is $6/2 = 3$; for $p = 2$ it is $6/\sqrt3 = 3.4641016151$.
The point drifts **right** as the function gets more convex, because a convex function spends more of the interval below its average and has to climb further to reach it.
So "the MVT point is the midpoint" is a statement about **this** $f$, not about averages.

**Two things the theorem does not say.**
It does not say $c$ is unique (for $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ there are two).
It does not say $c$ is interior in the pathological cases, though for a non-constant continuous $f$ it always can be taken interior.
Here $f$ is strictly increasing, so there is **exactly one** $c$, which was confirmed by a sign-change census on $f(x)-9$ over a 4001-point grid returning exactly 1.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Read off $a$ and $b$.** They are the ends of the physical object or the time window, and $b-a$ is its **width**. Write the width down before anything else; that is the number people forget.
> 2. **Write $f$** as a function of the slicing variable, using the same variable the stem uses.
> 3. **Integrate** to get the total. Use FTC part 2 if you can find an antiderivative.
> 4. **Divide by $(b-a)$**, not by $b$, not by $n$, not by anything else.
> 5. **Check the band.** Find $m$ and $M$ on $[a,b]$ and confirm $m \le f_{\text{avg}} \le M$.
> 6. **Check the units.** The answer carries the units of $f$. If it carries the units of $f$ times length, you skipped step 4.
> 7. **If asked, solve $f(c) = f_{\text{avg}}$** for the MVT point, and say whether it is interior.

**Classifying the question in one glance:**

| The stem says | You are being asked for | Divide by |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "the average temperature / value / concentration of $f$" | $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ | the width $b-a$ |
| "the average rate of change of $f$" | $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ | the width $b-a$ |
| "the average velocity" | either, and they **agree**: $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b v = \frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}$ | the width $b-a$ |
| "the total / net change / amount" | $\int_a^b f$ | nothing |
| "find $k$ so that the average is $A$" | solve $\frac{1}{k-a}\int_a^k f = A$ | the width, symbolically |

Row three is worth pausing on.
For a **rate**, the average value of the rate equals the average rate of change of its antiderivative, because the FTC turns one into the other.
That is why Q6.1 and Q19.6 look like different questions and are secretly the same arithmetic when $f$ happens to be a derivative.
Row one and row two are only different because $3x$ here is a **temperature**, not a rate of anything.

## 4. Q19.6 worked five ways

### Route A - FTC part 2 (use this one)

$$\int_0^6 3x\,dx = \Big[\tfrac32 x^2\Big]_0^6 = \tfrac32(36) - \tfrac32(0) = 54,\qquad T_{\text{avg}} = \frac{54}{6} = 9$$

Two lines.
Under exam pressure this is the route.

### Route B - pull the constant out first

$$T_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{6}\int_0^6 3x\,dx = \frac{3}{6}\int_0^6 x\,dx = \frac12 \cdot \frac{6^2}{2} = \frac{36}{4} = 9$$

Slightly less arithmetic, and it makes the structure visible: $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b x\,dx = \frac{a+b}{2}$, so the average of any multiple of $x$ is that multiple of the midpoint.

### Route C - the equal-area rectangle, by geometry only

The graph of $3x$ on $[0,6]$ is a straight line from $(0,0)$ to $(6,18)$.
The region under it is a right triangle of base 6 and height 18, so its area is $\frac12 \cdot 6 \cdot 18 = 54$.
A rectangle of width 6 with area 54 has height 9.
**No calculus at all**, and it is a complete solution for this stem.

### Route D - the affine shortcut

$f$ is affine, so its average is its midpoint value: $f(3) = 9$.
One line.
**But this route is only legitimate because you checked that $f$ is affine**, and on the exam nobody sees you check.
It is the fastest route and the most dangerous one, for exactly the reason in section 2.3.

### Route E - the discrete definition, no antiderivative

Sample $3x$ at $n$ midpoints of equal subintervals and take the plain mean.
At $n = 10$ this already returns 9 to ten decimal places, because a midpoint rule is exact on an affine integrand.
This is the route that shows what the formula **means**, and it is the arm used in verification that never touches a closed form.

### Route W - the natural wrong answers

| Wrong move | Value | Caught? |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| Report the integral, forgetting to divide | $54$ | Yes, by units and by the band |
| Average the endpoint temperatures | $9$ | **No** |
| Divide by $b = 6$ instead of $b-a$ | $9$ | **No** |
| Use the midpoint temperature | $9$ | **No** |
| Antiderivative $3x^2$, dropping the $\tfrac12$ | $18$ | Yes, but only against 9 |

### Route comparison

| Route | Steps | Arithmetic risk | Works when $f$ is not affine | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - FTC part 2 | 2 | low | yes | **Use this one under pressure** |
| B - constant out first | 2 | lowest | yes | Equal-best; prefer if the constant is ugly |
| C - geometry | 1 | none | only for lines and simple regions | Excellent sanity check, and a full solution here |
| D - affine shortcut | 1 | none | **no** | Fastest, but it needs a proof you are affine |
| E - discrete mean | many | high by hand | yes | Conceptual only; do not do this on paper |

**Use Route A, and use Route C as the check.**
Routes A and C are genuinely independent: one uses an antiderivative, the other uses the area of a triangle, and they share no step that could go wrong the same way.
Route D agrees with A here for a reason that will not survive a change of stem, so it is not a check.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

For a 3-point answer-only item, only the number is marked.
Write this anyway, because it is what stops you writing 54:

> The average temperature is
> $$T_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{6-0}\int_0^6 3x\,dx = \frac16\Big[\tfrac32 x^2\Big]_0^6 = \frac{54}{6} = 9\,^\circ\text{C}.$$
> The temperature ranges from $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $18\,^\circ\text{C}$, so an average of $9\,^\circ\text{C}$ is in range, and by the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals the rod is at exactly $9\,^\circ\text{C}$ at the point $x = 3$ m.

Three sentences.
The middle clause is the band check and the last clause is the MVT point; both are free and both are worth writing if the item ever appears in a long-answer paper.

**If the question adds "and find the point where the rod is at its average temperature":**

> Solve $3c = 9$, giving $c = 3$ m.
> Since $f$ is strictly increasing, this point is unique.

## 6. Verify before you commit

**The magnitude check.**
The rod runs from $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $18\,^\circ\text{C}$, so the answer must land in $[0, 18]$.
This is Q17.1's comparison property with the width divided out:

$$m \le f(x) \le M \text{ on } [a,b] \;\Longrightarrow\; m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f \le M(b-a) \;\Longrightarrow\; m \le f_{\text{avg}} \le M.$$

Here $m = 0$ and $M = 18$, and 9 sits $9$ above the floor and $9$ below the ceiling, exactly halfway.
**That halfway position is a property of an affine $f$, not a general fact.**
Computed: $x^1$ sits at 0.5 of the band, $x^2$ at $1/3$, $x^3$ at $1/4$, so "the average is the middle of the range" is false as soon as the function curves.

**How $m$ and $M$ were established, and why it needed three arms.**
$f' = 3$ everywhere, checked by a complex-step derivative at 401 points with maximum deviation below $10^{-12}$, so $\min|f'| = 3 > 0$ and there are **no interior critical points**.
A sign-change sweep of $f'$ over grids of $999$, $1000$, $2001$ and $2048$ points found zero.
A left-versus-right difference-quotient scan at 800 interior points found no kink, so there is no point where $f'$ fails to exist either.
Therefore the extremes are the endpoint values, which a brute grid of 200001 points confirmed.

That census would be worthless if it always returned zero, so it was controlled both directions.
On $3x - \tfrac12 x^2$ it finds the one interior critical point at $x=3$; on $x^3-9x$ it finds one root of $f'$ inside $[0,6]$ and two inside $[-6,6]$; on $|x-2|$ the kink scan fires, and on $\sin x$ it does not.
**One finding worth carrying:** on an even grid the point $x=3$ is a node, so $f'$ is **exactly** zero there and the strict sign-change test skips it and reports 0.
On an odd grid it reports 1.
A sign-change census must therefore count exact grid zeros as well as strict changes, and be run at both parities, or grid parity silently decides your answer.

**The units check, which is the one that catches the missing division.**

| Quantity | Units | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the constant 3 | $^\circ\text{C}/\text{m}$ | it is a temperature gradient |
| $T(x) = 3x$ | $^\circ\text{C}$ | a temperature |
| $\int_0^6 3x\,dx$ | $^\circ\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$ | **not** a temperature |
| $\frac{1}{6}\int_0^6 3x\,dx$ | $^\circ\text{C}$ | a temperature |

An average temperature carries the units of temperature.
If your answer carries degrees times metres, you skipped the division, and no amount of re-checking the arithmetic will find it.
The gate was seeded: it rejects $^\circ\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$, $^\circ\text{C}/\text{m}$, $\text{m}/^\circ\text{C}$ and the inhomogeneous sum $T + x$ as candidate temperatures, and accepts the correct relation.
A conversion arm confirms the same rod measured in centimetres (gradient $0.03\,^\circ\text{C}/\text{cm}$ over $600$ cm) still averages 9, and that forgetting to rescale the gradient gives 900.

**The honest limit of the units gate, stated because it matters here.**
Dividing by $b$ instead of $b-a$ is **dimensionally correct**: both are lengths.
So the units gate cannot see that error, and on this stem neither can the band check.
Nothing cheap catches it.
What catches it is writing $b-a$ symbolically before substituting.

**Numbers worth memorising for this family.**

| Fact | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b x\,dx$ | $\frac{a+b}{2}$, the midpoint |
| $\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L kx\,dx$ | $\frac{kL}{2}$ |
| $\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L x^p\,dx$ | $\frac{L^p}{p+1}$ |
| average of $\sin$ over $[0,\pi]$ | $\frac{2}{\pi} = 0.6366197724$ |
| average of $\sin$ or $\cos$ over a full period | $0$ |
| average of $\sin^2$ or $\cos^2$ over a full period | $\frac12$ |

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as $N$ facts.
The recurring structure is: change $f$, change $[a,b]$, or change what is being solved for, and the formula never changes.

### Family 0 - the exam's own question, and its immediate rescalings

Trigger: a linear quantity over a straight object or a straight time window.
Tool: $\frac{1}{L}\int_0^L kx\,dx = \frac{kL}{2}$, or just "the midpoint value".

| Stem | $f_{\text{avg}}$ |
| :--- | ---: |
| $3x$ on $[0,6]$ (**the exam**) | $9$ |
| $3x$ on $[0,10]$ | $15$ |
| $5x$ on $[0,6]$ | $15$ |
| $3x$ on $[2,6]$ | $12$ |
| $3x$ on $[-6,6]$ | $0$ |
| $3x + 20$ on $[0,6]$ | $29$ |
| $18-3x$ on $[0,6]$ | $9$ |

There are only two ideas in that table.
The average of $kx$ on $[0,L]$ is $\frac{kL}{2}$, and adding a constant to $f$ adds the same constant to the average.
The last row is the **invariance check**: measuring $x$ from the other end of the same rod replaces $3x$ by $3(6-x)$, and the average is unchanged, as it must be, because the rod does not know which end you called zero.

### Family 1 - a general interval, where $b-a$ stops being equal to $b$

Trigger: $a \ne 0$.
Tool: write $b-a$, do not write $b$.
This is the family that exposes the divide-by-$b$ mistake, which is invisible on the exam's own stem.

| Stem | correct $\frac{1}{b-a}\int$ | the wrong $\frac{1}{b}\int$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $3x$ on $[0,6]$ | $9$ | $9$ |
| $3x$ on $[2,6]$ | $12$ | $8$ |
| $3x$ on $[1,4]$ | $7.5$ | $5.625$ |
| $3x$ on $[-3,3]$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| $3x$ on $[3,6]$ | $13.5$ | $6.75$ |

Row 4 is a second coincidence and a good warning: with a symmetric interval both give 0, for a different reason again.
A sweep over 501 left endpoints from 0 to 5 in steps of 0.01, with the width held at 6, found the two agree at **exactly one** value of $a$, namely $a=0$.
So the divide-by-$b$ error hides if and only if the interval starts at the origin.

### Family 2 - a curved $f$, where the endpoint mean and the midpoint value both die

Trigger: $f$ is not affine.
Tool: actually do the integral.

| Stem | $f_{\text{avg}}$ | endpoint mean | midpoint value |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $x^2$ on $[0,6]$ | $12$ | $18$ | $9$ |
| $x^2$ on $[0,3]$ | $3$ | $4.5$ | $2.25$ |
| $x^3$ on $[0,2]$ | $2$ | $4$ | $1$ |
| $\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,9]$ | $2$ | $1.5$ | $2.1213203436$ |
| $e^x$ on $[0,1]$ | $1.7182818285$ | $1.8591409142$ | $1.6487212707$ |
| $1/x$ on $[1,e]$ | $0.5819767069$ | $0.6839397206$ | $0.5378828427$ |

**Read the shape:** the endpoint mean is above the truth in rows 1, 2, 3 and 5 (convex) and below it in row 4 (concave), and the midpoint value goes the other way every time.
That is not a coincidence, it is Jensen's inequality, and it means the two wrong answers **bracket** the right one whenever $f$ is convex or concave throughout.
A sweep confirmed the endpoint mean is exact on all three affine test instances and fails on all five non-affine ones; the midpoint value fails on the same five.

### Family 3 - periodic $f$, where the average is often 0 or $\tfrac12$

Trigger: sine, cosine, or a square of one, over a whole number of periods.
Tool: symmetry beats integration.

| Stem | $f_{\text{avg}}$ |
| :--- | ---: |
| $\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$ | $0$ |
| $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ | $\frac{2}{\pi} = 0.6366197724$ |
| $\cos x$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ | $\frac{2}{\pi} = 0.6366197724$ |
| $\cos^2 x$ on $[0,2\pi]$ | $\frac12$ |
| $A\sin(\omega t)$ over a full period | $0$ |
| $A\sin(\omega t)$ over a half period | $\frac{2A}{\pi}$ |

The $\frac{2}{\pi}$ is worth knowing by sight; it is the standard "average of a rectified sine" and it appears in physics and electrical-engineering versions of this question.
Note that the average of $\sin$ over a full period is 0 while the average of $|\sin|$ is $\frac{2}{\pi}$, which is Family 8's distinction arriving early.

### Family 4 - the MVT point asked for directly

Trigger: "find the point at which $f$ attains its average value", or "find $c$ guaranteed by the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals".
Tool: compute $f_{\text{avg}}$, then solve $f(c) = f_{\text{avg}}$, then check $c \in [a,b]$.

| Stem | $f_{\text{avg}}$ | $c$ | is $c$ the midpoint? |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| $3x$ on $[0,6]$ | $9$ | $3$ | **yes**, because $f$ is affine |
| $x^2$ on $[0,6]$ | $12$ | $2\sqrt3 = 3.4641016151$ | no |
| $x^3$ on $[0,6]$ | $54$ | $6/4^{1/3} = 3.7797631497$ | no |
| $x^4$ on $[0,6]$ | $259.2$ | $6/5^{1/4} = 4.0124418299$ | no |
| $x^2$ on $[0,3]$ | $3$ | $\sqrt3 = 1.7320508076$ | no |
| $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$ | $\frac{2}{\pi}$ | $\arcsin\frac{2}{\pi} = 0.6901070914$ **and** $\pi - 0.6901070914$ | no, and it is not unique |

Two lessons.
First, the answer is a **point on the $x$-axis**, not a value of $f$; a very common slip is to write 9 again.
Second, uniqueness is not part of the theorem, and the last row shows why.

### Family 5 - solve backwards for a parameter (this is Q19.7)

Trigger: the average is given and something else is unknown.
Tool: set up the average symbolically and solve.

| Stem | equation | answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| average of $\frac{5}{x^2}$ between 1 and $k$ is 32 | $\frac{5}{k} = 32$ | $k = \frac{5}{32} = 0.15625$ |
| average of $3x$ on $[0,L]$ is 9 | $\frac{3L}{2} = 9$ | $L = 6$ |
| average of $kx$ on $[0,6]$ is 9 | $3k = 9$ | $k = 3$ |
| average of $3x$ on $[a, a+6]$ is 12 | $3(a+3) = 12$ | $a = 1$ |

**Q19.7's answer is less than 1**, so "the interval between 1 and $k$" runs from $\frac{5}{32}$ up to 1, not from 1 up to $k$.
That is why the stem says "between" rather than "from 1 to $k$", and it is the trap on that question.
Note that $\frac{1}{k-1}\int_1^k \frac{5}{x^2}dx$ and $\frac{1}{1-k}\int_k^1 \frac{5}{x^2}dx$ are **the same expression**, both equal to $\frac{5}{k}$, because reversing the limits flips the sign of both the integral and the width.
Verified at $k = 2$, $5$, $1.3$ and at $k = \frac{5}{32}$ read backwards.

### Family 6 - piecewise or composite objects, where the average is WEIGHTED

Trigger: two materials, two rates, two phases.
Tool: $f_{\text{avg}}$ over $[a,b]$ is the **width-weighted** mean of the sub-averages, not their plain mean.

$$f_{\text{avg}}[a,b] \;=\; \frac{(c-a)\,f_{\text{avg}}[a,c] \;+\; (b-c)\,f_{\text{avg}}[c,b]}{b-a}$$

| Stem | sub-averages | correct answer |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $3x$ on $[0,3]$ and $[3,6]$ | $4.5$ and $13.5$ | $9$ (equal widths, so the plain mean also works) |
| $3x$ on $[0,2]$ and $[2,6]$ | $3$ and $12$ | $9$ |
| $3x$ on $[0,5]$ and $[5,6]$ | $7.5$ and $16.5$ | $9$ |
| rod at $3x$ on $[0,3]$, held at $9$ on $[3,6]$ | $4.5$ and $9$ | $6.75$ |

The weighted law was verified at five split points; the **unweighted** mean of the two sub-averages fails at all four of the uneven splits and succeeds only at $c=3$.
That is the whole family in one sentence: **halves may be averaged, unequal pieces must be weighted.**

### Family 7 - average of a RATE, which is Q6.1's object in disguise

Trigger: $f$ is itself a rate of change of something.
Tool: the FTC collapses the two averages into one.

$$\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b s'(t)\,dt \;=\; \frac{s(b)-s(a)}{b-a}$$

| Stem | average of the rate | average rate of change |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $v(t) = t^2-3t+2$ on $[0,3]$ | $\frac{1}{3}\int_0^3 v = 0.5$ | $\frac{s(3)-s(0)}{3} = 0.5$ |
| $r(t) = 180-6t$ litres/min on $[0,15]$ | $135$ litres/min | $\frac{2025}{15} = 135$ |
| $P'(y)$ on $[2,15]$ | $\frac{1}{13}\int_2^{15}P' $ | $\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13} = 1431$ (Q6.1) |

**The contrast with Q6.1 is the point of this family and it needs stating precisely.**
Q6.1's object is $\frac{P(b)-P(a)}{b-a}$, a **difference quotient**: it needs only the two endpoint values of $P$ and it ignores everything in between.
Q19.6's object is $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$, an **integral mean**: it needs $f$ everywhere on the interval and it weights every point equally.
They are averages of **different things over different objects**: one averages the *rate* of $P$, the other averages the *values* of $f$.
They coincide exactly when the $f$ you are averaging is the derivative of the $P$ whose rate you are quoting.
Applying Q6.1's formula to this question gives $\frac{f(6)-f(0)}{6-0} = \frac{18}{6} = 3$, which is the temperature **gradient**, in $^\circ\text{C}/\text{m}$, not the average temperature.
That is census row 11, and 3 is one of the piled-up wrong values on this question.

See `notes\Q6.1 - Average and instantaneous rate of population change.md`, section 2.1, for the side-by-side table of average versus instantaneous rate; the object in the left column of that table is the one being contrasted here.

### Family 8 - average of $f$ versus average of $|f|$

Trigger: the quantity can be negative and the stem says "distance", "total", or "magnitude".
Tool: split at the sign changes.
This is Q19.8's whole content.

| Stem | average of $f$ | average of $\lvert f\rvert$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $3x$ on $[-6,6]$ | $0$ | $9$ |
| $\lvert x\rvert$ on $[-2,4]$ | $\frac{5}{3} = 1.6666666667$ | same |
| $v(t)=t^2-3t+2$ on $[0,3]$ | $0.5$ | $\frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{11}{6}$ |
| $\sin x$ on $[0,2\pi]$ | $0$ | $\frac{2}{\pi}$ |

Row 1 is a good exam trap: a rod running from $-6$ to $6$ with $T = 3x$ has average temperature 0 even though it is nowhere near $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ at most points.
An average of 0 does **not** mean "nothing is happening".

### Family 9 - the sandwich as the whole question (this is Q17.1)

Trigger: "find an interval containing", "estimate", "bound".
Tool: $m \le f_{\text{avg}} \le M$, or its multiplied-out form $m(b-a) \le \int \le M(b-a)$.

| Stem | $m$ | $M$ | bound on $\int$ | bound on the average |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $3x$ on $[0,6]$ | $0$ | $18$ | $[0, 108]$ | $[0,18]$ |
| $\sec x$ on $[0,\pi/3]$ (Q17.1) | $1$ | $2$ | $[\frac{\pi}{3}, \frac{2\pi}{3}]$ | $[1,2]$ |
| $x^2$ on $[1,3]$ | $1$ | $9$ | $[2,18]$ | $[1,9]$ |
| $e^{-x^2}$ on $[0,2]$ | $e^{-4}$ | $1$ | $[2e^{-4}, 2]$ | $[e^{-4},1]$ |

Q17.1 is item 9 on the same paper as this question and its answer is $\left[\tfrac{\pi}{3}, \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right]$ against a true value of $\ln(2+\sqrt3) = 1.3169578969$.
See `notes\Q17.1 - Interval containing a secant integral.md`, section 2, for the comparison property in the form used here.
The average-value version is that same property with $(b-a)$ divided out, which is why the sandwich on the average is free once you know $m$ and $M$.

### Family 10 - units and conversions

Trigger: the stem mixes units, or asks for the answer in a unit the data is not in.
Tool: convert **before** integrating, or convert the answer, but never half of each.

| Stem | trap | correct |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| Rod 600 cm, $T = 0.03s$ with $s$ in cm | using the $3$ from the metre version | $9\,^\circ\text{C}$ |
| Rod 6 m, $T=3x$, answer wanted in $^\circ\text{F}$ | averaging then converting is fine here | $\frac95(9)+32 = 48.2\,^\circ\text{F}$ |
| Rate in litres/min, window in hours | mixing the two | convert the window first |

Fahrenheit is a legitimate case where converting after averaging is safe, because the conversion is **affine** and an affine map commutes with averaging.
A nonlinear conversion would not, which is worth knowing: the average of $T^2$ is not the square of the average of $T$ (here $\frac16\int_0^6 9x^2dx = 108$, while $9^2 = 81$).

### Family 11 - the discrete-versus-continuous average

Trigger: the stem gives a table of values, or says "sampled every metre".
Tool: a finite mean of $n$ values is not the integral mean, except by accident.

| Stem | value | matches 9? |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| mean of $T$ at $x=0,1,2,3,4,5,6$ (7 samples) | $9$ | **yes, by accident** |
| mean of $T$ at $x=1,2,3,4,5,6$ (6 samples, right ends) | $10.5$ | no |
| mean of $T$ at $x=0,1,2,3,4,5$ (6 samples, left ends) | $7.5$ | no |
| mean of $T$ at the 6 midpoints $0.5,\dots,5.5$ | $9$ | yes, midpoint rule is exact on a line |

Row 1 is a genuine hazard and it is census row 21.
The mean of an arithmetic progression equals its middle term, so sampling a **linear** temperature at equally spaced points including both ends returns the true average exactly.
It is right for the wrong reason, and it breaks the moment $T$ curves: sampling $x^2$ at $0,1,\dots,6$ gives $\frac{91}{7}=13$, against a true average of 12.

### Family 12 - mass- or area-weighted averages

Trigger: "average temperature of the rod by mass", or a rod of varying cross-section.
Tool: a weighted average, $\dfrac{\int_a^b f(x)\,w(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}$.

| Stem | weight $w$ | answer |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| uniform rod, $T=3x$ on $[0,6]$ | $1$ | $9$ |
| rod with linear density $x$ | $x$ | $\frac{\int_0^6 3x\cdot x\,dx}{\int_0^6 x\,dx} = \frac{216}{18} = 12$ |
| rod with density $6-x$ | $6-x$ | $\frac{108}{18} = 6$ |

Note that the unweighted formula is the special case $w \equiv 1$, and the denominator is then exactly $b-a$.
**That is the cleanest possible explanation of where the $(b-a)$ comes from**: it is the total weight.
An average is always (total of the thing) divided by (total of the weight), and for a plain average the weight is length.

## 9. The trap list

### 9.0 The wrong-answer census, in full

Twenty-four methods were computed on this stem: the correct one and twenty-three plausible wrong ones.
No candidate was excluded as too silly, because the point of a census is to find out which errors are **invisible**, and an error that looks silly can still be the one that hides.

| # | The method | Value | Verdict |
| ---: | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| 0 | $\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$ | $9$ | **correct** |
| 1 | forgot to divide by $(b-a)$ | $54$ | caught |
| 2 | averaged the two endpoint values | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 3 | divided by $b$ instead of $(b-a)$ | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 4 | used the midpoint value $f\!\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)$ | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 5 | averaged the $x$-values, not $f$ | $3$ | caught |
| 6 | antiderivative $3x^2$, dropped the $\tfrac12$ | $18$ | caught |
| 7 | integrated the constant $3$, not $3x$ | $3$ | caught |
| 8 | $F(b)$ with $F(a)$ never subtracted | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 9 | took the right endpoint value $f(b)$ | $18$ | caught |
| 10 | took the left endpoint value $f(a)$ | $0$ | caught |
| 11 | average **rate** of change $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ | $3$ | caught |
| 12 | divided by $(b-a)$ twice | $1.5$ | caught |
| 13 | multiplied by $(b-a)$ | $324$ | caught |
| 14 | reversed the limits of integration | $-9$ | caught |
| 15 | integrated over half the rod, divided by half | $4.5$ | caught |
| 16 | integrated over half the rod, divided by 6 | $2.25$ | caught |
| 17 | trapezoid with one panel, then divided | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 18 | Simpson with two panels, then divided | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 19 | averaged $m$ and $M$ | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 20 | divided by the number 3, the slope | $18$ | caught |
| 21 | mean of $f(0), f(1), \dots, f(6)$, seven samples | $9$ | **HIDES** |
| 22 | differentiated instead of integrating | $3$ | caught |
| 23 | used the rod length as the average | $6$ | caught |

**Eight of the twenty-three wrong methods return exactly 9.**
That is far and away the worst hiding rate in this category: Q19.4 had one hider out of seventeen, Q19.5 had one out of twenty.

**The collisions, derived from the value multiset and cross-checked by an independent pairwise sweep.**
Three values are reached more than once: **9 by nine candidates**, **3 by four**, **18 by three**.
So **16 of the 24 candidates sit on a value some other candidate also reaches**, and the biggest pile-up of all is on the correct answer.
The four candidates on 3 are especially worth noticing: the mean of the $x$-values, the integral of the constant 3, the average rate of change, and the derivative all land on the temperature **gradient**, which is a different physical quantity wearing the same number.

**What the cheap repairs buy, measured.**

| Repair | Rejects | Of 23 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| "an average temperature cannot be negative" | $1$ (row 14) | $4.3$ percent |
| "the average must be strictly positive" | $2$ (rows 10 and 14) | over-rejects, see below |
| the $[0,18]$ sandwich | $3$ (rows 1, 13, 14) | $13.0$ percent |

The sandwich is a **strict superset** of the non-negativity check, so it is the one to reach for.
Strict positivity is not a superset and is not safe: it rejects row 10's value of $0$, and $0$ is a **legal** value of this $f$, so "the average is positive" over-rejects.
This is the third instance in category 19 of Q19.4's pattern, **positivity near-inert, bracket helps**, and it does not follow Q19.1 or Q19.3 (where the sign repair rescues) or Q19.2 (where it harms).
What is new is that the bracket's reach **collapses** here: it rejects 3 of 23 against Q19.5's 14 of 20, and it cannot touch a single one of the eight hiders, because every one of them equals the truth.

**The conclusion, which is the reason this section exists.**
On this question no check that looks only at the **answer** can help you, because the wrong answers are the right answer.
The only discriminator is the **method**: did you write $(b-a)$, and did you divide.

### 9.1 Ranked traps

Ranked by how often each actually costs a mark on a paper like this one.

**1. Forgetting to divide by $(b-a)$, and writing 54.**
The most common error on every average-value question ever set.
It is the only one of the four named errors this stem **can** catch, and it is caught twice over, by the units and by the band.

**2. Averaging the endpoint values, and writing 9.**
This is right here and wrong almost everywhere else.
If you got 9 this way you have learned nothing, and Family 2 will end you.

**3. Dividing by $b$ instead of $b-a$, and writing 9.**
Also right here, because $a = 0$.
Nothing cheap catches this.
Write $b-a$ symbolically.

**4. Using the midpoint value, and writing 9.**
Right here because $f$ is affine.
The fix is Route D's health warning: the shortcut needs the affineness stated.

**5. Reporting the MVT point instead of the average, or the reverse.**
On this question both are the number 3 or the number 9 depending on which you mean, and $c = 3$ while $f_{\text{avg}} = 9$.
Read the last sentence of the stem again before typing.

**6. Confusing the average value with the average rate of change.**
Gives 3 instead of 9, and 3 is a plausible-looking number.
Family 7 is the fix.

**7. Dropping the $\tfrac12$ in the antiderivative of $3x$.**
$\int 3x\,dx = \tfrac32 x^2$, not $3x^2$.
Gives 18, which is also $f(6)$, so it looks like a defensible answer.

**8. Averaging $m$ and $M$.**
Gives 9 here, because $f$ is affine and monotone.
It is the same mistake as trap 2 wearing a different hat.

**9. Reversing the limits.**
Gives $-9$.
The one error that non-negativity catches.

**10. Reporting the average of $T^2$ or some other nonlinear function of $T$.**
Averaging does not commute with squaring: $108 \ne 81$.

## 10. Drill set

Score **magnitude** and **units** separately.
A magnitude error means you mis-integrated; a units error means you mis-divided, and those need different remediation.

1. Find the average value of $f(x) = 4x$ on $[0,5]$.
2. Find the average value of $f(x) = 3x$ on $[2,6]$. Then compute the same thing dividing by $b$ instead of $b-a$, and note the gap.
3. Find the average value of $f(x) = x^2$ on $[0,3]$, and the point $c$ where it is attained.
4. Find the average value of $\sin x$ on $[0,\pi]$.
5. Find the average value of $1/x$ on $[1,4]$.
6. A rod 10 m long has temperature $2x+5$ at distance $x$. Find its average temperature.
7. The rod of Q19.6, with $x$ measured from the **other** end, has $T = 18-3x$. Find the average temperature and explain why it must match.
8. A particle has velocity $v(t) = t^2-3t+2$ m/s. Find its average velocity on $[0,3]$.
9. Find the average value of $e^x$ on $[0,\ln 2]$.
10. Find the average value of $\lvert x\rvert$ on $[-1,3]$.
11. Find the average value of $\cos x$ on $[0,\pi/2]$.
12. Find a positive $k$ so that the average value of $\frac{5}{x^2}$ between 1 and $k$ is 32. State the interval you actually integrated over.

### Answer key

| # | exact | decimal | family and method |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| 1 | $10$ | $10.0000000000$ | Family 0; $\frac{kL}{2}$ with $k=4$, $L=5$ |
| 2 | $12$; the wrong version gives $8$ | $12.0000000000$ / $8.0000000000$ | Family 1; the divide-by-$b$ error stops hiding as soon as $a\ne0$ |
| 3 | $3$, attained at $c=\sqrt3$ | $3.0000000000$ / $1.7320508076$ | Family 4; $\frac{L^p}{p+1}$ then solve $c^2=3$; $c$ is **not** the midpoint $1.5$ |
| 4 | $\dfrac{2}{\pi}$ | $0.6366197724$ | Family 3; $\frac{1}{\pi}[-\cos x]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi}$ |
| 5 | $\dfrac{\ln 4}{3} = \dfrac{2\ln 2}{3}$ | $0.4620981204$ | Family 2; $\frac{1}{3}[\ln x]_1^4$ |
| 6 | $15$ | $15.0000000000$ | Family 0; affine, so the midpoint value $2(5)+5$ |
| 7 | $9$ | $9.0000000000$ | Family 0 invariance; the rod does not know which end is $x=0$ |
| 8 | $\dfrac12$ | $0.5000000000$ | Family 7; equals $\frac{s(3)-s(0)}{3}$, and note this is displacement, not distance |
| 9 | $\dfrac{1}{\ln 2}$ | $1.4426950409$ | Family 2; $\frac{1}{\ln 2}[e^x]_0^{\ln 2} = \frac{2-1}{\ln 2}$ |
| 10 | $\dfrac54$ | $1.2500000000$ | Family 8; split at $x=0$, areas $\frac12$ and $\frac92$, total $5$, width $4$ |
| 11 | $\dfrac{2}{\pi}$ | $0.6366197724$ | Family 3; same value as #4, different interval, and worth noticing |

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
Stem names an average.  Of WHAT?

+-- of the VALUES of f over an interval
|     |
|     +-- f affine?          -> midpoint value works, but SAY SO
|     +-- f curved?          -> (1/(b-a)) INT f, no shortcut
|     +-- f piecewise?       -> width-WEIGHTED mean of the sub-averages
|     +-- a weight w given?  -> (INT f w) / (INT w)
|
+-- of the RATE of change of something (Q6.1)
|     -> ( F(b) - F(a) ) / (b-a),  endpoints only, no integral
|
+-- of a rate, over time (Q19.3, Q19.8)
      -> the two coincide by the FTC; either route is fine

Then, ALWAYS, in this order:
  1. is the answer in [m, M]?              <- free, and it catches the missing division
  2. do the units match the units of f?    <- catches the missing division again
  3. did I divide by (b - a) or by b?      <- NOTHING catches this; check it by hand
  4. was the question asking for the average, or for the POINT c?
  5. preview the answer box
```
