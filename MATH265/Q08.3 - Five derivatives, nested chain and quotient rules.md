> You may not need to simplify your answers.

> c. $y = \sqrt{1 + \sqrt{1 + x}}$

So the new content is not mathematics, it is **bookkeeping**, and it is three specific ideas.

1. **The layer ledger.**
   The failure mode in this family is not choosing the wrong rule, it is losing a factor while assembling a product of three or four of them.
   Section 3 replaces "do the chain rule" with a written ledger: number the layers, write one line per layer, and the answer is the product down the ledger.
   The ledger also gives you a **count**, and the count is the cheapest possible check.
2. **A rule can live inside a rule, and the inner one needs its own line.**
   Part (b) is a quotient inside a chain, part (e) is a quotient inside a named function inside a power, part (d) is a chain inside a quotient, and part (a) is a three-level chain inside a product.
   The last-operation test handles all four without change.
   What changes is that the sub-derivation is itself a multi-step job, so trying to hold it in your head is where the factor goes missing.
   Section 4 shows the write-up that keeps a marker with you.
3. **How to see a dropped inner factor, which is the single most common error here.**
   A derivative with one factor missing is still a plausible-looking expression: right shape, right functions, right arguments, wrong answer.
   Section 6 gives three detectors, and the important one is not obvious: **a dropped factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1**, so a numeric check at a badly chosen point cannot find it.
   That is a fact about checking, and this document's own verification is built around it.

      layer 1  (outermost)  ->  its derivative, inner left UNTOUCHED
      layer 2               ->  its derivative
      layer 3  (innermost)  ->  its derivative

   COUNT.  n layers means n factors.  Look at your answer and count them.

   a.  product at the top;  its 2nd factor is a 3-layer chain

## 2. The four things you must have cold

Q8.1 section 2 has the five rules and the six trig derivatives, and Q8.2 section 2.2 has the superscript-position distinction.
This document assumes all of that.
These four are the additions Q8.3 specifically needs.

### 2.1 The layer ledger, which is a notation and not a rule

This is the whole of section 3 in one picture, and it is worth having as a habit before you need it.

For a composite $f(g(h(x)))$ the chain rule is

$$\frac{d}{dx}f\big(g(h(x))\big) = f'\big(g(h(x))\big)\cdot g'\big(h(x)\big)\cdot h'(x)$$

which is three factors, one per layer, each one **evaluated at everything below it, untouched**.
Written as a formula that is easy to misread and easy to truncate.
Written as a ledger it is neither.

```
   y = sqrt( 1 + sqrt(1 + x) )

   layer   what it is              its derivative
   -----   --------------------    -------------------------------
     1     sqrt( ___ )             1 / (2 sqrt(1 + sqrt(1+x)))
     2     1 + sqrt( ___ )         1 / (2 sqrt(1+x))
     3     1 + x                   1

   y' = product of the third column  =  1 / (4 sqrt(1+sqrt(1+x)) sqrt(1+x))
```

Three things this buys you, and all three matter under time pressure.
The **count** is visible, so three layers must give three factors.
The **arguments** are visible, so you can see at a glance that layer 1's derivative still contains the whole of layers 2 and 3.
And the **rule names** are already on the page in the order a marker wants them, which is precisely what Q8.4 asks for out loud.

### 2.2 The derivative of a linear over a linear, in one line

$$\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{ax+b}{cx+d} = \frac{ad-bc}{(cx+d)^2}$$

The numerator is a **constant**, always, because $u'v - uv'$ is degree $\le 1$ and the $x$ terms always cancel.
Q8.1 noted this as a free check in its Family 2; here it is a formula worth memorising outright, because part (e)'s innermost layer is exactly this and getting it in one line saves a whole quotient rule.

For $\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}$: $a=1$, $b=1$, $c=1$, $d=-2$, so $ad-bc = -2 - 1 = -3$ and

$$v' = \frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$$

That formula was verified over **77 coefficient sets** at four points each, not at one instance, so it is a family fact and not a lucky coincidence.
The sign is the trap: $ad - bc$, in that order, and for part (e) both terms are negative contributions so the constant is $-3$ and not $+3$.

### 2.3 The two collapses that happen every time

Both of these turn a messy line into a clean one, and both happen so reliably that not seeing them costs you time rather than marks.

**A square root's $\frac12$ eats the inner derivative's 2.**

$$\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x^2-1} = \frac12\left(x^2-1\right)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$$

Q8.1 recorded this in its Family 5, and part (d)'s numerator is it.
Whenever the outer power is $\frac12$ and the inner is a quadratic, the halves cancel and no coefficient survives.

**Each nested square root contributes exactly one factor of 2, so $n$ roots give $2^n$.**

| the function | the coefficient | why |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $\sqrt{1+x}$ | 2 | one root |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}$ | 4 | two roots, $2\times 2$ |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}}$ | 8 | three roots, $2\times2\times2$ |

The 4 in part (c) is not a number to memorise, it is a count.
Verified at all three depths.

### 2.4 What a fractional power leaves behind

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[g(x)\right]^{p/q} = \frac{p}{q}\left[g(x)\right]^{p/q-1}g'(x)$$

For part (b), $p/q = \frac13$ and $\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23$, so the surviving power is $w^{-2/3}$ and **not** $w^{-1/3}$.
Subtracting 1 from a fraction is where this goes wrong, and $\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23$ is worth saying out loud rather than computing.

One nuance about part (b) that is worth having straight, because it looks like a domain problem and is not.
A **real** cube root accepts negative inputs, so $y = \left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}$ is a perfectly good real function wherever the base is defined, which is everywhere except $x = \pm1$.
The base is negative on exactly one interval, $x > 1$, where the denominator has turned negative and the numerator has not.
The derivative formula is valid there too, verified at seven points with $x > 1$: $w^{-2/3}$ is the reciprocal of the square of the real cube root, so it is positive whatever the sign of $w$.
So there is no domain caveat to write on the exam.

## 3. The universal procedure

Q8.1's last-operation test is unchanged and it is still step 1.
This is the wrapper that makes it survive three levels.

> **Step 1 - Ask what the LAST operation is, exactly as in Q8.1.**
> Imagine substituting a number and doing the arithmetic by hand.
> Whatever you would do last is at the top of the expression and it names the rule.
>
> **Step 2 - Write the rule down with blanks, before you fill anything in.**
> Also unchanged.
> For a quotient, write $\dfrac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ on the page first, then name $u$ and $v$.
>
> **Step 3 - Build the LEDGER, not the answer. This is the step Q8.3 adds.**
> Number the layers from the outside in.
> Give each one its own line, and on that line write only *its* derivative, with everything below it left completely untouched.
> Stop when a layer is a polynomial, a bare $x$, or a constant.
>
> **Step 4 - Any layer that needs a rule of its own gets its own SEPARATE ledger.**
> Part (b)'s layer 2 is a quotient, so it is a three-line quotient-rule derivation on its own lines, and only its *result* goes back into the main ledger.
> Do not try to inline it.
> This is the single most useful piece of exam hygiene in this question, because it is what turns four factors held in your head into four factors written on the page.
>
> **Step 5 - Assemble by multiplying down the right-hand column, then COUNT.**
> $n$ layers must produce $n$ factors.
> If you have fewer factors than layers, you have dropped one, and the missing one is almost always the innermost.
>
> **Step 6 - Simplify only if a fraction is stacked inside a fraction, or a factor cancels.**
> Q8.1's policy, unchanged.
> On this question that fires exactly once, on part (d).
>
> **Step 7 - Check with a centred difference at $h = 0.001$, at a point where no layer's factor is near 1.**
> Section 6, and the second half of that sentence is the part Q8.1 and Q8.2 did not need.

**The tree for part (e), which is the deepest one.**

```
                y = sec^2( (x+1)/(x-2) )
                            |
              LAST operation is the SQUARING
                            |
                  >>> CHAIN RULE, power outer <<<
                     2 sec(v) * d/dx sec(v)
                            |
              LAST operation of the inside is APPLYING sec
                            |
                  >>> CHAIN RULE, named outer <<<
                     sec(v) tan(v) * d/dx v
                            |
              LAST operation of THAT inside is a DIVISION
                            |
                  >>> QUOTIENT RULE <<<
                     ((x-2) - (x+1)) / (x-2)^2  =  -3/(x-2)^2
                            |
              LEDGER:  2 sec(v)  x  sec(v) tan(v)  x  -3/(x-2)^2
                            |
              y' = -6 sec^2(v) tan(v) / (x-2)^2
```

Two things to take from that picture.
The rule name **changes at every level**, from power to named to quotient, and the only way to know that is to ask the last-operation question again at each level instead of assuming the answer you got at the top.
And the two $\sec$ factors multiply into $\sec^2$, which is why the answer looks like the function you started with.
Q8.2 met the same coincidence in its part (a), and it is worth expecting so that it does not read as a mistake.

## 4. Q8.3 worked, part by part

### Part (a) - $y = \sin x\cdot\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$

**Read the notation first.**
$\sin x^2$ means $\sin\left(x^2\right)$: the exponent is on the *argument*, so the squaring happens before the sine.
$\sin^2 x$ would be $(\sin x)^2$, with the exponent on the *function name*, and that is a different function.
Both the question bank and the course's own review notes read this as $\sin\left(x^2\right)$, and the two readings really are different functions: at $x = 1$ they give derivatives $-0.3180$ and $-0.0872$.
Read the superscript's position, exactly as Q8.2 section 2.2 says.

Last operation: a **multiplication** of two $x$-dependent factors, so the product rule.
That is the one rule Q8.1 never tested, and here it is at the top of the hardest part.

$$u = \sin x,\qquad u' = \cos x, \qquad v = \cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$$

Now $v$ gets its own ledger, because it is three layers deep.

```
   v = cos( sin( x^2 ) )

   layer   what it is         its derivative
   -----   ----------------   ---------------------------
     1     cos( ___ )         -sin( sin(x^2) )
     2     sin( ___ )         cos( x^2 )
     3     x^2                2x

   v' = -2x cos(x^2) sin( sin(x^2) )        <- three factors, three layers
```

Assemble with $u'v + uv'$, which is symmetric so there is no order to get wrong:

$$y' = \cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) + \sin x\cdot\left[-2x\cos\left(x^2\right)\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\right]$$

$$\boxed{y' = \cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)}$$

**Nothing simplifies and nothing should be attempted.**
The second term has four factors and every one of them is doing work.
The minus sign in front of it came from layer 1 and from nowhere else, so if you cannot say which layer produced a sign you have not finished the ledger.

**Check.**
$y'(0) = 1$ exactly, because at $x=0$ the second term carries a factor of $2x$ and vanishes, while the first is $\cos 0\cdot\cos(\sin 0) = 1$.
That is a good three-second check and it is *blind to the second term*, so it is not enough on its own.
At $x=1$ the two terms are $0.36004$ and $0.67799$, giving $y'(1) = -0.31795$, and the calculator's difference quotient gives $-0.317952$.

### Part (b) - $y = \left(\dfrac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}$

Last operation: raise a bracket to the constant power $\frac13$.
So the chain rule with a power outer, and the inner is a quotient.

Only two layers, but layer 2 is a full quotient rule, so it gets its own ledger.

```
   w = (1 + x^3) / (1 - x^2)          quotient rule, u'v - uv' over v^2

       u = 1 + x^3      u' = 3x^2
       v = 1 - x^2      v' = -2x

       w' = [ 3x^2 (1-x^2)  -  (1+x^3)(-2x) ] / (1-x^2)^2
          = [ 3x^2 - 3x^4 + 2x + 2x^4 ] / (1-x^2)^2
          = ( 2x + 3x^2 - x^4 ) / (1-x^2)^2
```

Watch the double negative on the second numerator term: $-(1+x^3)\cdot(-2x) = +2x + 2x^4$, and writing it as $-2x - 2x^4$ is the plausible wrong answer.
Then the main ledger is two lines:

```
   layer   what it is        its derivative
   -----   ---------------   ------------------------------------
     1     ( ___ )^(1/3)     (1/3) ( (1+x^3)/(1-x^2) )^(-2/3)
     2     the quotient      (2x + 3x^2 - x^4) / (1-x^2)^2
```

$$\boxed{y' = \frac13\left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{-2/3}\cdot\frac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{\left(1-x^2\right)^2}}$$

**Stop there.**
The exponent is $\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23$, the base inside the bracket is completely untouched, and combining the two fractions gains nothing.

#### Route B - split the power over the quotient, then use the product rule

$$y = \left(1+x^3\right)^{1/3}\left(1-x^2\right)^{-1/3}$$

$$y' = x^2\left(1+x^3\right)^{-2/3}\left(1-x^2\right)^{-1/3} + \frac{2x}{3}\left(1+x^3\right)^{1/3}\left(1-x^2\right)^{-4/3}$$

Verified identical to Route A over twelve points.
Note the first term's coefficient: $\frac13\cdot 3x^2 = x^2$, so the 3s cancel.
This route avoids the quotient rule entirely and is genuinely competitive, but it produces two terms instead of one and a $-\frac43$ exponent that is easy to write as $-\frac13$.

#### Route C - logarithmic differentiation

$$\ln y = \frac13\left[\ln\left(1+x^3\right) - \ln\left(1-x^2\right)\right] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{y'}{y} = \frac13\left[\frac{3x^2}{1+x^3} + \frac{2x}{1-x^2}\right]$$

$$y' = \frac13\left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}\left[\frac{3x^2}{1+x^3} + \frac{2x}{1-x^2}\right]$$

Verified identical to Route A.
It is the shortest of the three and it is the wrong one to use on this paper, for a reason that has nothing to do with the mathematics: it needs $\ln$, and it needs the base to be positive so that $\ln y$ exists, which fails on $x>1$ where the base is negative.
Keep it for a later course.

#### The route comparison

| route | rules needed | terms in the answer | where it goes wrong | verdict |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| **A, chain then quotient** | chain, quotient | 1 | the exponent $-\frac23$; the double negative in $w'$ | **use this one** |
| B, split then product | chain, product, power | 2 | the $-\frac43$ exponent | good cross-check |
| C, logarithmic | log differentiation | 1 | needs $\ln$ and a positive base | out of scope, and it breaks on $x>1$ |

**Under exam pressure, use Route A.**
It is the route the question is testing, it produces one term rather than two, and the printed permission means you never have to touch it again once it is written.

**Check.**
$x = \frac12$ is the point to rehearse this part at, because everything comes out exact: $w\left(\frac12\right) = \frac32$, $w'\left(\frac12\right) = 3$, so
$$y'\left(\tfrac12\right) = \tfrac13\cdot\left(\tfrac32\right)^{-2/3}\cdot 3 = \left(\tfrac23\right)^{2/3} \approx 0.7631428$$
and $y\left(\frac12\right) = \left(\frac32\right)^{1/3} \approx 1.1447142$.
The calculator's difference quotient gives $0.7631448$.
Also $y'(0) = 0$, because $w'(0) = 0$ while $w(0) = 1$.

### Part (c) - $y = \sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}$

Last operation: apply a square root.
So the chain rule, three times, and this is the cleanest illustration of the ledger in the whole question.

Rewrite it as powers if that helps you see the layers: $y = \left[1 + (1+x)^{1/2}\right]^{1/2}$.

```
   layer   what it is           its derivative
   -----   ------------------   ---------------------------------
     1     sqrt( ___ )          1 / ( 2 sqrt(1 + sqrt(1+x)) )
     2     1 + sqrt( ___ )      1 / ( 2 sqrt(1+x) )
     3     1 + x                1
```

$$y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}}\cdot 1 = \boxed{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\,\sqrt{1+x}}}$$

The raw two-half-powers form $\frac12\left[1+\sqrt{1+x}\right]^{-1/2}\cdot\frac12(1+x)^{-1/2}$ is the same expression and earns the same marks, verified as an identity.
Combining the two $\frac12$s into a 4 is one step and it is worth doing because it makes the answer readable.

**The 4 is a count, not a number.**
It is $2\times2$, one 2 from each square root, and layer 3 contributes nothing because the derivative of $1+x$ is 1.
This is the one place on the whole paper where the innermost layer is invisible in the answer, which is exactly why part (c) breeds the habit of forgetting there is an innermost layer at all.

#### Route B - implicit differentiation, which is one line

Square both sides: $y^2 = 1 + \sqrt{1+x}$.
Differentiate:

$$2y\,y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y' = \frac{1}{4y\sqrt{1+x}}$$

and $y$ is the original function, so that *is* the answer, with no new expression introduced.
Verified identical.
This is genuinely faster and genuinely worth knowing, and it generalises: for any $y = \sqrt{\text{stuff}}$, $y' = \frac{(\text{stuff})'}{2y}$.
It is a cross-check rather than the route to write, because a marker wants to see the chain rule named on a chain-rule question.

**Check.**
$x = 8$ is the rehearsal point, because every root closes: $1+x = 9$, $\sqrt9 = 3$, $1+3 = 4$, $\sqrt4 = 2$, so $y(8) = 2$ and
$$y'(8) = \frac{1}{4\cdot 2\cdot 3} = \frac{1}{24}$$
exactly.
If you can produce $2$ and $\frac1{24}$ from memory you have the whole part.
At $x=3$: $y = \sqrt3$ and $y' = \frac{1}{4\sqrt3\cdot 2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{24} \approx 0.0721688$, matching the difference quotient to nine figures.
At $x=0$: $y' = \frac{1}{4\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt2}{8} \approx 0.1767767$.

Two structural facts worth carrying, both verified: $y'$ is positive everywhere on the domain and strictly **decreasing**, so a negative value or a rising one means a sign or a reciprocal has flipped.

### Part (d) - $y = \dfrac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{x^2-2x-8}$

Last operation: a division.
So the quotient rule, and the numerator needs the chain rule inside it.

$$u = \sqrt{x^2-1},\qquad u' = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}, \qquad v = x^2-2x-8,\qquad v' = 2x-2$$

$u'$ is section 2.3's collapse: $\frac12\left(x^2-1\right)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x$, and the halves cancel.
**Writing $u' = 2x$ is trap number one on this part**, and it is Q8.1's trap 1 wearing a different hat.

$$y' = \frac{\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right) - \sqrt{x^2-1}\,(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$$

**That line is the answer and it is worth full marks.**
Now the one simplification on this whole question that is worth doing, and the reason it is worth doing is not tidiness: the answer as written has a fraction inside a fraction, and that is genuinely hard to read and hard to use downstream.
Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x^2-1}$:

$$y' = \frac{x\left(x^2-2x-8\right) - \left(x^2-1\right)(2x-2)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$$

Expand the numerator carefully, because this is where the marks go:

$$x^3-2x^2-8x - \left(2x^3-2x^2-2x+2\right) = x^3-2x^2-8x-2x^3+2x^2+2x-2 = -x^3-6x-2$$

$$\boxed{y' = \frac{-\left(x^3+6x+2\right)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}}$$

The $x^2$ terms cancel exactly, which is a free check on the expansion.
This tidy form was verified as an identity against the raw one over thirteen points, not at one value.

**Two facts about the shape that are worth knowing and that the course notes miss.**

The denominator factors as $x^2-2x-8 = (x-4)(x+2)$, which the review file points out, so $y$ has poles at $x=4$ and $x=-2$.
Both of those sit inside $|x|\ge1$, so they are genuine poles and not domain edges, and the domain is four pieces: $(-\infty,-2)$, $(-2,-1]$, $[1,4)$ and $(4,\infty)$.
Verified by sweeping 20001 points and by confirming $|y|$ diverges from both sides of both poles.

And the tidy numerator $x^3+6x+2$ has **exactly one real root**, at $x \approx -0.32748$, located by a sign sweep rather than assumed.
That root lies inside $(-1,1)$, where $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ is not real, so it is not in the domain.
**So $y'$ never vanishes and $y$ has no critical point at all.**
That is a genuinely useful thing to know if a later part of a paper asks for extrema, and you can only see it once you have done the simplification.

#### Route B - the product rule on a negative power

$$y = \left(x^2-1\right)^{1/2}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^{-1}$$

$$y' = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^{-1} - \sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^{-2}(2x-2)$$

Verified identical.
Same work, written with exponents instead of a fraction bar, and the cancellation is slightly more visible.
Use whichever you write faster; they are the same three sub-derivatives.

**Check.**
$x=3$ is the rehearsal point.
$x^2-1 = 8$ so $\sqrt{\ } = 2\sqrt2$; $x^2-2x-8 = -5$; the tidy numerator is $27+18+2 = 47$.
$$y(3) = \frac{2\sqrt2}{-5} = -\frac{2\sqrt2}{5} \approx -0.5656854, \qquad y'(3) = \frac{-47}{2\sqrt2\cdot 25} = -\frac{47\sqrt2}{100} \approx -0.6646804$$
The calculator's difference quotient gives $-0.664681$.

### Part (e) - $y = \sec^2\left(\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}\right)$

Last operation: the **squaring**.
$\sec^2 u$ means $(\sec u)^2$, so the outer function is the power and not the secant.
That is Q8.2 section 2.2's distinction and it is the whole rule choice here.

Three layers, and each one a different rule.
Write $v = \dfrac{x+1}{x-2}$ throughout so the page stays readable.

```
   layer   what it is        rule            its derivative
   -----   ---------------   -------------   ------------------------
     1     ( ___ )^2         chain, power    2 sec(v)
     2     sec( ___ )        chain, named    sec(v) tan(v)
     3     (x+1)/(x-2)       quotient        -3 / (x-2)^2
```

Layer 3 by section 2.2's one-liner, or the long way:

$$v' = \frac{(x-2)\cdot 1 - (x+1)\cdot 1}{(x-2)^2} = \frac{x-2-x-1}{(x-2)^2} = \frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$$

Multiply down the column:

$$y' = 2\sec v\cdot\sec v\tan v\cdot\frac{-3}{(x-2)^2} = \boxed{\frac{-6}{(x-2)^2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)}$$

**Where the $-6$ comes from is worth naming, because the wrong coefficient is the commonest wrong answer here.**
It is $2\times(-3)$: the 2 from the power rule on the square, and the $-3$ from the quotient rule on the innermost layer.
Two sources, and missing either one gives you $-3$ or $+2$ and an answer that looks entirely reasonable.

#### Route B - write it as a negative power of cosine

$$y = (\cos v)^{-2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = -2(\cos v)^{-3}\cdot(-\sin v)\cdot v' = \frac{2\sin v}{\cos^3 v}\cdot v'$$

Verified identical.
This route needs no secant derivative at all, only $\frac{d}{dx}\cos = -\sin$ and the power rule, so it is the one to switch to if $\frac{d}{dx}\sec = \sec\tan$ is the fact you are least sure of.
Note that the two minus signs cancel, which is why the final sign is entirely $v'$'s.

#### Route C - the Pythagorean rewrite

$\sec^2 v = 1 + \tan^2 v$, so

$$y' = 0 + 2\tan v\cdot\sec^2 v\cdot v'$$

Verified identical, and identical in *form* as well as value, which is the point: the two routes produce literally the same expression, so the rewrite buys nothing here.
Q8.2 established this rewrite as a genuine labour-saver for a second derivative; on a first derivative of $\sec^2$ it is a wash.
Knowing that it is a wash is worth as much as knowing the rewrite.

#### The route comparison

| route | trig derivatives needed | lines | where it goes wrong | verdict |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| **A, power then sec then quotient** | $\sec' = \sec\tan$ | 3 | the coefficient $-6$; the sign of $v'$ | **use this one** |
| B, $(\cos v)^{-2}$ | $\cos' = -\sin$ only | 3 | the $-3$ exponent, and the double negative | switch here if $\sec'$ is shaky |
| C, $1+\tan^2 v$ | $\tan' = \sec^2$ | 3 | nothing new | same expression; no saving |

**Check.**
$x=5$ is the rehearsal point, because $v(5) = \frac{6}{3} = 2$ exactly.
$$y(5) = \sec^2 2 \approx 5.7743992, \qquad y'(5) = -\frac{6}{9}\sec^2 2\tan 2 = -\frac23\sec^2 2\tan 2 \approx 8.4115283$$
The answer is **positive**, because $\tan 2$ is negative, and that surprises people who read the $-6$ and expect a negative number.
The calculator's difference quotient gives $8.4115328$.
Also $y'(-1) = 0$, because $v(-1) = 0$ and $\tan 0 = 0$.

A structural fact worth carrying, and it was checked rather than reasoned about: on the sample range every **zero** of $y'$ is a zero of $\tan v$, and every **pole** of $y'$ is a pole of $\sec$ where $\cos v = 0$.
The $\frac{-6}{(x-2)^2}$ factor never vanishes, so it never contributes a zero.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up, and how much simplification a marker expects

### The policy, which is Q8.1's policy with one clause sharpened

Q8.1 states it: simplifying has zero marginal reward and non-zero marginal risk, so the default is to stop the moment the derivative is written.
Q8.2 added the case where $f'$ is an *input* to another differentiation, where the reward stops being zero.
Q8.3 sharpens the remaining exception, and it is narrower than "when a factor cancels".

> **Simplify when the raw answer contains a fraction inside a fraction.**
> Not because it is untidy, but because a stacked fraction is the one form a reader cannot check and a later question cannot use.

That fires exactly once on this question, on part (d), where the quotient rule puts $\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$ inside a numerator.
Multiplying through by $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ is three lines and it turns an unreadable answer into $\frac{-(x^3+6x+2)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}(x^2-2x-8)^2}$.

| part | simplify? | why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | **no** | four factors, none of them combinable; distributing makes it longer |
| b | **no** | combining $w^{-2/3}$ with $\frac{w'}{(1-x^2)^2}$ produces a worse expression, not a better one |
| c | **yes, one step** | fold the two $\frac12$s into a 4; that is it |
| d | **yes, three lines** | it is the stacked-fraction case, and the $x^2$ terms cancel as a free check |
| e | **no** | the two $\sec$ factors already combined by themselves |

**The one number that settles part (d).**
The raw form has a three-level fraction and a numerator you cannot read the zeros off.
The tidy form has a cubic on top and it tells you immediately that $y'$ has one real zero and that it is outside the domain.
The first is worth full marks and the second is worth full marks *and* answers a question about critical points for free.

### The short form, which is what to write if time is tight

> a. Product rule with $u=\sin x$, $v=\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$, and chain rule three deep on $v$:
> $y' = \cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$.
>
> b. Chain rule with a power outer, quotient rule on the inner:
> $y' = \frac13\left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{-2/3}\cdot\frac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{(1-x^2)^2}$.
>
> c. Chain rule twice: $y' = \dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\,\sqrt{1+x}}$.
>
> d. Quotient rule with $u=\sqrt{x^2-1}$, $u'=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$, $v=x^2-2x-8$, $v'=2x-2$:
> $y' = \dfrac{-\left(x^3+6x+2\right)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$.
>
> e. Chain rule with a power outer, then a $\sec$ outer, then the quotient rule:
> $y' = \dfrac{-6}{(x-2)^2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)$.

### The full-credit form, which adds the clauses that show the method

This paper's stem does not say "show your work", unlike Midterm 2's, so on a written sitting the naming below is insurance rather than obligation.
It costs one clause per part and it is exactly what Q8.4 asks for out loud, so the habit pays twice.

> **a.** The outermost operation is a product of two $x$-dependent factors, so this is the **product rule** $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$ with $u = \sin x$ and $v = \cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$.
> Then $u' = \cos x$, and $v'$ is the **chain rule** applied three times, with the outer $\cos$ giving $-\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$, the middle $\sin$ giving $\cos\left(x^2\right)$, and the inner $x^2$ giving $2x$:
> $$y' = \cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right).$$
>
> **b.** The outermost operation is a cube root of a bracket, so this is the **chain rule** in its generalized-power form $\frac{d}{dx}[w]^{1/3} = \frac13 w^{-2/3}w'$.
> The inner function is a quotient, so $w'$ is the **quotient rule** with $u=1+x^3$ and $v=1-x^2$:
> $$w' = \frac{3x^2\left(1-x^2\right) - \left(1+x^3\right)(-2x)}{\left(1-x^2\right)^2} = \frac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{\left(1-x^2\right)^2},$$
> $$y' = \frac13\left(\frac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{-2/3}\cdot\frac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{\left(1-x^2\right)^2}.$$
>
> **c.** The outermost operation is a square root, so this is the **chain rule**, and its inner function contains a second square root, so the chain rule applies again.
> The outer root contributes $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}}$, the inner root contributes $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}}$, and the innermost $1+x$ contributes $1$:
> $$y' = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}}\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt{1+x}} = \frac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\,\sqrt{1+x}}.$$
>
> **d.** The outermost operation is a division, so this is the **quotient rule** $\left(\frac uv\right)' = \frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$ with $u = \sqrt{x^2-1}$ and $v = x^2-2x-8$.
> Then $u' = \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$ **by the chain rule**, since $u$ is a bracket to the power $\frac12$, and $v' = 2x-2$:
> $$y' = \frac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right) - \sqrt{x^2-1}\,(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}.$$
> Multiplying numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ clears the internal fraction:
> $$y' = \frac{-\left(x^3+6x+2\right)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}, \qquad |x|>1,\ x\neq 4,\ x\neq -2.$$
>
> **e.** Since $\sec^2 u$ means $(\sec u)^2$, the outermost operation is a **square**, so this is the **chain rule** in its generalized-power form with $2\sec v$ as the outer derivative.
> The next layer is $\sec$ applied to a quotient, so the **chain rule** applies again with $\frac{d}{dv}\sec v = \sec v\tan v$, and the innermost layer is the **quotient rule** on $v = \frac{x+1}{x-2}$, giving $v' = \frac{(x-2)-(x+1)}{(x-2)^2} = \frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$:
> $$y' = 2\sec v\cdot\sec v\tan v\cdot\frac{-3}{(x-2)^2} = \frac{-6}{(x-2)^2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right)\tan\left(\frac{x+1}{x-2}\right).$$

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The eight-second calculator check, and the one thing Q8.3 adds to it

The method is Q8.1's and the $h$ is Q8.2's measured one.

> Pick an $x$ that is easy, inside the domain, and not near a pole.
> Compute $\dfrac{f(x+0.001) - f(x-0.001)}{0.002}$.
> Compare with your $y'(x)$.
> Three or four matching significant figures means your derivative is right.

$h = 0.001$ is not a taste.
Q8.2 measured the whole valley on a simulated ten-digit machine across five functions: $h=0.001$ clears three figures on every one, $h=0.01$ misses that bar on $\tan(3x)$ by 22 percent, and $h=10^{-5}$ gives relative errors up to $1.0$, meaning zero correct figures on two of the five.
Read Q8.2 section 6 for the table; do not re-derive it.

**What Q8.3 adds is the choice of $x$, and it is not a detail.**

> **A dropped factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1.**
> So a check at a point where any layer's factor happens to be 1 cannot detect a drop in that layer, however many figures agree.

Part (a) is the clean demonstration.
Its innermost factor is $2x$, which equals 1 at $x = \frac12$.
Drop that factor and check at $x = \frac12$: the wrong answer and the right answer are **identical to the last bit**, and the check passes.
Check at $x=1$ instead and the wrong answer reads $0.021042$ against a measured $-0.317952$.
That was verified both ways, and the degenerate case is kept in this document's verification as a control precisely so the point stays visible.

The practical rule that follows is short.
Avoid $x = \frac12$ when the inner derivative is $2x$.
Avoid $x = 0$ when a factor is $\cos\left(x^2\right)$ or $\cos x$.
Avoid $x = 1$ when a factor is $x^n$.
When in doubt, check at **two** points rather than one, and pick them so that no factor is near 1 at both.

Here is the check table for this question, all five verified:

| part | $x$ | $y(x)$ | your $y'(x)$ | what the calculator shows | agrees to |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | $1$ | $0.5607283$ | $-0.3179546$ | $-0.3179518$ | 5 figures |
| b | $\tfrac12$ | $\left(\tfrac32\right)^{1/3} = 1.1447142$ | $\left(\tfrac23\right)^{2/3} = 0.7631428$ | $0.7631448$ | 5 figures |
| c | $8$ | $2$ | $\tfrac1{24} = 0.0416667$ | $0.0416667$ | 9 figures |
| d | $3$ | $-\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{5} = -0.5656854$ | $-\tfrac{47\sqrt2}{100} = -0.6646804$ | $-0.6646810$ | 6 figures |
| e | $5$ | $\sec^2 2 = 5.7743992$ | $-\tfrac23\sec^2 2\tan 2 = 8.4115283$ | $8.4115328$ | 6 figures |

### The factor count, which costs no arithmetic at all

This is the cheapest check in the whole of category 8 and it is specific to Q8.3's depth.

> Count the layers in the ledger.
> Count the factors in your answer.
> They must match.

| part | layers | factors that must be there |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| a, second term | 3 | $\sin x$, $\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$, $\cos\left(x^2\right)$, and the $2x$ |
| b | 2 | the $w^{-2/3}$ and the whole of $w'$ |
| c | 3 | two roots in the denominator, and a coefficient of 4 not 2 |
| d | 2 | $u'$ must be $\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$, not $2x$ |
| e | 3 | $\sec^2 v$, $\tan v$, and the $\frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$ |

**The count catches the top trap in this family and it takes two seconds.**
If part (e) has no $(x-2)^2$ anywhere in it, layer 3 is missing.
If part (c) has a 2 where the 4 belongs, one root's contribution is missing.
If part (a)'s second term has three factors instead of four, the $2x$ or the $\cos\left(x^2\right)$ is gone.

### The cheap structural invariants, part by part

| part | invariant | why it holds |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | $y'(0) = 1$ | the second term carries $2x$ and vanishes; the first is $\cos 0\cos 0$ |
| a | the arguments $\sin x^2$ and $x^2$ appear **unchanged** | the outer derivative never touches the inner function |
| b | the surviving exponent is $-\frac23$, never $-\frac13$ | $\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23$ |
| b | $y'(0) = 0$ | $w'(0) = 0$, because $w' = \frac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{(1-x^2)^2}$ has a factor of $x$ |
| b | the base inside the bracket is **unchanged** | the same reason as (a) |
| c | the coefficient is 4, and it is $2\times2$ | one factor of 2 per square root |
| c | $y'$ is positive and **decreasing** everywhere on the domain | $y$ is a concave increasing function of $x$ |
| d | the denominator's degree is 4 plus a half-power, not 2 | the quotient rule squares $v$, and $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ came up from the numerator |
| d | the $x^2$ terms cancel in the tidy numerator | $-2x^2 + 2x^2 = 0$; if they do not, the expansion slipped |
| d | $y'$ has **no** zero on the domain | $x^3+6x+2$ has one real root and it is at $-0.327$, inside $(-1,1)$ |
| e | the coefficient is $-6$, and it is $2\times(-3)$ | power rule, then the quotient rule's $ad-bc$ |
| e | $y'(5) > 0$ despite the $-6$ | $\tan 2$ is negative |
| e | $y'$ vanishes only where $\tan v = 0$ | the $(x-2)^{-2}$ factor has no zero |

**The most useful one is part (e)'s sign.**
A $-6$ out front makes a positive answer look wrong, so a student who trusts the sign of the coefficient rather than the sign of $\tan$ will "correct" a right answer.
The check at $x=5$ settles it in eight seconds.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both exams are delivered through Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a right derivative with a bad keystroke scores zero.
Q8.1 section 7 has the general rules and Q8.2 section 7 has the exponent-position rules.
These are the ones Q8.3 adds.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| write $\sec^2 v$ as `sec((x+1)/(x-2))^2`, with the exponent on the closing bracket | `sec^2((x+1)/(x-2))`, which most parsers refuse or read as something else |
| bracket a fractional exponent: `^(-2/3)` | `^-2/3`, which parses as $\frac{-2}{3}$ applied to the wrong thing |
| bracket a nested argument all the way: `sin(sin(x^2))` | `sin(sin x^2)`, which is not valid input syntax |
| bracket the whole numerator of part (d): `(-(x^3+6*x+2))/(...)` | `-(x^3+6*x+2)/(...)`, which is fine here but a habit that breaks elsewhere |
| keep $\left(1-x^2\right)^2$ as `(1-x^2)^2` | `1-x^2^2`, which squares only the $x$ twice |
| **preview every answer before submitting** | trusting that what you typed is what it read |

**The single most expensive typo in this question** is the fractional exponent in part (b).
`((1+x^3)/(1-x^2))^(-2/3)` is right; `((1+x^3)/(1-x^2))^-2/3` is the same expression divided by 3 and is silently a different function.
The preview shows the difference immediately, which is the entire reason to use it.

**The second most expensive** is the exponent position in part (e), which is the same mistake Q8.2 flagged on `csc^2(2*x)`.
It is worth noticing that this is the second paper in a row where it appears, so it is not a one-off.

Unsimplified answers are accepted, because Möbius grades by algebraic equivalence rather than by string match.
So part (d) can be entered exactly as the quotient rule produced it, internal fraction and all, and part (c) can be entered as the raw two half-powers.
**That is the safety valve: when you are unsure of an algebraic step, enter the form you are sure of.**

### Family 0 - the nesting shape itself

The trigger is any expression with something inside something.
The tool is the ledger, and this table is how you know how many lines it will have.

| the shape | what the ledger looks like | Q8.3 part |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| bracket to a power, whose inside is a quotient | 2 lines, chain then quotient | (b) |
| root of a root of a polynomial | 3 lines, all chain | (c) |
| quotient whose numerator is a composite | 2 lines plus a sub-ledger for $u'$ | (d) |
| power of a named function of a quotient | 3 lines: power, named, quotient | (e) |
| product one of whose factors is a composite | 2 lines plus a sub-ledger for $v'$ | (a) |

**All fifteen sub-parts of category 8 are classified by that table plus Q8.1's, with no residue.**
Nothing in Q8.3 needs a rule that Q8.1 did not already give you; what it needs is a place to write four factors down.

### Family 1 - three-layer chains with named-function outers

The trigger is a trig function or a root wrapped around another trig function or root.
The tool is the chain rule, three times, and the only thing that varies is which two outer derivatives you write.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | $-2x\cos\left(x^2\right)\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | **Q8.3a**, inner factor |
| $\sin\left(\cos x^2\right)$ | $-2x\sin\left(x^2\right)\cos\left(\cos x^2\right)$ | |
| $\cos(\sin 3x)$ | $-3\cos(3x)\sin(\sin 3x)$ | |
| $\tan\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | $2x\cos\left(x^2\right)\sec^2\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | |
| $\sec(\tan 2x)$ | $2\sec(\tan 2x)\tan(\tan 2x)\sec^2(2x)$ | |
| $\sin\sqrt{x^2+1}$ | $\dfrac{x\cos\sqrt{x^2+1}}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$ | |

**Six rows and one idea**: each of the three outer derivatives from Q8.1 section 2.2, evaluated at the untouched thing below it, multiplied together.
The signs are not six facts either: they are the minus signs from $\cos$ and from nothing else in this table, exactly as Q8.1 says.

**Row 5 is the one to look at twice**, because $\tan$ appears in two different roles: once as the middle layer's function, giving a $\sec^2$ for its derivative, and once as part of $\sec$'s derivative $\sec\tan$.
Reading $\sec(\tan 2x)$ correctly means seeing that the argument of the outer $\sec$ is $\tan 2x$ and stays $\tan 2x$ throughout.

**Row 6 is Family 1 meeting section 2.3's collapse.** The $\frac12$ from the root and the $2x$ from the quadratic cancel, so no coefficient survives.

### Family 2 - nested roots, and the $2^n$ coefficient

The trigger is a square root whose inside contains another square root.
The tool is the chain rule and a count.

| $f$ | $f'$ | coefficient |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\,\sqrt{1+x}}$ | 4 |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt x\,\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}}$ | 4 |
| $\sqrt{x+\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac{1 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt x}}{2\sqrt{x+\sqrt x}}$ | 2, then an inner sum |
| $\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}}$ | $\dfrac{1}{8\sqrt x\,\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}\,\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+\sqrt x}}}$ | 8 |
| $\left(1+(1+x)^{1/3}\right)^{1/2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{6(1+x)^{2/3}\sqrt{1+(1+x)^{1/3}}}$ | 6 |

**Read the coefficient column as the whole family.**
Two roots give 4, three give 8, and a root over a cube root gives $2\times3 = 6$.
The coefficient is the product of the denominators of the fractional exponents, and nothing else contributes to it.

**Row 3 is the exception that proves the rule.**
$\sqrt{x+\sqrt x}$ has an inner function $x + \sqrt x$ whose derivative is a *sum*, not a single root, so the clean $2^n$ pattern breaks and you get $1 + \frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ instead of a bare $\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$.
The cue is that the inner expression contains a bare $x$ as well as a root.
Q8.3c's inner is $1 + \sqrt{1+x}$, where the bare term is a **constant** and contributes nothing, which is exactly why (c) comes out clean.

### Family 3 - a quotient inside a power, which is Q8.3b

The trigger is a fraction raised to a power, of any kind.
The tool is the chain rule with a power outer, and a full quotient rule for the inner.

| $f$ | $f'$ | the inner numerator |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\left(\dfrac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{1/3}$ | $\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1+x^3}{1-x^2}\right)^{-2/3}\dfrac{2x+3x^2-x^4}{\left(1-x^2\right)^2}$ | $2x+3x^2-x^4$ |
| $\left(\dfrac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^2$ | $\dfrac{4(1+x)}{(1-x)^3}$ | $2$, a constant |
| $\left(\dfrac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{1/2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{1-x}}}$ | $2$, a constant |
| $\left(\dfrac{x^2+1}{x+2}\right)^3$ | $3\left(\dfrac{x^2+1}{x+2}\right)^2\dfrac{x^2+4x-1}{(x+2)^2}$ | $x^2+4x-1$ |
| $\left(\dfrac{1+x^2}{1-x}\right)^{1/3}$ | $\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1+x^2}{1-x}\right)^{-2/3}\dfrac{1+2x-x^2}{(1-x)^2}$ | $1+2x-x^2$ |

**The whole family is one formula with two numbers in it**: the outer exponent $n$, which becomes $n\,w^{n-1}$, and the inner quotient's numerator $u'v-uv'$.
Rows 2 and 3 are the same row, because both have a linear over a linear inside, so section 2.2 applies and the inner numerator is the constant $ad-bc = 1\cdot1 - 1\cdot(-1) = 2$.
Rows 1, 4 and 5 have a genuine polynomial inner numerator and it has to be expanded.

**Row 2 is worth doing both ways once.**
Via the chain rule it is $2w\cdot\frac{2}{(1-x)^2}$, and that tidies to $\frac{4(1+x)}{(1-x)^3}$, verified as an identity.
Count the denominator power rather than guessing it.
$w^{n-1} = w^{1}$ contributes one $(1-x)^{-1}$ and $w'$ contributes $(1-x)^{-2}$, so the total is $(1-x)^{-3}$.
Getting that count wrong is the commonest slip in this family, and it is a check you can run without recomputing anything.

**The most likely variation of Q8.3b is a change of the outer exponent.**
$\frac13$ leaves $w^{-2/3}$; $\frac12$ leaves $w^{-1/2}$; $2$ leaves $w^{1}$; $-1$ leaves $w^{-2}$.
The rule is $n-1$, always, and if you memorise "the answer has a $-\frac23$" you will get every other exponent wrong.

### Family 4 - a chain inside a quotient, which is Q8.3d

The trigger is a division where the numerator, the denominator, or both, need a rule of their own.
The tool is the quotient rule with a sub-ledger per piece, written on separate lines.

| $f$ | $f'$ | which piece needed a rule |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2-1}}{x^2-2x-8}$ | $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)-\sqrt{x^2-1}(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$ | $u$, a chain; **Q8.3d** |
| $\dfrac{\sin 2x}{x^2+1}$ | $\dfrac{2\cos 2x\left(x^2+1\right)-2x\sin 2x}{\left(x^2+1\right)^2}$ | $u$, a chain |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{x+3}$ | $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}(x+3)-\sqrt{x^2+4}}{(x+3)^2}$ | $u$, a chain; $v'=1$ |
| $\dfrac{\cos\left(x^2\right)}{\sin x}$ | $\dfrac{-2x\sin\left(x^2\right)\sin x-\cos\left(x^2\right)\cos x}{\sin^2 x}$ | $u$, a chain |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+9}}{x-1}$ | $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}(x-1)-\sqrt{x^2+9}}{(x-1)^2}$ | $u$, a chain |
| $\dfrac{\sin x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$ | $\dfrac{\cos x\left(1+x^2\right)-x\sin x}{\left(1+x^2\right)^{3/2}}$ | $v$, a chain |
| $\dfrac{x^2-\cos 3x}{x\sin 2x}$ | see Q8.2 section 4 | both, and $v'$ is a **product** |

**Every one of these answers is final as written**, and that is the whole reason the printed permission exists.
The structure is identical in all of them: two products in the numerator with a minus between them, and the untouched denominator squared underneath.
What varies is only which of the four slots $u$, $u'$, $v$, $v'$ needed a rule of its own, and the discipline that makes this family safe is to compute all four **before** you write the fraction.

**Row 6 is the one that tidies, and it tidies differently from Q8.3d.**
Its internal fraction is in the second numerator term rather than the first, and multiplying through by $\sqrt{1+x^2}$ raises the denominator from $\left(1+x^2\right)^{1}$ to $\left(1+x^2\right)^{3/2}$.
So the tidy-up rule is not "the denominator picks up a root", it is "multiply by whatever the internal denominator is and see where it lands".
Verified.

**Row 7 is Q8.2c**, and it is here to make the point that Q8.2's Family 8 and Q8.3's Family 4 are the same family looked at from two papers.
If you have done Q8.2c you have done Q8.3d.

### Family 5 - a squared or cubed trig of a composite, which is Q8.3e

The trigger is a trig function carrying an exponent on its **name**.
The tool is the chain rule with a power outer, and then whatever the layers below need.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sec^2\left(\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}\right)$ | $\dfrac{-6}{(x-2)^2}\sec^2 v\tan v$ | **Q8.3e** |
| $\cos^2\left(\dfrac{x+1}{x-2}\right)$ | $\dfrac{6}{(x-2)^2}\cos v\sin v$ | |
| $\sec^2(3x)$ | $6\sec^2(3x)\tan(3x)$ | |
| $\tan^2\left(x^2\right)$ | $4x\tan\left(x^2\right)\sec^2\left(x^2\right)$ | |
| $\sin^3(2x)$ | $6\sin^2(2x)\cos(2x)$ | |
| $\cos^3\left(x^2\right)$ | $-6x\cos^2\left(x^2\right)\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | **Q8.4b** |
| $\sqrt{\sec 2x}$ | $\tan(2x)\sqrt{\sec 2x}$ | |

**Every row is $n\left[T(u)\right]^{n-1}\cdot T'(u)\cdot u'$, and the coefficient is the product of $n$ and whatever $u'$ contributes.**
Row 1's $-6$ is $2\times(-3)$, row 2's $+6$ is $2\times(-1)\times(-3)$ where the extra minus is $\cos$'s, row 3's $6$ is $2\times3$, row 4's $4$ is $2\times 2x$'s coefficient, rows 5 and 6's $6$ is $3\times 2$.
There are seven rows and one multiplication.

**Rows 1 and 2 are the pair to study**, because they differ only in the outer trig function and the signs come out opposite: $\sec^2$ gives $-6$ and $\cos^2$ gives $+6$, since $\cos$ contributes a minus that $\sec$ does not.
Getting that from the table rather than from the rule is how you end up guessing.

**Row 7 is the pleasant surprise.**
$\sqrt{\sec 2x} = (\sec 2x)^{1/2}$, so $f' = \frac12(\sec 2x)^{-1/2}\sec 2x\tan 2x\cdot 2 = \tan(2x)\sqrt{\sec 2x}$, verified.
The $\frac12$ and the inner 2 cancel, and $(\sec)^{-1/2}\cdot\sec = (\sec)^{1/2}$, so the answer is shorter than the raw line.
That is a real simplification worth doing.

### Family 6 - a product with a nested factor, which is Q8.3a

The trigger is two $x$-dependent factors multiplied, at least one of which is a composite.
The tool is $u'v + uv'$, which is symmetric so there is no order to get wrong, plus a sub-ledger for the composite factor.

| $f$ | $f'$ | where |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | **Q8.3a** |
| $\sin x\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | $\cos x\cos\left(x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | the two-layer warm-up |
| $x\cos\sqrt{x-3}$ | $\cos\sqrt{x-3} - \dfrac{x\sin\sqrt{x-3}}{2\sqrt{x-3}}$ | review question 1 |
| $x^2\sin(\cos x)$ | $2x\sin(\cos x) - x^2\sin x\cos(\cos x)$ | |
| $x\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | $\sin\left(x^2\right) + 2x^2\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | |

**Row 2 is row 1 with one layer removed, and comparing them is the fastest way to see what the third layer contributes.**
Row 2's second term has three factors, row 1's has four, and the extra factor is exactly the $\cos\left(x^2\right)$ that the middle $\sin$ layer produced.
If you can write row 2 and then insert one factor, you can write row 1.

**Row 5 is the trap in miniature.**
$x\sin\left(x^2\right)$ looks like it should give $2x^2\cos\left(x^2\right)$ and nothing else, because the second term is where all the interesting work is.
The first term $\sin\left(x^2\right)$, from differentiating the bare $x$, is the one that gets dropped.
The product rule is symmetric, which means both halves are equally easy to forget.

### Family 7 - an evaluation at a designed point

The trigger is a vertical bar with $x=a$ on it, or an evaluation point that looks arbitrary.
The tool is: **differentiate symbolically first, substitute last**, and then look for what the point was designed to kill.

| asked | answer | what the point was for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y'(8)$ for Q8.3c | $\dfrac1{24}$ | $1+x=9$ and $1+3=4$, so both roots close |
| $y'\left(\tfrac12\right)$ for Q8.3b | $\left(\tfrac23\right)^{2/3}$ | $w=\tfrac32$ and $w'=3$, both exact |
| $y'(5)$ for Q8.3e | $-\tfrac23\sec^2 2\tan 2$ | $v(5)=2$ exactly |
| $y'(3)$ for Q8.3d | $-\dfrac{47\sqrt2}{100}$ | $x^2-1=8$ and $x^2-2x-8=-5$, both clean |
| $y'(0)$ for Q8.3a | $1$ | kills the whole second term |
| $y'(-1)$ for Q8.3e | $0$ | $v=0$, so $\tan v = 0$ |
| $\left.\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos^3\left(x^2\right)\right|_{x=\sqrt\pi/2}$ | $-\dfrac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{4} \approx -1.87997$ | $x^2 = \tfrac\pi4$, where $\sin=\cos=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ |

**The last row is how these are constructed and it is Q8.4b.**
$\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}$ looks arbitrary until you square it and get $\frac\pi4$.
**Whenever an evaluation point looks strange, square it, double it, or substitute it into the innermost layer and see what it was designed to become.**
That is a five-second move and it converts a messy evaluation into an exact one.

Rows 5 and 6 are the other half of the same idea: sometimes the point is chosen to make a whole term vanish, and spotting that means you never compute the term at all.

### Family 8 - the simplification that pays, and the one that does not

The trigger is having a correct derivative and choosing to tidy it.
The tool is care, and the point of this family is that **the decision is different for each of the five parts**.

| the raw form | the tidy form | worth it? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac12\left[1+\sqrt{1+x}\right]^{-1/2}\cdot\frac12(1+x)^{-1/2}$ | $\dfrac{1}{4\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\sqrt{1+x}}$ | **yes**, one step, and it makes the answer readable |
| $\dfrac{\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)-\sqrt{x^2-1}(2x-2)}{\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$ | $\dfrac{-\left(x^3+6x+2\right)}{\sqrt{x^2-1}\left(x^2-2x-8\right)^2}$ | **yes**, three lines, and it clears a stacked fraction |
| $2\sec v\cdot\sec v\tan v\cdot\frac{-3}{(x-2)^2}$ | $\dfrac{-6\sec^2 v\tan v}{(x-2)^2}$ | **yes**, but only the arithmetic; it is one multiplication |
| $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right) - 2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | there is none | **no** |
| $\frac13 w^{-2/3}\cdot\frac{w'}{(1-x^2)^2}$ | combining into one fraction | **no**, it gets longer |
| $\frac12(\sec 2x)^{-1/2}\sec 2x\tan 2x\cdot 2$ | $\tan(2x)\sqrt{\sec 2x}$ | **yes**, three cancellations |

**Every entry in this table was checked as an identity over many sample points**, not at one value, because two expressions can agree at a single point by accident.
A seeded off-by-one denominator power was confirmed to be rejected by that check.

The row to study is row 2, because the $x^2$ cancellation is the single most common place to lose a mark on part (d).
$-\left(x^2-1\right)(2x-2)$ is $-2x^3+2x^2+2x-2$, and the plausible wrong answer drops one of those four signs.
The free check is that the $x^2$ terms must cancel exactly: $-2x^2$ from the first product and $+2x^2$ from the second.
If an $x^2$ survives, the expansion slipped.

### Family 9 - what a dropped factor looks like

The trigger is that your answer looks right.
The tool is the factor count and a check at a well-chosen point, and this family exists so you know what you are looking for.

| the drop | what you write | how to see it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| (a), the innermost $2x$ | $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)-\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | count: 3 factors where 4 belong. **Invisible at $x=\frac12$.** |
| (a), the middle $\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)-2x\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | count. **Invisible at $x=0$.** |
| (a), the whole chain | $\cos x\cos\left(\sin x^2\right)-\sin x\sin\left(\sin x^2\right)$ | 2 factors where 4 belong |
| (b), the inner $w'$ | $\frac13 w^{-2/3}$ | no $(1-x^2)^2$ anywhere |
| (c), the middle root | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}}$ | no $\sqrt{1+x}$ in the denominator, and a 2 not a 4 |
| (c), one factor of 2 | $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1+\sqrt{1+x}}\sqrt{1+x}}$ | the coefficient is not $2^2$ |
| (d), the chain in $u'$ | $u' = 2x$ | no $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ upstairs |
| (e), the innermost $v'$ | $2\sec^2 v\tan v$ | no $(x-2)^2$ anywhere, and the coefficient is 2 not $-6$ |
| (e), the power rule's 2 | $\dfrac{-3\sec^2 v\tan v}{(x-2)^2}$ | the coefficient is $-3$ not $-6$ |

**All nine of these were seeded into this document's verification and every one was confirmed to be caught**, and the two marked "invisible" were confirmed to be invisible at the point named.
That is the whole reason section 6 tells you to choose the check point rather than picking the first easy number.

**Read the last column as one instruction: look for the innermost layer's contribution by name.**
For (b) it is a $(1-x^2)^2$, for (c) it is a $\sqrt{1+x}$, for (d) it is a $\sqrt{x^2-1}$ upstairs, for (e) it is an $(x-2)^2$.
Each of those is a distinctive string that either is in your answer or is not, and checking for it takes one second.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks in this family.

1. **Dropping the innermost layer's factor.** Family 9 is the whole catalog. This is the number one mark-loser in the entire course, it is worst on a three-level tree because there are more places for it to hide, and the factor count in section 6 catches it without any arithmetic.
2. **Getting the fractional exponent wrong in (b).** $\frac13 - 1 = -\frac23$, not $-\frac13$ and not $\frac13$. Say it out loud rather than computing it.
3. **Writing $u' = 2x$ in (d).** The numerator is $\left(x^2-1\right)^{1/2}$, so its derivative is $\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-1}}$. This is trap 1 wearing a different hat, and Q8.1 recorded the same trap on its part (b)'s denominator.
4. **A sign slip expanding $-\left(x^2-1\right)(2x-2)$ in (d).** Four terms, four signs, and the free check is that the $x^2$ terms must cancel.
5. **Reading $\sec^2 v$ as $\sec\left(v^2\right)$.** It changes which rule the top layer needs, so it is a wrong method rather than a notation slip. Q8.2 section 2.2 is the same distinction and it cost marks there too.
6. **The coefficient in (e).** $-6$ is $2\times(-3)$, and losing either factor gives $-3$ or $+2$. Count the sources.
7. **The sign of $v'$ in (e).** $(x-2) - (x+1) = -3$, not $+3$. Section 2.2's $ad-bc$ gets it right every time.
8. **A 2 where the 4 belongs in (c).** The coefficient is a count of the roots, not a number to remember.
9. **The double negative in (b)'s $w'$.** $-\left(1+x^3\right)(-2x) = +2x+2x^4$. Two minus signs, so the term is positive.
10. **Reading $\sin x^2$ as $\sin^2 x$ in (a).** They are different functions with different derivatives, $-0.3180$ and $-0.0872$ at $x=1$. Read the superscript's position.
11. **Forgetting the first term of the product rule in (a).** $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ and it multiplies the whole untouched second factor. The product rule is symmetric, so both halves are equally forgettable.
12. **Checking at a point where a factor equals 1.** The check passes and the answer is wrong. Section 6.
13. **Using $h$ too small on the calculator check.** Q8.2 measured it: $h=10^{-5}$ can give zero correct figures, so a right answer looks wrong and you "fix" it.
14. **Simplifying (a), (b) or (e) at all.** There is nothing there, so any algebra is pure risk. The printed permission covers you.
15. **Missing brackets on the fractional exponent at entry.** `^(-2/3)` versus `^-2/3`. Preview catches this and nothing else will.

|              a fraction is stacked INSIDE a fraction  -> yes, clear it
|              two coefficients can multiply             -> yes, one step

|              a trig product would be DISTRIBUTED       -> NO

+-- STEP 7.  CHECK.  [f(x+0.001) - f(x-0.001)] / 0.002
             h = 0.001, NOT smaller.  (Q8.2 measured the valley.)
             PICK x so no layer's factor is near 1, or the check is BLIND.
               inner 2x     -> avoid x = 0.5
               inner cos    -> avoid x = 0

      NAME IT   look for the innermost layer's signature string:

      SUPERSCR  on the NAME -> power outer.  Inside the bracket -> named.
