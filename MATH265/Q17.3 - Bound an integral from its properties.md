## 2. The six things you must have cold

### 2.1 The comparison property, restated as a recipe

If $m \le f(x) \le M$ for every $x$ in $[a,b]$, then
$$m(b-a) \;\le\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\le\; M(b-a).$$
This is Q17.1's and Q17.2's tool, unchanged.
The whole procedure is: find $m$ and $M$, multiply each by the interval's length, write the inequality.

### 2.2 An item with two parts sharing one function gives you $m$ and $M$ for free

When part (a) asks for the absolute extreme values of $f$ on $[a,b]$ and part (b) asks you to bound
$\int_a^b f$, part (a)'s **maximum IS $M$** and its **minimum IS $m$**.
This is Q17.1's Family 2, and it applies here exactly as it applied to Q17.2.
Re-deriving the extreme-value work in part (b) wastes time that the exam does not give back.

### 2.3 $f(x) = (x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ is never negative, and that makes $m = 0$ a live possibility

$(x^2+2x)^{2/3} = \left((x^2+2x)^2\right)^{1/3}$, a cube root of a square, so $f \ge 0$ everywhere it is
defined, and it is defined for every real $x$.
Unlike $\sec x$ (Q17.1) or $1/(x^2+1)$ (Q17.2), this integrand can actually attain $0$, so $m=0$ is not a
sign of a mistake - it is the correct floor.

### 2.4 Where $f'$ is undefined, from Q15.5, re-confirmed independently here

$$f'(x) = \frac{4(x+1)}{3(x^2+2x)^{1/3}}, \qquad x^2+2x = x(x+2) = 0 \iff x \in \{-2,\,0\}.$$
$x=0$ is interior to $[-2,3]$ and is a genuine **cusp**: the one-sided slopes diverge to $-\infty$ on the
left and $+\infty$ on the right, confirmed numerically at $h=10^{-8}$ ($f'(-h) = -491.2042$,
$f'(h) = 491.2042$), not merely asserted.
$x=-2$ is the same kind of point but it sits at the left **endpoint**, so it never needs to be found by a
domain census - it is already on every candidate list.

### 2.5 The candidate list for $[-2,3]$

$f'=0$ at $x=-1$ (interior); $f'$ undefined at $x=-2$ (endpoint) and $x=0$ (interior cusp); plus the two
endpoints themselves.
Full list: $\{-2,\,-1,\,0,\,3\}$.
$f(-2)=0$, $f(-1)=1$, $f(0)=0$, $f(3)=15^{2/3}\approx 6.0822019956$.
So $m=0$ (tied, at two points) and $M=15^{2/3}$ (at the right endpoint) - Q15.5's own answer, reproduced.

### 2.6 A bound is not an estimate

An **estimate** (Q17.2's verb) rewards the single closest number.
A **bound** (this question's verb) only has to be true - it can be as loose as $[0,\,10^6]$ and still be
correct, though a marker will usually want the *tightest bound the properties actually give*, which is
$m(b-a)$ and $M(b-a)$ with $m,M$ the real extremes, not any weaker pair.
Looseness is not an error; an *invalid* $m$ or $M$ (one that is not really a bound on $f$) is.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Identify $f$, $a$, $b$.** Confirm $f$ is continuous (or at worst has removable/cusp-type
>    non-smoothness, not a pole) on $[a,b]$ - a pole makes the integral improper and there is no finite
>    $M$ (Q17.1's Family 6).
> 2. **Check for a free part (a).** If the item pairs an absolute-extrema question with a bound question
>    on the same function and interval, copy $m$ and $M$ from it. Otherwise find them yourself: solve
>    $f'=0$ **and** find where $f'$ is undefined, screening every candidate, then evaluate $f$ at every
>    candidate plus both endpoints.
> 3. **Compute $b-a$.** This is the step every wrong answer in this category shares a name with.
> 4. **Write $m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f \le M(b-a)$.** Simplify the two products; do not round early.
> 5. **Sanity check.** Confirm $m \ge 0$ is possible for this integrand (not automatic - $\sec x$ and
>    $1/(x^2+1)$ cannot both be non-negative for free the way this one is), and confirm the interval you
>    wrote is not empty or backwards.

Classify by verb before doing any algebra:

| Verb in the wording | What is wanted | This category's example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "find an interval where the value is located" | The inequality itself, as an interval | Q17.1 |
| "estimate" | A single number, usually the midpoint of the bounds | Q17.2 |
| "bound" | The inequality itself; looseness is acceptable, invalidity is not | Q17.3 |

## 4. Q17.3 worked three ways

### Route A: copy from part (a) (the one to use)

$m=0$, $M=15^{2/3}$ from Q15.5.
$b-a = 3-(-2) = 5$.
$$0\cdot 5 \;\le\; \int_{-2}^{3}(x^2+2x)^{2/3}\,dx \;\le\; 15^{2/3}\cdot 5$$
$$0 \;\le\; I \;\le\; 30.4110099779.$$
Two multiplications and one sentence, exactly Q17.1's Family 2 pattern.

### Route B: re-derive $m,M$ from scratch (in case part (a) is not given, or as a self-check)

Solve $f'(x) = \dfrac{4(x+1)}{3(x^2+2x)^{1/3}} = 0 \Rightarrow x=-1$; find where $f'$ is undefined,
$x^2+2x=0 \Rightarrow x\in\{-2,0\}$; evaluate $f$ at $\{-2,-1,0,3\}$: $\{0,1,0,6.0822\}$.
$m=0$, $M=6.0822019956$ - matches Route A exactly.
This route costs the whole closed-interval method again; use it only to *check* Route A, never to
replace it when a part (a) already exists.

### Route C: split at the branch points first, then bound each piece and sum (the tightening move)

$$\int_{-2}^{3} = \int_{-2}^{-1} + \int_{-1}^{0} + \int_{0}^{3}.$$
On $[-2,-1]$: $m_1=0,\,M_1=1$, width $1$, piece $\subseteq[0,1]$.
On $[-1,0]$: $m_2=0,\,M_2=1$, width $1$, piece $\subseteq[0,1]$.
On $[0,3]$: $m_3=0,\,M_3=6.0822019956$, width $3$, piece $\subseteq[0,18.2466059867]$.
Sum: $[0,\;20.2466059867]$ - the same floor, but the ceiling is $33.4\%$ tighter than the one-piece bound
($30.4110099779\to20.2466059867$), because $x=-1$ is a genuine local maximum inside the interval and
splitting there stops it from being "wasted" against the far-away global maximum at $x=3$.

### Route comparison

| Route | Effort | Ceiling produced | When to use |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| A - copy from part (a) | 2 multiplications | $30.4110099779$ | Always, when part (a) exists |
| B - re-derive | full closed-interval method | $30.4110099779$ | Part (a) is missing, or as a check |
| C - split at branch points | 3x the multiplications | $20.2466059867$ | Only if the wording explicitly asks to *tighten* the bound |

**Use Route A under pressure.** Route C is correct and instructive but is not what "use the properties of
definite integrals to bound the value" is asking for on a 6-point item that already paid for the extrema
in part (a).

## 6. Verify before you commit

- **$m \le M$**: $0 \le 15^{2/3}$. True by inspection.
- **$m \ge 0$ is consistent with the integrand**: $f(x)=(x^2+2x)^{2/3}\ge 0$ everywhere, confirmed
  algebraically (a cube root of a square) and numerically (sampled, zero negative values found).
- **The bound is not empty or backwards**: $0 \le 30.4110099779$, not the other way round.
- **Magnitude check**: the interval has width $5$ and $f$ peaks at about $6.08$, so a ceiling near $30$
  is the right order of magnitude; a ceiling of $6$ (the dropped-factor mistake) is suspiciously close to
  $M$ itself and should read as "I forgot to multiply by something."
- **True value confirmed to fall inside, by two independent quadrature methods**: a smooth
  cusp-removing substitution ($x=-2+s^3$, $x=-s^3$, $x=s^3$ on the three pieces, stable to $10^{-14}$
  across $n=40$ to $320$) and split tanh-sinh quadrature at the interior branch point $x=0$ (stable to
  $10^{-14}$ across levels 6-12).
  Both give $I = 10.6736300365$ (to twelve digits, $10.673630036466$), matching the carryover's independently-verified value
  $10.673630036465604$ to $1.1\times10^{-14}$.
  **Margins: the true value is $10.6736300365$ above the floor and $19.7373799414$ below the ceiling**
  (only $35.0979\%$ of the ceiling - this bound is loose, and that is fine).

**A quadrature warning specific to this integrand.** Left un-split, tanh-sinh over $[-2,3]$ gives
$10.6733805848$ at level 8, wrong by $2.5\times10^{-4}$, because double-exponential clustering only helps
at the endpoints of a piece, not at an interior singularity.
**Split at $x=0$ first.**
Composite Simpson on the raw (unsplit-at-the-cusp) integrand measures an observed order of only
**$1.6666$**, not $4$ - confirmed on a bounded ladder from $n=64$ to $4096$ - because the cusp kills the
smoothness Simpson's error formula assumes.
The control that proves this is the integrand, not the method: substituting away the cusp
($x=\pm(\text{branch})\pm s^3$) restores order $4.0000$ on the very same routine.
**A digit-lock here needs the substitution, not just two ladders agreeing** - Q15.5 already found that
two resolutions of a cusp integrand can agree to $3\times10^{-9}$ while both being $4.3\times10^{-9}$
wrong, and this integrand is exactly that case (it is the same $f$).

### Family 1 - splitting at the critical points to tighten the bound (Route C generalised)

**Trigger.** "Improve your bound" or "use a finer partition."
**Tool.** $\int_a^b = \sum_i\int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}$; the sandwich's width is $(M-m)(b-a)/k$ for $k$ EQUAL
pieces of a monotone function, but for THIS function that formula needs a correction, because $f$ has
**three monotone branches** on $[-2,3]$ (increasing on $[-2,-1]$, decreasing on $[-1,0]$, increasing on
$[0,3]$), not one.

| $k$ (equal pieces) | bound | width | textbook $(M-m)(b-a)/k$ | ratio |
| ---: | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1$ | $[0,\ 30.4110099779]$ | $30.4110099779$ | $30.4110099779$ | $1.0000$ |
| $5$ | $[6.0800838231,\ 14.1622858186]$ | $8.0822019956$ | $6.0822019956$ | $1.3288$ |
| $20$ | $[9.6014733864,\ 11.6220238853]$ | $2.0205504989$ | $1.5205504989$ | $1.3288$ |
| $200$ | $[10.5711357902,\ 10.7731908401]$ | $0.2020550499$ | $0.1520550499$ | $1.3288$ |

**The ratio converges to $1.3288$, not $1$**, and it is not the number of branches ($3$) either.
It is $\dfrac{\sum(\text{branch spans})}{M-m} = \dfrac{1.0000000000+1.0000000000+6.0822019956}{6.0822019956} = 1.3288282766$.
**Verified directly against the branches-vs-critical-points split (Route C)**, which gets a DIFFERENT,
sharper improvement (a $33.4\%$ ceiling cut at $k=1\to3$ pieces) because it puts the split exactly at the
branch boundaries instead of at equal-length pieces.
General rule: equal-width splitting only ever telescopes toward $\sum(\text{branch spans})/(M-m)$; splitting
AT the critical points gets the full benefit in one step.

### Family 2 - a factor-wise sandwich, when the integrand factors

**Trigger.** The integrand is a product of two pieces, each individually bounded on the interval.
**Tool.** $(x^2+2x)^{2/3} = x^{2/3}(x+2)^{2/3}$ on $[0,3]$ (both factors non-negative there); bound each
factor separately and multiply the bounds into the easy integral of one factor.
**Worked shape.** On $[0,3]$: $2^{2/3}\le(x+2)^{2/3}\le5^{2/3}$, so
$$2^{2/3}\int_0^3 x^{2/3}\,dx \;\le\; \int_0^3(x^2+2x)^{2/3}\,dx \;\le\; 5^{2/3}\int_0^3 x^{2/3}\,dx.$$
$\int_0^3 x^{2/3}dx = \tfrac{3}{5}3^{5/3} = 3.7441508815$, so the sandwich is
$[5.9434690480,\ 10.9479635920]$, width $5.0044945440$ - **against the crude one-piece width $18.2466059867$
on that same subinterval, a $72.6\%$ narrower ceiling-to-floor gap**, and it is confirmed pointwise over
$200001$ samples with zero violations in either direction.
Combined with the crude $[0,2]$ on $[-2,0]$: $[5.9434690480,\ 12.9479635920]$, still containing the true
value $10.6736300365$.
**This family needs a factorable integrand and $\sqrt[3]{\cdot}$-with-two-nonnegative-parts, which
Q17.1's $\sec x$ and Q17.2's $1/(x^2+1)$ do not have** - it is genuinely new to this question.

### Family 3 - domination by a simpler comparable function (Q17.1's Family 3, reused)

**Trigger.** "Show that $I<N$" using a dominating function rather than a constant.
**Tool.** $f\le g$ on $[a,b]\Rightarrow\int f\le\int g$; pick $g$ with an easy antiderivative.
**Worked shape and verified table:**

| $g$ on $[-2,3]$ | $\int g$ | crude $M(b-a)$ | true value | valid? |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| $g=15^{2/3}$ (the constant $M$) | $30.4110099779$ | $30.4110099779$ | $10.6736300365$ | yes (this IS the crude bound) |
| $g=(x+2)^{2/3}\cdot3^{2/3}$ | $18.2466059867$ | $30.4110099779$ | $10.6736300365$ | yes, checked pointwise, $0$ violations |

A series-based sandwich (Q17.2's Family 3, from $1-u\le\tfrac1{1+u}\le1-u+u^2$) has **no analogue here**:
$(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ has no convenient alternating-series expansion on this interval because of the cusp, so
this family is absent for Q17.3 where it exists for Q17.2 - itself worth noting as a variation-family gap.

### Family 4 - the average value and the Mean Value Theorem for integrals

**Trigger.** "Find $c$ such that $f(c)$ equals the average value," or a question about where the sandwich
is tightest.
**Tool.** $f_{\text{avg}} = \dfrac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f$, and $m\le f_{\text{avg}}\le M$; the MVT for
integrals guarantees at least one $c\in[a,b]$ with $f(c)=f_{\text{avg}}$.
**Worked shape.** $f_{\text{avg}} = 10.6736300365/5 = 2.1347260073$.
$0\le2.1347260073\le6.0822019956$. True.
$c=1.0295281386$ is the unique point on $[0,3]$ where $f(c)=f_{\text{avg}}$ (unique on the whole interval
because $f<f_{\text{avg}}$ everywhere on $[-2,0]$: the local max there is only $1.0000000000$).
**A wrong $m'$ hides iff $m'\le f_{\text{avg}}$; a wrong $M'$ hides iff $M'\ge f_{\text{avg}}$** - this is
Q17.1's boundary (22.3) and Q17.2's flipped-orientation version (23.4) applied to a THIRD shape: here
there is exactly one crossing on the rising branch, because the falling branch never reaches the average.

### Family 5 - estimates built FROM the bound, and why the usual winner loses here

**Trigger.** A follow-up asks for a single number after the bound is found.
**Tool.** Midpoint of the bounds, midpoint rule, or trapezoid - Q17.2's ranking, re-tested here.

| estimate | value | error |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| midpoint of the bounds $\tfrac{0+30.4110099779}{2}$ | $15.2055049889$ | $4.5318749525$ |
| midpoint rule $f(0.5)\cdot5$ | $5.8019860420$ | $4.8716439944$ |
| trapezoid $\tfrac12(f(-2)+f(3))\cdot5$ | $15.2055049889$ | $4.5318749525$ |
| midpoint of the 3-piece (Route C) bound | $10.1233029934$ | $0.5503270431$ |

**Q17.2 recommended "midpoint of the bounds" as the cheapest reliable estimate. Here it is nearly the
WORST of the four**, because $m=0$ makes it just half the ceiling, carrying zero information from the
floor.
**When $m=0$, an estimate built from the raw one-piece bound is close to useless; refine the bound
(Route C) before estimating from it.**
This is the reason Q17.3's own wording says "bound," not "estimate" - the examiner already knows the
naive estimate is bad here.

### Family 6 - one-sided bounds, and what a floor of zero is worth

**Trigger.** "Find a lower bound" or "show the integral is at least..."
**Tool.** The comparison property still applies to just one side.
**Worked shape.** $f\ge0$ everywhere makes the floor $m(b-a)=0$ **true for every subinterval of every
question of this shape** - it is correct and it is empty of information.
A non-trivial floor needs a non-trivial comparison: on $[0,3]$, $f\ge2^{2/3}x^{2/3}$, giving
$$\int_{-2}^{3}f \;\ge\; \int_0^3 2^{2/3}x^{2/3}\,dx = 5.9434690480,$$
which is $55.6837\%$ of the true value - a genuinely useful floor, unlike the trivial $0$.
**Trigger for when zero is acceptable versus when it is not**: if the wording says "bound," $m=0$ is a
complete, correct answer for the floor.
If it says "show the integral is positive and at least [some number]," the trivial floor does not answer
the question and a non-trivial comparison like Family 2/6 above is required.

### Family 7 - the cusp-omission propagation test (Q15.5's finding, checked here rather than assumed)

**Trigger.** A student solves only $f'=0$ and forgets to screen for where $f'$ is undefined - Q15.5's own
trap 1, which that page found does not show on the exam's own interval $[-2,3]$ (both extremes happen to
be attained anyway).
**Tool.** Compare the bound built with the cusp candidate $x=0$ against the bound built without it, on
several intervals, and check whether the true value still falls inside the wrong bound.

| interval | bound WITH the cusp | bound WITHOUT it | true value | effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks (inference; no marking scheme exists).

1. **Dropping the $(b-a)$ factor.** Reports $[0,\,6.0822019956]$, which **excludes** the true value
   $10.6736300365$ - unlike Q17.1, where the analogous slip is invisible.
   **This is the trap the category exists to punish, and here it shows.**
   The general rule (from Q17.2's derivation): it hides exactly when the interval's width is at most
   $M/f_{\text{avg}} = 2.8491722005$; the exam's width is $5$, well past that threshold, so the slip is
   caught with room to spare.

2. **Typing `15^2/3` for the maximum's value.** Möbius returns $75$ (operator precedence), not
   $6.0822019956$. Section 7's rule: preview before submitting.

3. **Solving only $f'=0$ and never screening for where $f'$ is undefined.** On this interval it is
   ABSORBED (section 8, Family 7) - it does not cost marks on the bound itself, because $x=-2$ already
   forces the minimum to $0$.
   It DOES cost marks if part (a) is graded separately and asks you to locate all points where the
   minimum occurs, since the answer there is "$x=-2$ AND $x=0$," not just one of them.

4. **Re-deriving $m$ and $M$ from scratch when part (a) is already solved.** Not a wrong answer, but a
   time cost on a 6-point item that already paid for the extreme-value work.

5. **Reading $(-1)^{2/3}$ as $-1$ instead of $+1$.** This would introduce a wrong critical value at
   $x=-1$; it does not change $m$ or $M$ here (the true value at $x=-1$, $f(-1)=1$, is neither the min
   nor the max), so the mistake happens to be invisible on THIS question even though the identical
   misread is visible on Q15.5's own page.

6. **Writing the interval backwards, $[M(b-a),\,m(b-a)]$.** An inverted interval is empty and contains
   nothing; the census does not silently re-sort it, and neither should a marker.

7. **Reporting the exact value instead of the bound.** The wording asks to "bound the value," and while
   $10.6736300365$ happens to fall inside the correct bound (so it is not literally caught by a
   containment check), it answers a different question than the one assigned.

### The census, measured

Twenty distinct wrong answers, tags computed rather than typed, indexed by the ERROR rather than by the
interval it produces (a slip that lands on the same output as a different slip is still two rows):

$$\textbf{6 valid+containing} \;+\; \textbf{6 invalid-but-containing (HIDE)} \;+\; \textbf{8 caught} \;=\; 20.$$

Against Q17.1's $6/4/8$ and Q17.2's $5/3/10$: **Q17.3 catches proportionally MORE of its census than
either predecessor** ($8/20=40\%$ here versus $8/18=44\%$ for Q17.1 and $10/18=56\%$ for Q17.2 - so by
this one measure Q17.3 sits between the two, closer to Q17.1's leniency than Q17.2's).
The six hiders here: reading the width as $3$ (using $|b|$ instead of $b-a$); integrating only over
$[0,3]$ while keeping the whole-interval $m,M$; reporting $M=3$ (the location, not the value); taking
$m=1$ from the interior critical value $f(-1)=1$; the same $m=1$ slip combined with a cusp omission; and
writing the exact value alone as a degenerate "bound." **Loosening the bound in any direction was tested
separately and NEVER fails to contain the true value** (over $180001$ sampled loosenings, zero failures) -
this is 22.4's asymmetry, reproduced a third time: a containment check can only ever catch
over-tightening, never over-loosening, so the verification arm for a bound question must check the
JUSTIFICATION of $m$ and $M$, not merely whether the final interval contains the answer.

## 11. Closing the category: one procedure for all three questions

### The one procedure

> 1. Read the verb: "interval" wants the inequality as a location; "estimate" wants one number;
>    "bound" wants the inequality and rewards a valid one even if loose.
> 2. Confirm the integrand has no pole inside $[a,b]$ (Q17.1's Family 6) - if it does, say so and stop;
>    there is no finite $M$.
> 3. Get $m$ and $M$: copy from a paired part (a) if one exists (Q17.2 and Q17.3 both have one; Q17.1
>    does not and needs the closed-interval method run in place).
> 4. Compute $b-a$ and multiply. This line is the category's one idea and its one trap.
> 5. If "estimate," reduce the bound to one number - midpoint of the bounds is usually safest, EXCEPT
>    when $m=0$ makes it half the ceiling and worthless (Q17.3's Family 5); refine the bound first in
>    that case.
> 6. Verify: does $m\le M$? Is the interval non-empty? Does an independent quadrature (or, for a smooth
>    integrand, the exact antiderivative) fall inside?

### What the three questions divide up

| Question | Verb | $m,M$ source | Integrand shape | Does the $(b-a)$-drop slip hide? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Q17.1 | "interval" | derived in place (no part a) | smooth, monotone, has a nearby pole to screen for | **YES** - width $\pi/3=1.0472$ is close to $1$ |
| Q17.2 | "estimate" | copied from Q15.3 | smooth, even, non-monotone (one interior max) | no - width $2$, threshold $4/\pi=1.2732$ |
| Q17.3 | "bound" | copied from Q15.5 | non-negative, has a cusp, three monotone branches | no - width $5$, threshold $M/f_{\text{avg}}=2.8492$ |

**The category's defining error (dropping $(b-a)$) hides on the question that opens it and is caught on
both that follow.**
Q17.1 is the machinery and the one hiding case; Q17.2 is the free-inputs question and the "estimate"
verb, where the ranking of estimates can invert depending on where the integrand's extremum sits; Q17.3
is the one-sided-bound question and the only one whose integrand is non-smooth, non-negative, and built
from more than one monotone branch - which is exactly why splitting (Family 1) helps here more than on
the other two, and why $m=0$ makes "bound" the honest verb rather than "estimate."

### The one-page decision tree for this question

```
READ THE VERB
  |
  +-- "interval" / "bound"?  ->  produce the inequality; do NOT collapse it to one number
  |                              unless separately asked
  +-- "estimate"?            ->  reduce to one number, but check m first: if m=0, the naive
                                 midpoint-of-bounds estimate is nearly worthless (Family 5);
                                 split the interval first (Route C) and estimate from THAT bound

DOES A PAIRED PART (a) EXIST?
  |
  +-- YES -> copy its max/min as M/m.  DO NOT redo the closed-interval method.
  +-- NO  -> run it: f'=0 candidates + f' UNDEFINED candidates + both endpoints, screened BOTH ways.

MULTIPLY m AND M BY (b - a).  <-- the one line the whole category is testing.

CHECK: is (b-a) close to 1?
  |
  +-- close to 1  -> the drop-the-factor slip would HIDE; do not trust "my interval looks fine"
  +-- far from 1  -> the slip would be CAUGHT; if your own answer looks wrong, it probably is

VERIFY: m <= M, interval not empty, magnitude sane, true value (if computable) falls inside.
PREVIEW the Mobius entry string before submitting.
```
