## Course Orientation

### Introduction

More than ever before, the choices of those who work in computing, information, and data sciences impact society. COMP 351 provides an ethical foundation, a framework for ethical considerations, and an opportunity for increased awareness of ethical challenges in light of changes in society where automation of information processing and data-based decision making is becoming prevalent, and its impacts can be far reaching.

The objective of this course is to support your understanding of how the choices of those creating systems and accessing data as part of their work in computing and data science impact society. A foundation in ethical thinking supports computing and data science practitioners to make thoughtful choices in their approaches to and implementation of algorithms and data-driven systems. The course then dives deeper into cases of ethical uses of data and ethical uses of models and systems. The course concludes with a review of current and future ethical issues in computing and information systems.

COMP 351 is organized into four parts:

- **Part 1: Foundations** is complemented by small quizzes that will prepare you for the rest of the course.
- **Part 2: About the Data** builds understanding of ethical issues created by the selection and collection of data.
- **Part 3: About Models and (Mis)Use** is all about what is implemented. It builds understanding of ethical issues created by developers and users.
- **Part 4: Ethical Challenges and More** describes present and future ethical issues in computing and information systems.

---

### Learning Outcomes

Upon successful completion of this course, you should be able to:

- explain the interconnected and interdisciplinary nature of data used in computing and information systems.
- describe your own ethical position and its implications for decision making.
- develop and use foundational logical and critical-thinking skills.
- analyze the impacts (social, economic, cultural, environmental, and/or sustainability) of:
  - lapses in security and privacy of data.
  - data collection without a rationale or business case.
  - the use of an unsuitable analytic model / machine learning / artificial intelligence technique.
  - poor communication in reporting results.
- predict and evaluate near-future issues in the ethical relationships between humans and data-driven technologies.

#### 2.1 Why Bother with Ethics and Philosophy?

Although determining right and wrong (moral philosophy) is a part of ethics, ethical considerations cover more. What is right and wrong depends on your cultural context; what is acceptable *here* may not be acceptable *there*. So, the ability to understand a situation from different contexts is something that ethics provides.

AND

Ethics change with time as humans become more educated and innovative; as society evolves, so too must the ethics it employs. The development of technologies changes the way society thinks about the work, which means right and wrong are reconsidered; what is acceptable *today* may not be acceptable *tomorrow*.

AND

When applying ethical thought, intentions and consequences from a number of perspectives are considered. This can be advantageous not just in building understanding of situations, but also in detecting risks not previously considered.

> **Optional Reading:** Consider reading Juan Enriquez's eyebrow-raising introduction to the topic of ethics in his book *Right/Wrong: How Technology Transforms Our Ethics*.

Computer, data, and information scientists—or those in related fields—heavily depend on data, its computations, and its outcomes. However, few people are specialists in ethics, philosophy, and governance. This highlights a need for computer, data, and information scientists to be ethically aware, and attempt to not compound the already difficult job humans have living harmoniously in society.

In this course, the interest is in ethics not as a discipline, but as an approach to help understand what decisions need to be made in the work of computer, data, and information scientists. Living and working in a technology-prevalent society is changing the types of ethical questions needing to be addressed.

Ethical considerations change as society changes, and in current times, change is rapid. To handle the shifting ethical ground, moral philosophy can support and provide stable tools for thinking about ethics.

> **Note:** Ethical thoughts of religion, free will, and faith will not be considered in this course. Your perspectives on these topics will guide you to determine your own personal philosophical perspective.

> **Required Reading:** Download and read Chapter 2: Introduction to Ethics in Michael J. Quinn's *Ethics for the Information Age*.

Before proceeding, it's helpful to understand what you think. Begin building your understanding of your personal moral philosophy (the process of figuring out right and wrong in choosing an action) before moving on.

#### 3.2 Rules

The rules used in CS and IS worlds are the common laws of the country in which one is residing, the country in which your organization is based, and professional guidance.

Professional ethics codes and guides are presented in terms of codes of conduct, regulations, or professional norms. Some examples include:

- The Association for Computing Machinery Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct
- The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers Code of Ethics
- The Canadian Information Processing Society Code of Ethics
- The NeurIPS Code of Ethics

Examples of common law and policy associated with CS and IS include:

- **In Canada:**
  - The Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA) and other privacy laws in Canada
  - The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA), part of Bill C-27, the Digital Charter Implementation Act, 2022
- **In the United States of America:**
  - The USA Patriot Act
- **In the European Union:**
  - The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
  - The AI Act
- **In other areas:**
  - Free Software Foundation and its history
  - Open Source

In addition to published rules (i.e., laws and policy), people have role-based rules and personal rules; each of these different rules connect decisions individuals make to actions they take. You may work with a variety of different roles and rules that influence you, for example:

- Personal ethical philosophy
- Professional code of conduct
- Organization culture and expectations
- Manager responsibilities
- C-level responsibilities, including fiduciary responsibility to stakeholders

These differing perspectives can cause confusion and slow decision making. To handle conflicts in the rules, you need to be able to think critically to find the most suitable approach for the context.

#### 3.3 Critical Thinking Skills

- differentiating between facts vs. opinions and/or beliefs.
- avoiding bias and fallacies (beliefs presented as truths).
- considering the counter position of the information.

#### 4.1 Where Does Data Come From?

The simplistic answer to this question is potentially from any digitally stored data, including anything on the internet, social media, health records, surveillance, text sources (e.g., Google's ngram project). Some data is accessed legally, some illegally, and some immorally.

Consider an infamous data capture case: Cambridge Analytica's use of Facebook data in April 2018. Cambridge Analytica used data captured by Facebook about their users. Many users were unaware that their data was being re-used for political campaign targeting. This was legal because users had agreed to Facebook's data sharing terms and conditions. Rarely do users read all terms and conditions, or they choose not to deny themselves a service they would like to use because there are limited opt-in and opt-out options for terms and conditions.

The phrase "if you are not paying for a service, you are the product" is well known but true. Each user has a monetary value to these companies (e.g., in 2020, each user was worth $182 to Google, $158 to Facebook, and $733 for Amazon[^1]).

[^1]: Shah, C. (2020). *A hands-on introduction to data science*. Cambridge University Press.

---

#### 4.2 Who Owns Data?

Who owns data, and why does it matter? Consider the value of data sovereignty for the individual and society.

Dr. Stephanie Russo Caroll talks about data sovereignty from an Indigenous point of view. Her points about staying connected to your own data and the impact of becoming disconnected from data are relevant to all.

Data scraping and deep fakes are an example of challenges to data ownership and sovereignty. For example, in December 2023, the New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft for copyright infringement of their published data. In May 2024, Open AI's voice for ChatGPT sounded very similar to that of actor Scarlett Johansson without her permission.

---

#### 4.3 Why Big Data Became a Game Changer

It's not just the data itself but the volume of data, through the ubiquitous action of collection, that makes big data a game changer. Coupled with increased storage and processing speeds, the potential for analyzing data seems unending.

There are technologies freely available that collect internet data: crawlers, spiders, scrapers, etc. Basically, tech helping tech feed tech.

It has been suggested that controls be focused earlier in the process on the ethics of data collection rather than solely on protecting and securing data after collection.

When ethically collecting data, questions about the need for the capture of data, the purpose of the collection, and the duration of collection should all be considered. The pre-2010s strategy of capturing everything in case it's useful is now ethically questionable.

> **Required Reading:** Review the Guide for Ethical Data Science from the UK's Royal Statistical Society (RSS) and the Institute and Faculty of Actuaries (IFoA). It includes an implementation checklist that is particularly useful.

#### 4.4 Ensuring Quality of Data

Although not a new topic, data preprocessing and cleaning is an important topic. Questions to uncover ethical issues in the decisions made when preprocessing and cleaning data include:

- What does preprocessing and cleaning choices mean for data quality?
- How does the "cleaned" data set impact system outcomes?

It is worth remembering that data inaccuracies and bias can have real world impacts. In nursing, "research has direct implications for practice and changes could be introduced without further studies being conducted."[^2]

Even published standard test data sets have come under scrutiny and are fraught with bias and proxies for bias (e.g., postcodes and electoral wards can be reliable indicators of economic status or ethnicity). Software that checks for bias in data or software that can un-bias is now available. Consider the following questions:

- What data remains in the data set?
- What data is removed?
- Are any data gaps existent, and how are they detected?
- Is data generated to complete the data set?
- What are the parameters for allowing data generation?

[^2]: Hayes. P. (2004). The ethics of cleaning data. *Clinical Nursing Research*, 13(2), 95–97. https://doi.org/10.1177/1054773804263173.

When automated decision making uses biased data, statistically significant bias already in the data impacts the decision making more overtly than when humans used the same data. The bias becomes obvious and can be recorded.

#### 6.1 Data Security and Privacy

By law, data must be protected in two ways. Data must be held:

- **securely.** This means an organization must keep data it holds protected from anyone who should not have access to it. This can refer to people external to an organization or internally within an organization depending on their work role.
- **privately.** This means that data about an individual belongs to that individual. The data should only be stored with the individual's consent, and it should not be shared without their consent.

Ethically, data should only be collected for a specific purpose (i.e., not speculatively or just in case). This means that data cannot be neutral. As soon as data has been collected and applied (e.g., to answer a question, feed an algorithm, make a decision, etc.), it ceases to be neutral.

#### 7.3 Does the Algorithm Meet its Intent?

Designing algorithms needs careful and conscious decision making. When developing decision points or establishing training data, you must consider who is making the decision about what is **correct** and what is **desired as the output**. This may not be one person; it can be an organization, a political culture, or the popular consensus.

Since its inception, decision making by algorithm has always been open to query. Since big data and machine learning have become usual, transparency and explainability have become more complex to achieve. The desire for transparency is written into the EU's GDPR. It requires that organizations be able to explain their algorithmic decisions.

Algorithmic audit can inform system preference. An audit is not an automated correctness process; it must take a wider perspective that is interdisciplinary and holistic.

The main considerations of an algorithmic audit include:

- **bias:** the risk that the system treats individuals or groups unfairly
- **efficacy:** the risk that a system underperforms relative to its intended use case, particularly on unseen data
- **robustness:** the risk that a system fails in response to changes in datasets or adversarial attacks by malicious actors
- **privacy:** the risk that a system is sensitive to personal or critical data leakage or facilitates the reverse engineering of such data
- **explainability:** the risk that an AI system is not understandable to users and developers caused by not effectively communicating important information[^7]

The stages of an audit (Table 7.1):

| Stage | Explainability | Robustness | Fairness/Bias | Privacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Data and task setup | Data collection and labelling | Data accuracy | Population balance | DPIA |
| Feature pre-processing | Dictionary of variables | Feature engineering | Fair representations | Data minimization |
| Model selection | Model complexity | Model validation | Fairness constraints | Differential privacy |
| Post-processing and reporting | Auxiliary tools | Adversarial testing | Bias metric assessment | Model inversion |
| Productionizing and deploying | Interface and documentation | Concept drift detection and continuous integration | Real-time monitoring of bias metrics | Rate-limiting and user's queries management |

Despite multiple proposals, a standard framework does not yet exist.

#### 8.2 Setting Boundaries for Algorithmic-Based Digital Technologies

Ethical issues that need guardrails can be summarized as:

1. **Transparency requirements:** Mandating clear disclosure about system capabilities, limitations, and decision-making processes
2. **Privacy protection:** Implementing strict data anonymization and consent mechanisms to prevent unauthorized personal information usage
3. **Safety protocols and bias mitigation:** Developing rigorous testing protocols to identify and minimize discriminatory algorithmic outputs across diverse populations, and creating comprehensive guidelines to prevent potential misuse in sensitive domains like healthcare, finance, and critical infrastructure
4. **Accountability mechanisms:** Establishing legal and regulatory frameworks to assign responsibility for outcomes and potential harms
5. **Societal impact:** Public education, public discourse and consultation, reporting mechanisms of misuse, and impact analysis of technologies now and in the future

Legal, operational, and social boundaries for algorithmic-based technologies are evolving as technologies develop. There are legal frameworks, such as:

- **Data protection laws.** Regulations like GDPR and CCPA set strict requirements for AI systems handling personal data, including rights to explanation and deletion.
- **Sector-specific regulations.** Special rules for AI in high-stakes areas like healthcare (FDA guidelines), financial services (algorithmic trading rules), and autonomous vehicles.
- **AI-specific legislation.** The EU AI Act and similar emerging laws categorize AI systems by risk level and impose corresponding obligations.

In Canada, specifically, the Government of Canada has:

- The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA).
- The Guide on the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence.
- The Algorithmic Impact Assessment Tool.

Social and operational controls include:

1. **Deployment restrictions and human rights**
   - Prohibited use cases (e.g., social scoring or profiling based on restricted data)
   - Controlled application in critical operational sectors
   - Geographic restrictions based on local laws
   - Public consultation on AI policy and practice
   - Disclosure of AI use and interactions with AI in services

2. **Responsible human oversight**
   - Professional codes of conduct (e.g., IEEE, ACM, BCS)
   - Ethics review boards
   - Educational development to support informed use of AI
   - Required human supervision for high-risk applications
   - Clear chains of responsibility
   - Intervention protocols for system failures
   - Open reporting of AI impacts

3. **Transparency and technical safeguards**
   - Detailed model documentation
   - Training data transparency
   - Decision-making audit trails
   - Built-in limitations on system capabilities
   - Kill switches and emergency controls
   - Regular security updates and vulnerability assessments

4. **Testing, verification, and evaluation**
   - Mandatory bias and fairness assessments
   - Regular safety validation
   - Performance benchmarking against established standards
   - Independent auditors and monitors

#### 9.6 Specific Ethical Issues Around Societal Impact

##### Fairness and Value Alignment

Technology has made both the creation and distribution of information faster and more widespread. This is both of value (open, accurate, and transparent) and problematic (open and inaccurate or non transparent).

Creating and distributing believable disinformation, misinformation, and malinformation are common mechanisms of influence over societal decision making and norms.

- **Misinformation** is information that is not true. It is generally not intended to cause harm, but it can. Deepfakes are misinformation. Social media echo-chambers make misinformation hard to correct.

- **Disinformation** is deceptive information. It is intended to be manipulative and can cause harm. It is not necessarily false but is used in a context other than the one originally intended. The intent is to guide the reader to a specific belief or direction. Forms include:
  - Authentic material used in the wrong context
  - Imposter news sites designed to look like brands we already know
  - Fake news sites
  - Fake information
  - Manipulated content
  - Parody content

- **Malinformation** is information that is not necessarily false in its origin but is exaggerated or promoted in a way that leads it to be false. Misunderstandings about mRNA technology led to trust conversations about vaccines, which was then promoted to malinformation by influencers.

Issues with fairness can raise ethical questions, but fairness is hard to define because it is an alignment problem and is dependent on situational context. Fairness issues can arise from data, cognitive bias, and systemic bias.

Because of this, context is very important in determining ethical use. It is not just what happens, but how it is done that must be considered. As a consequence of the changes Dr. Franklin identified, she also observed that "global reformation of major social forces and the social contract… would be necessary… for the security of the world and citizens."[^11]

#### 9.7 Trust in Computer-Based Systems

Although not a specific ethical concern, trust is a very important factor in the human-technology relationship. Misplaced trust in a system often allows ethical issues to go unchallenged or—worse—unnoticed.

The accuracy of computer-generated information is important when the information generated is critical, such as legislation, medical records, etc. Computer-based systems usually undergo quality assurance testing, which involves checks that the systems work as designed (validation) and that they generate the correct results (veracity). The deterministic nature of computing up until the 2010s has led to a trust in technology-driven outcomes.

> **Required Reading:** Read Peter V. Coveney and Roger R. Highfield's article about trusting computers.

With the release of AI based in probabilistic reasoning, this trust may now be misplaced. The fluency and plausibility of the suggested outcome are the main factors leading to a proposed outcome being taken as truthful. If an outcome is not a factual outcome, then it is called a *hallucination* or a *confabulation*.

#### 10.2 AI and Robot Moral Rights

##### Understanding Anthropomorphism

*Anthropomorphism* is the tendency of humans to treat non-living objects and systems like sentient beings. Even with very simple systems, the power of human emotionality and attachment are evident. For example, some humans name their cars, robot vacuums, etc.

Consider the work of Dr. Kate Darling, a robot ethicist at MIT, who researches human-robot interactions.

The more human-like computer systems become, the more likely it is for humans to treat these systems like sentient beings (i.e., anthropomorphize them). This behaviour is likely to lead humanity towards the consideration of rights for AI agents.

##### AI Rights

What rights might AI systems have in the future if or when they become autonomous beings? A symposium session from the University of Toronto's Centre for Ethics had presenters discuss whether AI should be provided moral consideration and have moral protection.

##### Emotion and Empathy in AI Agents

Emotion detection is a current ethical issue. Detecting emotion or sentiment provides digital systems with information about human status that is not consciously given. Future ethics will need to contend with AI agents that can both perceive and respond empathically.

> **Required Reading:** Read Catrin Misselhorn's article in *The Conversation* about empathic machines.

When humans can create virtual worlds at will, such as in a metaverse, humans will have removed some certainties of the physical world by providing more options. Such change is not without ethical question.

##### Brain-Computer Interfaces (BCI)

Medical initiatives using brain-computer interfaces are underway at the clinical trial level. For example, the University of Calgary and the Alberta Children's Hospital have integrated BCI for a patient with cerebral palsy to allow him to control his wheelchair.
