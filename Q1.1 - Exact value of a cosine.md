## 0. The 30-second version

If you only remember one thing, remember this chain.

```
cos(-pi/12)
  -> cos is EVEN, so drop the minus sign          cos(pi/12)
  -> pi/12 is 15 deg, and 15 = 45 - 30            cos(pi/4 - pi/6)
  -> cos(A-B) = cosA cosB + sinA sinB             (r2/2)(r3/2) + (r2/2)(1/2)
  -> combine over 4                               (r6 + r2)/4
```

That is four lines, about twenty seconds, and no calculator.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is question 1 of the midterm and it is worth 5 of 64 points, but it is not a warm-up.
It is a gate.
The examiner is checking three separate reflexes at once, and each one of them reappears later in the paper.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| Parity of the trig functions (cos is even, sin is odd) | limits at negative arguments, symmetry in curve sketching |
| Decomposing a non-standard angle into standard ones | trig limits, the derivative-from-definition question |
| Producing an *exact* surd rather than a decimal | every "exact value" instruction on the paper |

The phrase "exact value" is the whole instruction.
It means no decimals, no rounding, no calculator output.
An answer of `0.9659` is wrong even though it is numerically close, because the question asked for a

### 2.1 The angle table: every angle that has an exact value

This table holds every multiple of $15^\circ$ from $0$ to $360$, plus every odd multiple of
$22.5^\circ$ ($\pi/8$).
That is 33 rows: 25 from the 15-degree family and 8 from the 22.5-degree family.

**Every row here has an exact value, and that is the point of the table.**
These 33 angles are, essentially, the complete list of angles an "exact value" question can use.
An angle that is not in this table, such as $5^\circ$, $10^\circ$ or $20^\circ$, has no expression in
square roots at all; it needs cube roots, because a regular 72-sided polygon is not constructible.
So if a question hands you an angle, the first thing to check is whether it is in this table.
If it is not, you have misread the question or the angle needs reducing first.

Four things about the columns before you read a single number.

All six functions are here, in the order $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$, $\csc$, $\sec$, $\cot$, so an
"exact value" question about any of the six is one lookup rather than a lookup and a division.
Two of the six are marked **(even)**, and that mark is the whole reason Q1.1 opens the way it does:
$\cos(-x)=\cos x$ and $\sec(-x)=\sec x$, so on those two columns a minus sign inside the bracket
evaporates before you do anything else.
The other four are odd, and pull the minus out in front of the answer instead.

The $\cos(-x)$ column is **identical to the $\cos x$ column on every row**.
That is not a printing mistake, it is that same parity fact made visual.
It is here so your eye learns it, because that is the first move of Q1.1.

$\csc x=\dfrac{1}{\sin x}$ and $\sec x=\dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, so those two columns are the sine and
cosine columns turned upside down, sign and all, because a reciprocal keeps the sign of whatever it
came from.
Their absolute value is never less than $1$, since sine and cosine never exceed $1$, and that is a
free sanity check on any secant or cosecant you write down.
They also stop looking like their parents once you rationalise: $\dfrac{4}{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}$ becomes
$\sqrt6+\sqrt2$, so the reciprocal columns carry the same surds as the sine and cosine columns but
without the quarter, and $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}$ becomes $\sqrt{4+2\sqrt2}$.

$\cot x = \dfrac{1}{\tan x} = \dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}$, so the tangent and cotangent columns are
reciprocals of each other, and each is undefined exactly where the other is zero.
$\tan$ and $\sec$ are undefined at $90^\circ$ and $270^\circ$, where cosine is zero.
$\cot$ and $\csc$ are undefined at $0^\circ$, $180^\circ$ and $360^\circ$, where sine is zero.

The practical way to use the table is never to memorise 33 rows: reduce your angle to a reference angle

Two symmetries worth using as checks.
Sine is a mirror image of itself about $90^\circ$, because $\sin(180-x)=\sin x$, which is why rows
$15$ and $165$ share a sine and rows $22.5$ and $157.5$ share one.
And the whole table repeats with both signs flipped after $180^\circ$, because $\sin(x+180)=-\sin x$
and $\cos(x+180)=-\cos x$, which is why row $195$ carries row $15$'s surds with flipped signs.
Tangent and cotangent, by contrast, repeat every $180^\circ$ with **no** sign change, which is why
rows $15$ and $195$ have the identical tangent $2-\sqrt3$.

For an angle outside $[0,360]$, subtract or add full turns until it lands in the table.
$\dfrac{25\pi}{12}$ is $375^\circ$, which is coterminal with $15^\circ$.
For a negative angle, use parity first: cosine and secant drop the sign, everything else pulls it out
in front.

### 2.2 Parity

| Even (drop the minus) | Odd (pull the minus out front) |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\cos(-x)=\cos x$ | $\sin(-x)=-\sin x$ |
| $\sec(-x)=\sec x$ | $\tan(-x)=-\tan x$ |
| | $\csc(-x)=-\csc x$, $\cot(-x)=-\cot x$ |

Two even, four odd.
Cosine and its reciprocal are the even pair, everything else is odd.

### 2.3 Quadrant signs (ASTC)

Quadrant I: **A**ll positive.
Quadrant II: **S**ine only.
Quadrant III: **T**angent only.
Quadrant IV: **C**osine only.

Reduce any angle to its reference angle first, get the magnitude, then attach the sign from ASTC.
The reference angle is the acute angle to the nearest part of the $x$-axis, never to the $y$-axis.

### 2.4 The two formula families

This is the reference card only.
Sections 3A, 3B and 3C below are the actual tutorials for the three tools, with worked examples.

**Sum and difference.**

$$\cos(A\mp B)=\cos A\cos B \pm \sin A\sin B$$

$$\sin(A\pm B)=\sin A\cos B \pm \cos A\sin B$$

$$\tan(A\pm B)=\frac{\tan A\pm\tan B}{1\mp\tan A\tan B}$$

The cosine one is the trap: its middle sign is the *opposite* of the sign in the bracket.
So $\cos(A-B)$ has a **plus** in the middle.
Say "cosine flips" out loud every time you write it.

**Half angle.**

$$\cos\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}},\qquad
\sin\frac{\theta}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\theta}{2}}$$

The $\pm$ is not decoration.
You choose the sign from the quadrant of $\theta/2$, not of $\theta$.

## 3. The universal procedure

Every "exact value" question on this course is the same four moves.
Learn the moves, not the answers.

> **Step 1 - Strip.**
> Kill the minus sign with parity.
> Reduce anything past $2\pi$ by subtracting full turns.
> Note which quadrant the surviving angle lives in, and hold that sign in your head.
>
> **Step 2 - Classify.**
> Look at the denominator of the angle.
> That single number picks your tool.
>
> | Angle looks like | Tool |
> | :--- | :--- |
> | multiple of $\pi/6$, $\pi/4$, $\pi/3$, $\pi/2$ | straight recall, no formula |
> | odd multiple of $\pi/12$ (15, 75, 105, 165, ...) | **sum or difference formula** (Section 3A) |
> | odd multiple of $\pi/8$ (22.5, 67.5, ...) | **half-angle formula** (Section 3B) |
> | odd multiple of $\pi/24$ or worse | half-angle applied twice |
> | contains $\arcsin$, $\arccos$, $\arctan$ | **reference triangle** (Section 3C), no formula |
>
> **Step 3 - Substitute.**
> Write the formula out in full symbols first, then substitute.
> Never substitute into a half-remembered formula.
>
> **Step 4 - Simplify and sanity-check.**
> Put everything over one denominator.
> Then run the decimal check in Section 6 before you write the answer down.

For Q1.1: Step 1 gives $\cos(\pi/12)$, quadrant I so positive.
Step 2 sees $\pi/12$ and picks the difference formula.
Steps 3 and 4 are below.

## 3A. Tool 1 - the sum and difference formulas

**Use it when:** the angle is an odd multiple of $\pi/12$, which in degrees means it ends in
$15$ or $75$ ($15, 75, 105, 165, 195, 255, 285, 345$).

### What the tool is for

The unit circle only hands you a small set of angles for free: multiples of $30$, $45$, $60$ and $90$.
A sum or difference formula is the bridge from that small set to an angle just outside it.
The entire idea is: *if I cannot reach this angle directly, can I reach it as a sum or a difference of
two angles I already know?*

$15^\circ$ is the classic case, because $15 = 45 - 30$ and both of those are free.

### The formulas

$$\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B \qquad\qquad \cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B$$

$$\sin(A+B)=\sin A\cos B+\cos A\sin B \qquad\qquad \sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B$$

$$\tan(A+B)=\frac{\tan A+\tan B}{1-\tan A\tan B} \qquad\qquad \tan(A-B)=\frac{\tan A-\tan B}{1+\tan A\tan B}$$

### The sign discipline, which is where the marks go

This is the only hard part of the tool, so make it a slogan rather than a memory.

> **Cosine flips. Sine follows. Tangent flips downstairs.**

- **Cosine flips:** the sign in the middle of the expansion is the *opposite* of the sign in the
  bracket. $\cos(A-B)$ has a plus. $\cos(A+B)$ has a minus.
- **Sine follows:** the sign in the middle matches the bracket. $\sin(A-B)$ has a minus.
- **Tangent flips downstairs:** the numerator matches the bracket, and the denominator is the opposite.

There is also a structural tell that catches a misremembered formula instantly.
In the cosine expansion the two terms are *like with like*: $\cos\cos$ and $\sin\sin$.
In the sine expansion they are *mixed*: $\sin\cos$ and $\cos\sin$.
If you have written $\cos(A-B)=\cos A\sin B + \ldots$ you have already gone wrong, whatever the sign is.

### Where the formulas come from

You are not asked to prove these, but knowing the shape means you can rebuild one if you blank.
Think of $\cos(A-B)$ as the cosine of the angle *between* two unit vectors, one at angle $A$ and one at
angle $B$.
Those vectors are $(\cos A,\sin A)$ and $(\cos B,\sin B)$, and the cosine of the angle between two unit
vectors is just their dot product:

$$\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B$$

That is the difference formula, derived in one line, and it explains why the cosine version pairs
like with like: a dot product multiplies first components together and second components together.
Every other formula in the family follows from this one by replacing $B$ with $-B$ and using parity.

### How to find the decomposition

Do not hunt. Work backwards from the target with this procedure.

> 1. Convert the target angle to degrees. Radians hide the arithmetic.
> 2. Write the free list: every multiple of $30$ and every multiple of $45$, so
>    $30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 135, 150, 180, 210, 225, 240, 270, 300, 315, 330$.
> 3. Find a pair from the free list whose sum or difference is your target.
> 4. **Verify the split in fractions before you use it.** One line, and it prevents the worst class of
>    error, which is a correct formula applied to a wrong split.

**There are only eight targets in the whole course.**
This tool applies to angles ending in $15$ or $75$, and between $0$ and $360$ there are exactly eight
of them. Here is every one, with every usable split.

### Read the last column, then do the least work

The "faster route" column is the recommendation, and it changes the shape of the job.

**Only two of the eight targets ever need this formula.**
For $15^\circ$ and $75^\circ$ you have no choice, because they are their own reference angles and there
is nothing to reduce. For the other six, reducing to a reference angle of $15^\circ$ or $75^\circ$ and
taking the sign from ASTC is strictly less work than expanding a sum formula: two moves instead of a
four-term expansion with a sign trap in the middle.

So the real procedure for this family is:

> Reduce to a reference angle first.
> If it comes out as $15$ or $75$, look the magnitude up or compute it once with the formula, then
> attach the sign from ASTC.
> Only expand a sum or difference formula when you are actually sitting on $15^\circ$ or $75^\circ$.

Worked both ways on $\cos 165^\circ$, so you can see the difference.

*Via a split:* $165=120+45$, so
$\cos 120\cos 45-\sin 120\sin 45=\left(-\frac12\right)\frac{\sqrt2}{2}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}
=-\frac{\sqrt2+\sqrt6}{4}$. Four terms, two of them signed, one sign trap.

*Via a reference angle:* $165=180-15$, so the magnitude is $\cos 15^\circ=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$, and
quadrant II makes the cosine negative, giving $-\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$. Two moves.

Same answer, verified numerically as $-0.9659$.

### What this tool cannot reach, and why

No sum or difference of free angles will ever give you $22.5^\circ$, $67.5^\circ$ or any of that family.
Every angle on the free list is a multiple of $15$, and adding or subtracting multiples of $15$ can only
ever produce another multiple of $15$.
$22.5$ is not one, so the arithmetic is closed against you.
That is not a gap in your memory, it is a fact about the tool, and it is exactly why Section 3B exists.

Two more boundaries worth stating.
For a target outside $[0,360]$, reduce by full turns first: $375^\circ$ is coterminal with $15^\circ$.
For a negative target, apply parity first, which is precisely the first move of Q1.1.

### Worked example 1: $\sin\dfrac{5\pi}{12}$

**Classify.** Denominator 12, so this is the sum-and-difference family.

**Split.** $\dfrac{5\pi}{12}$ is $75^\circ$, and $75 = 45 + 30$.
Verify: $\dfrac{\pi}{4}+\dfrac{\pi}{6}=\dfrac{3\pi+2\pi}{12}=\dfrac{5\pi}{12}$. Good.

**Substitute.** Sine follows, so a plus bracket keeps a plus, and the terms are mixed:

$$\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$

$$=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac12=\frac{\sqrt6}{4}+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}
=\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}}$$

**Check.** $\approx 0.9659$. $75^\circ$ is close to $90^\circ$ where sine is 1, so a value just under 1
is right. Verified numerically.

### Worked example 2: $\cos\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$, where the answer is negative

**Classify.** Denominator 12 again.

**Split.** $\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$ is $105^\circ$. It is past $90^\circ$, so no difference of two free
angles under $90$ will reach it. Use $105 = 60 + 45$.
Verify: $\dfrac{\pi}{3}+\dfrac{\pi}{4}=\dfrac{4\pi+3\pi}{12}=\dfrac{7\pi}{12}$. Good.

**Substitute.** Cosine flips, so a **plus** bracket gives a **minus** in the middle, like with like:

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}-\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4}$$

$$=\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}=\frac{\sqrt2}{4}-\frac{\sqrt6}{4}
=\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt2-\sqrt6}{4}}$$

**Check.** $\approx -0.2588$, and it must be negative because $105^\circ$ is in quadrant II where
cosine is negative. The sign came out of the algebra and it agrees with ASTC, which is the check
working. Verified numerically.

Note the shape of the answer: $\dfrac{\sqrt2-\sqrt6}{4}$, with the smaller root first.
Writing it as $-\dfrac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}$ is the same number and equally correct.

### Worked example 3: $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{12}$, and why you might skip the formula

**With the tangent formula.** $15 = 45-30$, and tangent flips downstairs, so a minus bracket puts a
plus in the denominator:

$$\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\frac{\tan\frac{\pi}{4}-\tan\frac{\pi}{6}}{1+\tan\frac{\pi}{4}\tan\frac{\pi}{6}}
=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}$$

Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt3$ to clear the inner fractions:

$$=\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}$$

That is exact but not simplified, so rationalise by multiplying top and bottom by $\sqrt3-1$:

$$=\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{(\sqrt3)^2-1^2}=\frac{3-2\sqrt3+1}{2}=\frac{4-2\sqrt3}{2}=\boxed{2-\sqrt3}$$

**Without it.** If you already have $\sin\frac{\pi}{12}$ and $\cos\frac{\pi}{12}$, just divide:

$$\tan\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}
\ \xrightarrow{\ \times\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}\ }\
\frac{8-4\sqrt3}{4}=2-\sqrt3$$

Both routes verified numerically as $0.2679$.
Dividing is usually faster in the exam, because it reuses work you have already done and needs one
formula fewer.

### The four errors that actually happen

| Error | What it looks like | Guard |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Wrong middle sign | $\cos(A-B)$ written with a minus | say "cosine flips" as you write it |
| Wrong pairing | $\cos(A-B)=\cos A\sin B+\ldots$ | cosine is like-with-like, sine is mixed |
| Unverified split | using $45-30$ for $105^\circ$ | check the fraction arithmetic in one line |
| Stopping too early | leaving $\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}$ | a surd in a denominator is not finished |

## 3B. Tool 2 - the half-angle formula

**Use it when:** the angle is an odd multiple of $\pi/8$, which in degrees means it ends in
$22.5$ or $67.5$ ($22.5, 67.5, 112.5, 157.5$).

### What the tool is for

Tool 1 fails here, and it fails for a concrete reason: **no sum or difference of standard angles can
produce $22.5^\circ$.** Every standard angle is a multiple of $15^\circ$, and adding or subtracting
multiples of 15 can only ever give you another multiple of 15. $22.5$ is not one.

So you need a tool that *halves* rather than adds, and $22.5$ is exactly half of $45$.

That gives you the classification rule in one line:
**denominator 12 means add or subtract, denominator 8 means halve.**

### Where the formula comes from

Derive it rather than memorise it, because the derivation is three lines and the formula has a
$\pm$ that memory tends to drop.

Start from the double-angle identity for cosine, in the two forms that contain only one function each:

$$\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \cos 2\theta = 1 - 2\sin^2\theta$$

Solve the first for $\cos^2\theta$ and the second for $\sin^2\theta$:

$$\cos^2\theta = \frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2} \qquad\qquad \sin^2\theta = \frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}$$

Now rename. Let $\theta = \dfrac{\varphi}{2}$, so $2\theta = \varphi$, and take the square root:

$$\cos\frac{\varphi}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}} \qquad\qquad
\sin\frac{\varphi}{2}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\varphi}{2}}$$

Two things fall out of that derivation, and both are examinable.

The **$\pm$ is the price of the square root.** Squaring destroyed the sign, so taking the root cannot
recover it, and you have to supply it from outside the algebra.

**Cosine gets the plus, sine gets the minus**, inside the fraction.
If you mix those up, check against a value you know: at $\varphi = 0$, $\cos 0 = 1$, so
$\cos\frac{0}{2}$ should be $\sqrt{\frac{1+1}{2}}=1$, which is correct, while the sine version gives
$\sqrt{0}=0$, also correct. That one substitution settles which is which in five seconds.

### Choosing the sign, which is the whole difficulty

> **The sign comes from the quadrant of the half angle $\dfrac{\varphi}{2}$, the angle you are
> computing. It does not come from $\varphi$.**

This is the single most common half-angle error, so make it a separate written step.
Before you touch the algebra, write down "the half angle is $X^\circ$, which is in quadrant $Q$, so the
sign is $\pm$".

| you are computing | half angle lands in | $\cos$ | $\sin$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cos 22.5^\circ$ | QI | $+$ | $+$ |
| $\cos 67.5^\circ$ | QI | $+$ | $+$ |
| $\cos 112.5^\circ$ | QII | $-$ | $+$ |
| $\cos 157.5^\circ$ | QII | $-$ | $+$ |

### The answer form: leave the nested radical alone

A half-angle answer is a **nested radical**, a root inside a root, like
$\dfrac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}$.
That is the correct and expected final form for the $\pi/8$ family.
Do not try to flatten it.
It usually cannot be flattened, and the attempt is where time and marks disappear.

The contrast with Tool 1 is worth holding in your head: the $\pi/12$ family produces clean surds like
$\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$, and the $\pi/8$ family produces nested ones.
If you are staring at a nested radical for a $\pi/12$ angle, you probably reached for the wrong tool.

### Worked example 1: $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{8}$

**Classify.** Denominator 8, so halve.

**Set up.** $\dfrac{\pi}{8}$ is $22.5^\circ$, which is half of $45^\circ$, so $\varphi=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

**Sign first.** The half angle is $22.5^\circ$, quadrant I, cosine positive. Take the plus.

**Substitute.**

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{8}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos\frac{\pi}{4}}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}}$$

Clear the inner fraction by multiplying top and bottom inside the root by 2:

$$=\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx 0.9239$$

That last step is worth naming: $\sqrt{\dfrac{a}{4}}=\dfrac{\sqrt a}{2}$, because $\sqrt4=2$.
Pulling the 4 out of the root is what turns the answer into standard form.

**Check.** $22.5^\circ$ is a small angle, so its cosine should be a little under 1. $0.9239$ fits.

### Worked example 2: $\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$

$\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$ is $67.5^\circ$, which is half of $135^\circ$, so $\varphi=\dfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and
$\cos\varphi=-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$.
The half angle $67.5^\circ$ is in quadrant I, so sine is positive.

$$\sin\frac{3\pi}{8}=+\sqrt{\frac{1-\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}}
=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx0.9239$$

Watch the double negative in the first step: the formula has $1-\cos\varphi$, and $\cos\varphi$ is
itself negative, so the two minuses make a plus.
That is the second most common error in this tool, after the $\pm$.

Sanity check on the answer: $\sin 67.5^\circ$ should equal $\cos 22.5^\circ$, because
$67.5 + 22.5 = 90$ and sine and cosine are complements. They match. Verified numerically.

### Worked example 3: $\cos\dfrac{5\pi}{8}$, the case where the sign matters

$\dfrac{5\pi}{8}$ is $112.5^\circ$, half of $225^\circ$, so $\varphi=\dfrac{5\pi}{4}$ and
$\cos\varphi=-\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$.

**Sign first, and this is the point of the example.** The half angle is $112.5^\circ$, which is in
quadrant **II**, where cosine is **negative**. Take the minus.

$$\cos\frac{5\pi}{8}=-\sqrt{\frac{1+\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)}{2}}=-\sqrt{\frac{2-\sqrt2}{4}}
=-\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}\approx-0.3827$$

Had you taken $\varphi=225^\circ$ and reasoned "quadrant III, cosine negative" you would have got the
right sign here **by luck**. Reason from the half angle every time and it is never luck.
Verified numerically.

### The tangent half-angle, when you need it

There is a neater route for tangent that avoids nested radicals entirely:

$$\tan\frac{\varphi}{2}=\frac{\sin\varphi}{1+\cos\varphi}$$

For $\varphi=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$:

$$\tan\frac{\pi}{8}=\frac{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2+\sqrt2}
=\frac{\sqrt2(2-\sqrt2)}{(2+\sqrt2)(2-\sqrt2)}=\frac{2\sqrt2-2}{2}=\sqrt2-1\approx0.4142$$

No nested radical at all, and it carries its own sign, so there is no $\pm$ to choose.
Verified numerically.
This is the exception to "the $\pi/8$ family gives nested radicals": the tangents come out clean.

### The four errors that actually happen

| Error | What it looks like | Guard |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sign from the wrong angle | using $\varphi$'s quadrant | write "half angle is $X$, quadrant $Q$" as its own line |
| Lost double negative | $1-(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2})$ read as $1-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | bracket $\cos\varphi$ before subtracting |
| Never pulling out the 4 | leaving $\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}}$ | $\sqrt{a/4}=\frac{\sqrt a}{2}$, always finish it |
| Trying to denest | grinding at $\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}$ | nested is the answer for this family |

## 3C. Tool 3 - the reference triangle

**Use it when:** an inverse trig function appears inside the expression, such as
$\sin\!\left(\arccos\frac35\right)$ or $\tan(\arcsin x)$.

### What the tool is for

There is no identity for these, and looking for one is the trap.
The tool is a picture.

The unlock is realising what an inverse trig function *is*.
**$\arccos\frac35$ is not an operation waiting to happen. It is a number: an angle.**
Read it out loud as "the angle whose cosine is $\frac35$".

So the expression $\sin\!\left(\arccos\frac35\right)$ is really asking:

> There is some angle. I am telling you its cosine is $\frac35$. What is its sine?

Written that way it stops being an inverse-function problem and becomes a right-triangle problem, and
you have been able to do those since high school.
The inverse function hands you **one** of the three ratios.
The triangle plus Pythagoras gives you the other two.

### The procedure

> 1. **Name it.** Let $\theta$ be the inner angle, and write the defining equation.
>    $\theta = \arccos\frac35$ means $\cos\theta = \frac35$.
> 2. **Draw a right triangle** and label the two sides the ratio gives you, using SOH CAH TOA.
>    Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse, so $\text{adj}=3$ and $\text{hyp}=5$.
> 3. **Pythagoras** for the missing side.
> 4. **Read off** the ratio the outer function asks for.
> 5. **Fix the sign from the RANGE of the inverse function**, not from the triangle.
>    Side lengths in a drawing are always positive; the answer may not be.

Step 5 is the only step that is not mechanical, and it is where the marks are.

### Step 5 in detail: the range decides the sign

An inverse function has to return exactly one angle, so its output is restricted to one band.
Those bands are not the same for each function, and that is what decides your sign.

| function | range of the output angle | which quadrants | consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\arcsin$ | $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ | IV and I | output cosine is always $\ge 0$ |
| $\arctan$ | $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ | IV and I | output cosine is always $> 0$ |
| $\arccos$ | $\left[0,\pi\right]$ | I and II | output sine is always $\ge 0$ |

Read the "consequence" column as a shortcut, because it does most of the work:

- Inside an $\arcsin$ or $\arctan$, **cosine and secant come out positive**, whatever the sign of the input.
- Inside an $\arccos$, **sine and cosecant come out positive**, whatever the sign of the input.
- A negative input pushes $\arcsin$ and $\arctan$ into quadrant IV, and pushes $\arccos$ into quadrant II.

So: get the **magnitude** from the triangle, then get the **sign** from the quadrant that the range
forces. Two independent steps, never mixed.

### Worked example 1: $\sin\!\left(\arccos\dfrac35\right)$

**Name it.** Let $\theta=\arccos\dfrac35$, so $\cos\theta=\dfrac35$.
The input is positive and $\arccos$ lands in $[0,\pi]$, so $\theta$ is in quadrant I and everything
will be positive.

**Draw it.** Cosine is adjacent over hypotenuse:

```
                      /|
                     / |
              5     /  |
        (hyp)      /   |   opp = ?
                  /    |
                 / θ   |
                --------
                 adj = 3
```

**Pythagoras.** $\text{opp}=\sqrt{5^2-3^2}=\sqrt{25-9}=\sqrt{16}=4$.

This is the 3-4-5 triangle, and it turns up constantly. Recognising it saves the square root.

**Read off.** The outer function is sine, which is opposite over hypotenuse:

$$\sin\left(\arccos\frac35\right)=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}=\boxed{\dfrac45}$$

**Check.** $0.8$, and it is positive as the range demanded. Also $\left(\frac35\right)^2+\left(\frac45\right)^2=1$,
which is the Pythagorean identity confirming the pair is consistent. Verified numerically.

That last check is worth keeping: any $(\sin,\cos)$ pair you produce must satisfy
$\sin^2+\cos^2=1$. It is free and it catches a botched Pythagoras immediately.

### Worked example 2: $\tan\!\left(\arccos\!\left(-\dfrac23\right)\right)$, the sign case

**Name it.** Let $\theta=\arccos\!\left(-\dfrac23\right)$, so $\cos\theta=-\dfrac23$.

**Range first.** $\arccos$ lands in $[0,\pi]$, and the input is negative, so $\theta$ is in
**quadrant II**. In quadrant II sine is positive and tangent is **negative**. Expect a negative answer.

**Draw the magnitudes.** Use $|{\text{adj}}|=2$ and $\text{hyp}=3$:

$$\text{opp}=\sqrt{3^2-2^2}=\sqrt{9-4}=\sqrt5$$

**Read off, then apply the sign.** Tangent is opposite over adjacent. The magnitude is
$\dfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$, and quadrant II makes it negative:

$$\tan\left(\arccos\left(-\frac23\right)\right)=\boxed{-\dfrac{\sqrt5}{2}}\approx-1.1180$$

**Check.** Note what did *not* flip: $\sin\theta=+\dfrac{\sqrt5}{3}$ stays positive, because the range
of $\arccos$ guarantees a non-negative sine. Verified numerically.

If you had put $-2$ into the drawing as a side length and let the algebra carry it, you would get the
same answer here, but that habit breaks as soon as you need $\sin$ or a squared quantity.
Keep lengths positive and handle the sign separately.

### Worked example 3: $\sec(\arctan 2)$

**Name it.** $\theta=\arctan 2$, so $\tan\theta=2=\dfrac21$.
Tangent is opposite over adjacent, so $\text{opp}=2$ and $\text{adj}=1$.
$\arctan$ of a positive number is in quadrant I, so everything is positive.

$$\text{hyp}=\sqrt{2^2+1^2}=\sqrt5$$

Secant is hypotenuse over adjacent:

$$\sec(\arctan 2)=\frac{\sqrt5}{1}=\boxed{\sqrt5}\approx2.2361$$

Note the difference from example 1: with tangent you are given the two **legs** and must find the
hypotenuse, so Pythagoras **adds** instead of subtracting.
With sine or cosine you are given one leg and the hypotenuse, so it subtracts.
Getting that backwards produces $\sqrt{4-1}$ and a wrong answer that still looks plausible.

### Worked example 4: the algebraic version, $\tan(\arcsin x)$

Identical method, with a letter instead of a number.
Let $\theta=\arcsin x$, so $\sin\theta = \dfrac{x}{1}$: put $\text{opp}=x$ and $\text{hyp}=1$.

$$\text{adj}=\sqrt{1-x^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \tan(\arcsin x)=\boxed{\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}}$$

Two conditions come with it, and stating them is part of a full-credit answer.
The expression needs $1-x^2>0$, so $-1<x<1$, which is exactly where $\arcsin$ is defined and its
tangent is finite.
And the denominator is written as $+\sqrt{1-x^2}$ with no $\pm$, because the range of $\arcsin$ forces
the cosine to be non-negative.
Sanity check at $x=0.6$: the formula gives $\dfrac{0.6}{0.8}=0.75$, and $\tan(\arcsin 0.6)=0.75$.
Verified numerically.

### Worked example 5: when the outer function is doubled

$\cos\!\left(2\arcsin\dfrac13\right)$ combines this tool with a double-angle identity, and the shortcut
is to pick the identity form whose input you *already* have.

Let $\theta=\arcsin\dfrac13$, so $\sin\theta=\dfrac13$ by definition.
You know the **sine**, so choose the double-angle form written in sine only:

$$\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta=1-2\left(\frac13\right)^2=1-\frac29=\boxed{\dfrac79}\approx0.7778$$

You never drew a triangle and never computed $\cos\theta$ at all.
Choosing the right form of the identity is the entire saving.
The companion case does need the triangle: $\sin\!\left(2\arccos\frac35\right)$ uses
$\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, and the 3-4-5 triangle gives
$2\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac35=\dfrac{24}{25}$. Verified numerically.

### The four errors that actually happen

| Error | What it looks like | Guard |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Hunting for an identity | trying to expand $\sin(\arccos u)$ with a formula | there is none; draw the triangle |
| Sign from the triangle | reporting $+\frac{\sqrt5}{2}$ in example 2 | the range decides the sign, not the drawing |
| Pythagoras the wrong way | $\sqrt{4-1}$ in example 3 | given two legs it adds, given a leg and the hypotenuse it subtracts |
| Dropping the domain | $\frac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$ with no condition | state $-1<x<1$ as part of the answer |

## 4. Q1.1 worked three ways

All three routes are legitimate and all three give the same surd.
You should be able to run route A in your sleep, recognise route B, and keep route C in reserve.

### Route A - parity first, then the difference formula (use this one)

$$\cos\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{12}\qquad\text{(cosine is even)}$$

Now split $\dfrac{\pi}{12}$.
In degrees $15=45-30$, so

$$\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}$$

Check that split before you use it: $\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}=\dfrac{3\pi-2\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{12}$.
Good.

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{4}\cos\frac{\pi}{6}+\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\sin\frac{\pi}{6}$$

$$=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}
=\frac{\sqrt6}{4}+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}
=\boxed{\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}}$$

### Route B - difference formula without using parity

You do not have to use parity.
Keep the minus sign inside and split $-\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{6}-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$:

$$\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{6}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\sin\frac{\pi}{4}
=\frac{\sqrt6}{4}+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}$$

Same answer, which is the point.
Cosine does not care about the order of the subtraction, because it is even.
This route is a useful self-check but it is one more thing to get right under pressure.

Another valid split is $\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{3}-\dfrac{\pi}{4}$:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\sin\frac{\pi}{4}
=\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}
=\frac{\sqrt2}{4}+\frac{\sqrt6}{4}$$

Verified numerically: both splits give 0.9659258263.

### Route C - half angle from $\pi/6$

Since $\dfrac{\pi}{12}$ is half of $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, and $\dfrac{\pi}{12}$ sits in quadrant I so the
cosine is positive:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/6)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}}{2}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt3}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}$$

That is a correct exact value, and it is numerically 0.9659258263, but it is an ugly *nested* radical.
It equals the clean form:

$$\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}{2}=\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$$

Both were verified numerically to 1e-11.
Denesting that by hand is real work, so if a marker or a machine wants the tidy form you have paid
extra for no reason.

### Which route, and why

| | Route A (parity + difference) | Route B (difference, no parity) | Route C (half angle) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Lines of work | 4 | 4 | 5, plus denesting |
| Where errors hide | the middle sign of $\cos(A-B)$ | the middle sign, *and* the split direction | choosing the $\pm$, and the nested radical |
| Output form | clean surd | clean surd | nested radical |
| Verdict | **use this** | good cross-check | reserve for the $\pi/8$ family |

**Under exam pressure, use Route A.**
Half-angle is the right tool for $\pi/8$ angles, where no sum of standard angles will reach the target.
It is the wrong tool for $\pi/12$ angles, where a difference of standard angles lands exactly on it.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

If the paper you sit asks for method, these are the four sentences that earn the marks.
Copy the shape, not just the numbers.

> 1. Since cosine is an even function, $\cos\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)$.
> 2. Writing $\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ expresses the angle as a difference of two standard angles.
> 3. By the cosine difference identity $\cos(A-B)=\cos A\cos B+\sin A\sin B$,
>    $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{12}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}+\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\dfrac12$.
> 4. Therefore $\cos\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{12}\right)=\dfrac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}$.

Three things a marker looks for that students routinely skip: **naming** the parity property,
**naming** the identity used, and stating the decomposition as a deliberate choice rather than letting
$\dfrac{\pi}{4}-\dfrac{\pi}{6}$ appear from nowhere.
Those are free marks.

## 6. Verify before you commit

This takes eight seconds and it catches nearly every sign slip.

**The magnitude check.**
$\sqrt6\approx 2.449$ and $\sqrt2\approx 1.414$, so $(\sqrt6+\sqrt2)/4\approx 3.863/4\approx 0.966$.
Is that plausible?
$-\pi/12$ is $-15^\circ$, a tiny angle, and $\cos 0=1$, so the answer must be positive and a hair
under 1.
0.966 fits.

**The three cheap invariants.**

1. **Range.** Any $\sin$ or $\cos$ answer must sit in $[-1,1]$. If you produce $(\sqrt6+\sqrt2)/2\approx1.93$ you have dropped a factor of 2.
2. **Sign.** Get the quadrant from ASTC *independently* of the algebra, then check the algebra agrees. Two independent routes to the same sign is what makes it a check rather than a restatement.
3. **Neighbour sanity.** $\cos$ is decreasing on $[0,\pi]$, so $\cos(\pi/12)$ must be bigger than $\cos(\pi/6)\approx0.866$ and smaller than $\cos 0 = 1$. It is.

**The four numbers worth memorising outright.**

$$\frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4}\approx 0.9659,\qquad \frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{4}\approx 0.2588,\qquad
2-\sqrt3\approx 0.2679,\qquad \frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx 0.9239$$

Every answer in the 15-degree family is one of the first three, up to a sign.
If your surd does not evaluate to 0.9659, 0.2588 or 0.2679 in absolute value, you have made an
arithmetic error rather than discovered a new value.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

The exam is machine-marked by Möbius, which hides two different parsers behind one answer box, and
nothing in the interface tells you which one you are in.
Grading is by mathematical equivalence, so a correct answer is never rejected for its *form* - only for
being *parsed* differently than you intended.

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Multiplication | `2*x` | `2x` |
| Roots | `sqrt(6)` | a root glyph, or `6^.5` |
| Denominators | `(sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4` | `sqrt(6)+sqrt(2)/4` |
| Natural log | `ln(x)` | `log(x)` |
| Exact form | `(sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4` | `0.9659` |

The single most expensive mistake available on this question is `sqrt(6)+sqrt(2)/4`, which parses as
$\sqrt6+\frac{\sqrt2}{4}\approx 2.80$.
Bracket the whole numerator.

**Always click the preview icon.**
An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics.
All of these are accepted as equivalent, so pick whichever you can type without a typo:
`(sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4`, `sqrt(6)/4+sqrt(2)/4`, `cos(pi/12)`, `sqrt(2+sqrt(3))/2`.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
Everything below has been numerically verified.

### Family 0 - the freebies (multiples of $\pi/6$, $\pi/4$, $\pi/3$)

No formula.
Reference angle plus ASTC.
$\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}=-\frac12$, $\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}=\frac12$, $\tan\frac{7\pi}{4}=-1$,
$\cos\frac{5\pi}{2}=0$.
If the denominator is 2, 3, 4 or 6, you are in this family and you should not be writing a formula.

### Family 1 - the $\pi/12$ (15 degree) family: sum and difference

The tool itself is taught in Section 3A; this is the verified table of what it produces.
This is Q1.1's own family, and it is the single most likely variation to appear.
Every entry is built from $15=45-30$, $75=45+30$, or $105=60+45$.

Read the table as structure, not as 24 separate facts.
There are only two magnitudes here, 0.9659 and 0.2588, and cosine and sine swap which one they get as
you move between the 15 and 75 columns.
Negative angles fold in through parity: $\cos(-5\pi/12)=\cos(5\pi/12)$ and
$\sin(-\pi/12)=-\sin(\pi/12)$.

Decompositions that reach each one: $15=45-30$, $75=45+30$, $105=60+45$, $165=120+45$ or $135+30$,
$195=150+45$, $255=210+45$, $285=225+60$, $345=300+45$.
In practice, reduce to a reference angle of $15^\circ$ or $75^\circ$ first and let ASTC supply the sign.
That is fewer moves than hunting for a clever split.

### Family 2 - the $\pi/8$ (22.5 degree) family: half angle

The tool itself is taught in Section 3B.
No sum of standard angles hits $22.5^\circ$, so here half-angle is the *only* tool.

| angle | deg | $\cos$ | $\sin$ | $\tan$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\pi/8$ | 22.5 | $\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\sqrt2-1$ |
| $3\pi/8$ | 67.5 | $\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\sqrt2+1$ |
| $5\pi/8$ | 112.5 | $-\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $-(\sqrt2+1)$ |
| $7\pi/8$ | 157.5 | $-\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt2}}{2}$ | $-(\sqrt2-1)$ |

Worked example, $\cos\frac{\pi}{8}$.
$\frac{\pi}{8}$ is half of $\frac{\pi}{4}$, and $\frac{\pi}{8}$ is in quadrant I so take the plus sign:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{8}=+\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/4)}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{2}}
=\sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2}\approx0.9239$$

The nested radical is the *expected* answer form here.
Do not try to denest it.
Möbius accepts `sqrt(2+sqrt(2))/2`.

### Family 3 - reciprocal functions

Do the cosine or sine first, then flip and rationalise.
Never look for a "secant difference formula"; there is not one.

$$\sec\left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\frac{1}{\cos(\pi/12)}=\frac{4}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}
=\frac{4(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)}{(\sqrt6)^2-(\sqrt2)^2}=\frac{4(\sqrt6-\sqrt2)}{4}=\sqrt6-\sqrt2\approx1.0353$$

$$\csc\frac{\pi}{12}=\sqrt6+\sqrt2\approx3.8637,\qquad \cot\frac{\pi}{12}=2+\sqrt3\approx3.7321$$

The conjugate step is the whole content of this variation.
Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt6-\sqrt2$, and note that the difference of squares collapses the
denominator to $6-2=4$, which cancels the 4 on top.
That is why the secant and cosecant of $\pi/12$ come out so clean.

### Family 4 - tangent of a 15-degree angle

Two routes.
The tangent difference formula:

$$\tan\frac{\pi}{12}=\tan(45^\circ-30^\circ)=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}{1+\frac{1}{\sqrt3}}
=\frac{\sqrt3-1}{\sqrt3+1}$$

That is exact but not simplified.
Rationalise by multiplying top and bottom by $\sqrt3-1$:

$$\frac{(\sqrt3-1)^2}{3-1}=\frac{3-2\sqrt3+1}{2}=\frac{4-2\sqrt3}{2}=2-\sqrt3\approx0.2679$$

Or just divide the two values you already have:
$\tan\frac{\pi}{12}=\frac{\sin}{\cos}=\frac{\sqrt6-\sqrt2}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}$, then rationalise to the
same $2-\sqrt3$.
If you already computed both the sine and the cosine, dividing beats learning a third formula.

### Family 5 - inverse trig composition (reference triangle)

The tool itself is taught in Section 3C, with five worked examples.
$\sin(\arccos\frac35)$ and its relatives.
There is no identity for these.
**Draw the triangle.**

$\arccos\frac35$ is "the angle whose cosine is $\frac35$", so label a right triangle with
adjacent $=3$ and hypotenuse $=5$.
Pythagoras gives opposite $=\sqrt{25-9}=4$.
Then read off whatever the outer function wants:

$$\sin\left(\arccos\frac35\right)=\frac{\text{opp}}{\text{hyp}}=\frac45$$

$$\cos\left(\arcsin\frac13\right)=\frac{\sqrt8}{3}=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\approx0.9428,\qquad
\tan\left(\arcsin\frac13\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt8}=\frac{\sqrt2}{4}$$

$$\tan\left(\arccos\left(-\frac23\right)\right)=-\frac{\sqrt5}{2}\approx-1.1180$$

That last one carries the trap.
The *range* of $\arccos$ is $[0,\pi]$, so a negative cosine puts the angle in quadrant II, where
tangent is negative.
Get the magnitude from the triangle, then get the sign from the inverse function's range.
The ranges you need: $\arcsin$ and $\arctan$ land in $[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]$, and $\arccos$
lands in $[0,\pi]$.

The algebraic version uses the same method: $\tan(\arcsin x)=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, from a triangle
with opposite $x$ and hypotenuse 1.

### Family 6 - double-angle composition

$\cos(2\arcsin\frac13)$ looks worse than it is.
Use $\cos2\theta=1-2\sin^2\theta$ and let $\theta=\arcsin\frac13$, so $\sin\theta=\frac13$ by definition:

$$\cos\left(2\arcsin\frac13\right)=1-2\left(\frac13\right)^2=1-\frac29=\frac79\approx0.7778$$

The whole trick is picking the *form* of the double-angle identity whose input you already know.
You know $\sin\theta$, so choose the $1-2\sin^2\theta$ form and never compute $\cos\theta$ at all.

More of the same:
$\sin(2\arccos\frac35)=2\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac35=\frac{24}{25}$ via $\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta$
and the 3-4-5 triangle.
$\tan(2\arctan\frac12)=\frac{2\cdot\frac12}{1-\frac14}=\frac43$.
$\cos(2\arctan\frac34)=\frac{7}{25}=0.28$, from the 3-4-5 triangle and $2\cos^2\theta-1$.

### Family 7 - the identity read backwards

Sometimes the question hands you the *right-hand* side of an identity and the exact value is trivial
once you recognise it.
Do not grind these.

$$\sin75^\circ\cos15^\circ+\cos75^\circ\sin15^\circ=\sin(75^\circ+15^\circ)=\sin90^\circ=1$$

$$\cos^2\frac{\pi}{8}-\sin^2\frac{\pi}{8}=\cos\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$$

$$2\sin\frac{\pi}{12}\cos\frac{\pi}{12}=\sin\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac12$$

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{12}\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+\sin\frac{\pi}{12}\sin\frac{\pi}{4}
=\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\pi}{12}\right)=\cos\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$$

The recognition cue: two products added or subtracted, with the *same two angles* appearing in both
terms, is always a sum or difference formula collapsing.
A squared cosine minus a squared sine of the same angle is always a double angle collapsing.

### Family 8 - deeper nesting

$\pi/24$ is $7.5^\circ$, which is half of $15^\circ$.
Apply half-angle to the answer you already have:

$$\cos\frac{\pi}{24}=\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos(\pi/12)}{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt3}}}{2}\approx0.9914$$

Verified numerically.
Doubly nested radicals are the correct final form.
This is unlikely on the midterm, but knowing the pattern continues tells you the method is general
rather than a bag of memorised special cases.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often they actually cost marks.

1. **The middle sign of $\cos(A\pm B)$.** The formula's sign is the *opposite* of the bracket's, so $\cos(A-B)$ has a plus in the middle. This is the number one error on this question.
2. **Bracketing the numerator on entry.** `sqrt(6)+sqrt(2)/4` is a different number from `(sqrt(6)+sqrt(2))/4`.
3. **Giving a decimal.** "Exact value" forbids it, whatever the precision.
4. **Applying odd-function parity to cosine.** $\cos(-x)=+\cos x$. Writing $-\cos x$ flips the sign of the answer for free.
5. **Choosing the half-angle $\pm$ from the wrong quadrant.** The sign comes from $\theta/2$, the angle you are computing, not from $\theta$.
6. **Degrees versus radians.** $\pi/12$ is $15^\circ$, not $12^\circ$. If the calculator is in degrees, every numerical check silently lies to you.
7. **Forgetting to reduce past $2\pi$.** $\frac{25\pi}{12}$ is coterminal with $\frac{\pi}{12}$.
8. **Not rationalising a reciprocal.** $\frac{4}{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}$ is exact but may be marked unsimplified on a written paper. Möbius accepts it; a human marker may not.
9. **Reaching for a formula in Family 0.** If the denominator is 2, 3, 4 or 6, recall is the method.
10. **Trying to denest a $\pi/8$ answer.** The nested radical *is* the answer there.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
Exact value of a trig function of THETA?
|
+-- Is there an inverse trig function inside?
|     YES -> draw a REFERENCE TRIANGLE. Magnitude from the sides,
|            sign from the inverse function's RANGE.
|            (arcsin, arctan -> [-pi/2, pi/2];  arccos -> [0, pi])
|            Outer function doubled? Use a DOUBLE-ANGLE identity
|            in the form whose input you already know.
|
+-- Is it two products added, or cos^2 - sin^2?
|     YES -> an identity is collapsing. Read it BACKWARDS. Usually one line.
|
+-- Otherwise: STRIP first.
      1. Negative angle?  cos/sec: drop the sign.  Others: pull it out front.
      2. Bigger than 2pi? Subtract full turns.
      3. Note the quadrant. Get the sign from ASTC and set it aside.
      4. Reduce to a reference angle in [0, pi/2].
      Now look at the denominator of the reference angle:
        2, 3, 4, 6      -> RECALL. No formula.
        12              -> SUM/DIFFERENCE formula (15 = 45-30, 75 = 45+30).
        8               -> HALF-ANGLE formula. A nested radical is the answer.
        24 or worse     -> HALF-ANGLE twice.
      5. Reattach the sign from step 3.
      6. Check: is it in [-1,1]? is the decimal 0.9659 / 0.2588 / 0.2679 / 0.9239 / 0.3827?
      7. Type it with every bracket, and PREVIEW.
```
