# Q17.1 - Interval containing a secant integral

**The question (Final Exam 1, item 9, 5 points).**
"Use the properties of the integral to find an interval where the value of the integral
$$\int_0^{\pi/3} \sec x\,dx$$
is located."

**The answer.**
$$\frac{\pi}{3} \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx \;\le\; \frac{2\pi}{3}$$
that is, the interval $\left[\tfrac{\pi}{3},\,\tfrac{2\pi}{3}\right] \approx [1.0471975512,\ 2.0943951024]$.
The true value is $\ln(2+\sqrt3) \approx 1.3169578969$, which sits $0.2697603457$ above the floor and $0.7774372055$ below the ceiling.

**What to type into Möbius:** `pi/3` for the lower bound and `2*pi/3` for the upper bound.
If a single box asks for the interval, `[pi/3,2*pi/3]`.
Never type the decimals unless the question asks for them, and never write `2pi/3` without the star.

## 0. The 30-second version

```
1. CHECK the integrand is continuous on [a,b]  ->  sec has poles at pi/2 + k*pi; pi/3 < pi/2, so we are safe
2. FIND m and M, the min and max of f on [a,b]  ->  sec is increasing here, so m = sec 0 = 1, M = sec(pi/3) = 2
3. MULTIPLY BOTH BY (b - a)                     ->  b - a = pi/3
4. WRITE  m(b-a) <= I <= M(b-a)                 ->  pi/3 <= I <= 2pi/3
```

Step 3 is the whole question.
A student who does steps 1, 2 and 4 and skips step 3 writes $1 \le I \le 2$, and on this particular integral that statement is still true.
That is why this question is dangerous, and section 9 is about nothing else.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is the FIRST of three questions in the properties-of-the-definite-integral category, and it is the one that establishes the machinery the other two reuse.
Q17.2 estimates an integral from the same property; Q17.3 bounds one.
Get the foundation right here and the other two are re-runs with different numbers.

| The reflex being tested | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| An integral is an area, so it is trapped between two rectangles | Q17.2, Q17.3, and every "estimate without integrating" question |
| The min and max of $f$ on $[a,b]$ are found by the Q15 candidate recipe | Q15.1, Q15.3, Q15.4, Q15.5 - literally the same work |
| The width $(b-a)$ is a FACTOR, not decoration | The single most expensive slip in this category |
| A bound is a claim, not an approximation | Q17.3, where the honest bound is very loose and that is fine |
| Continuity on the closed interval is a precondition, not a formality | Any question whose integrand has a pole |

**The exam fact that halves the work.**
On Final Exam 2, this category is welded to category 15.
Item 3 has two parts: part (a) is Q15.3 (absolute extremes of $1/(x^2+1)$ on $[-1,1]$ by the Extreme Value Theorem) and part (b) is Q17.2 (estimate $\int_{-1}^{1} dx/(x^2+1)$).

## 2. The five things you must have cold

**(a) The comparison property, in the only form that matters.**

> If $m \le f(x) \le M$ for every $x$ in $[a,b]$, then
> $$m\,(b-a) \;\le\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\le\; M\,(b-a).$$

**(b) The four supporting properties.**

| Property | Statement | What it buys you here |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Monotonicity | $f \le g$ on $[a,b]$ $\Rightarrow$ $\int_a^b f \le \int_a^b g$ | The comparison property is this with $g$ constant |
| Additivity | $\int_a^b = \int_a^c + \int_c^b$ | Splitting the interval sharpens the bound |
| Orientation | $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$, and $\int_a^a f = 0$ | Swapping the limits negates BOTH bounds and swaps them |
| Linearity | $\int (\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha\int f + \beta\int g$ | Lets you pull a constant out before bounding |

**(c) The secant values on the unit circle.**

| $x$ | $0$ | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $\cos x$ | $1$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ | $\tfrac12$ | $0$ |
| $\sec x$ | $1$ | $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3} = 1.1547005384$ | $\sqrt2 = 1.4142135624$ | $2$ | undefined |

**(d) Where secant is defined.**
$\sec x = 1/\cos x$ is undefined exactly where $\cos x = 0$, that is at $x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$.
The nearest one to our interval is $\pi/2 = 1.5707963268$, and $\pi/3 = 1.0471975512$.
The clearance is $\pi/2 - \pi/3 = \pi/6 = 0.5235987756$, so the integrand is continuous on the whole closed interval $[0,\pi/3]$ and the property applies.

**(e) Why the minimum of $\sec$ on this interval is at the LEFT end.**
$\cos$ is decreasing on $[0,\pi/2]$ and stays positive there.
A decreasing positive denominator makes the fraction increase.
So $\sec$ is increasing on $[0,\pi/3]$, and an increasing function takes its minimum at the left endpoint and its maximum at the right endpoint.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1 - state the domain.**
> Write down where the integrand is undefined, and check that none of those points lies in $[a,b]$.
> If one does, the comparison property does not apply at all and the answer is that the integral is improper.
>
> **Step 2 - find $m$ and $M$ honestly, by the Q15 recipe.**
> Compute $f'$.
> Solve $f'(x) = 0$ on $(a,b)$, and separately list every point where $f'$ does not exist.
> Evaluate $f$ at those critical points AND at both endpoints.
> The smallest of those values is $m$; the largest is $M$.
>
> **Step 3 - compute the width $b-a$.**
> Write it on its own line so you cannot forget it.
>
> **Step 4 - assemble.**
> $m(b-a) \le \int_a^b f \le M(b-a)$, with the arithmetic done in exact form.
>
> **Step 5 - sanity check.**
> The interval must have positive width unless $f$ is constant, the two bounds must be in the right order, and if you can estimate the integral any other way it must land inside.

**Classify the integrand before step 2, because it decides how much work step 2 costs.**

| What the integrand looks like on $[a,b]$ | How to get $m$ and $M$ | Cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Monotone (you can see $f' > 0$ or $f' < 0$ throughout) | Read them off the two endpoints | 2 evaluations |
| One interior critical point | Endpoints plus that one point | 3 evaluations |
| Has a corner or cusp (a point where $f'$ is undefined) | Endpoints plus zeros of $f'$ plus the non-differentiable points | 4+ evaluations |
| Discontinuous somewhere in $[a,b]$ | The property does not apply | stop |

$\sec x$ on $[0,\pi/3]$ is the first row.
$f'(x) = \sec x \tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x}$, which is strictly positive for $0 < x \le \pi/3$, so the only place $f'$ vanishes on the closed interval is $x=0$, and that is already an endpoint.

## 4. Q17.1 worked five ways

### Route A - the comparison property, endpoints only (the intended route)

$\sec$ is increasing on $[0,\pi/3]$, so
$$m = \sec 0 = 1, \qquad M = \sec\tfrac{\pi}{3} = 2, \qquad b-a = \tfrac{\pi}{3} - 0 = \tfrac{\pi}{3}.$$
Therefore
$$1\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{3} \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx \;\le\; 2\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{3},
\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \frac{\pi}{3} \le I \le \frac{2\pi}{3}.$$

### Route B - the Q15 machinery in full, with no shortcut

Do not assume monotonicity; earn it.
$f'(x) = \sec x\tan x$.
Set $f'(x) = 0$: since $\sec x \ne 0$ ever, this needs $\tan x = 0$, so $x = k\pi$, and the only such point in $[0,\pi/3]$ is $x=0$, an endpoint.
$f'$ is undefined only where $\cos x = 0$, and no such point is in $[0,\pi/3]$.
Candidate list: $\{0,\ \pi/3\}$.
$f(0) = 1$, $f(\pi/3) = 2$.
So $m=1$, $M=2$, and route A's assembly follows.
This is the route to write out when the marker can see your working, because it is the one that shows you know $m$ and $M$ are extreme VALUES and not just endpoint values.

### Route C - beat the ceiling with a dominating function

The property is really monotonicity with a constant on the right.
Nothing stops you using a non-constant comparison.
On $[0,\pi/3]$ it happens that $\sec x \le 1 + x^2$ (checked at 100001 points, zero violations; the two curves do not cross until $x = 1.1025058244$, which is past $\pi/3$).
Then
$$\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/3}(1+x^2)\,dx \;=\; \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{(\pi/3)^3}{3} \;=\; 1.4299910905.$$
That ceiling is much tighter than $2\pi/3 = 2.0943951024$: it removes $85.46\%$ of the crude ceiling's excess over the true value.
**It is a better bound and it is the wrong answer to this question**, because the question says "use the properties of the integral to find an interval", and the expected property is the $m$-$M$ one.
Keep this route for the day a question says "show that $I < 1.5$".

### Route D - split the interval to sharpen

Additivity lets you bound each piece separately, using each piece's own $m$ and $M$.
With $k$ equal pieces on a monotone integrand the total width of the sandwich is exactly $(M-m)(b-a)/k$.

| $k$ | lower bound | upper bound | width |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1$ | $1.0471975512$ | $2.0943951024$ | $1.0471975512$ |
| $2$ | $1.1281985637$ | $1.6517973393$ | $0.5235987756$ |
| $3$ | $1.1762070751$ | $1.5252729255$ | $0.3490658504$ |
| $4$ | $1.2053741969$ | $1.4671735847$ | $0.2617993878$ |
| $10$ | $1.2677506328$ | $1.3724703879$ | $0.1047197551$ |
| $100$ | $1.3117535646$ | $1.3222255401$ | $0.0104719755$ |

Every row contains $1.3169578969$.
To force a width below $0.01$ you need $k \ge \lceil (M-m)(b-a)/0.01 \rceil = 105$, which is not exam work.
Route D is the honest answer to "how would you make this bound as tight as you like", and it is the bridge to Riemann sums.

### Route E - compute the integral and then quote an interval

$\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C$, so
$$I = \ln\left(2+\sqrt3\right) - \ln 1 = \ln(2+\sqrt3) = 1.3169578969.$$
Now say "$I$ lies in $[1.31, 1.32]$".
This is a correct sentence and it will very likely score badly, because the question named the method.
Worse, on the exam this antiderivative is not in your toolkit until later, and the whole point of the item is that you can bound an integral you cannot evaluate.
Use route E only as a private check, which is what it is for.

### Route comparison

| Route | What it needs | Time | Risk | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - endpoints | seeing that $\sec$ increases | 30 s | forgetting $(b-a)$ | fast but assert the monotonicity |
| B - full Q15 | $f'$, critical points, candidate table | 90 s | none | **use this one under pressure** |
| C - dominating function | inventing a comparison | 3 min+ | the comparison may be false | wrong tool for this wording |
| D - splitting | $k$ evaluations | grows with $k$ | arithmetic slips | use only if asked to sharpen |
| E - evaluate exactly | the $\sec$ antiderivative | 60 s | answers a different question | private check only |

**Use route B.**
It costs one extra line over route A, it produces the sentence a marker is looking for, and it is the same three lines you already write for every Q15 question.
Route A is route B with the justification deleted, and the justification is where the method marks live (inference: no marking scheme exists in the course files).

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Copy this shape.
Every sentence is doing a job.

> $f(x) = \sec x$ is continuous on $[0,\pi/3]$, since $\sec$ is undefined only at $x = \pi/2 + k\pi$ and no such point lies in $[0,\pi/3]$.
>
> $f'(x) = \sec x\tan x$, which is zero only where $\tan x = 0$, i.e. at $x = 0$, and is defined everywhere on $[0,\pi/3]$.
> Since $f'(x) > 0$ on $(0,\pi/3]$, $f$ is increasing on $[0,\pi/3]$.
>
> Hence the absolute minimum is $m = f(0) = \sec 0 = 1$ and the absolute maximum is $M = f(\pi/3) = \sec(\pi/3) = 2$, so $1 \le \sec x \le 2$ on $[0,\pi/3]$.
>
> By the comparison property of the definite integral, with $b - a = \pi/3$,
> $$1\cdot\frac{\pi}{3} \;\le\; \int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx \;\le\; 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{3}.$$
>
> Therefore the value of the integral lies in $\left[\dfrac{\pi}{3},\ \dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right] \approx [1.047,\ 2.094]$.

That is five sentences for five points.
The last one restates the answer as an interval because the question asked for an interval, not for an inequality.

**Short version, if the box is answer-only:**
$\sec$ increases on $[0,\pi/3]$, so $1 \le \sec x \le 2$; multiplying by $b-a=\pi/3$ gives $\pi/3 \le I \le 2\pi/3$.

## 6. Verify before you commit

**The magnitude check.**
$\sec$ runs from $1$ to $2$ across an interval of width about $1.05$, so the integral must be a little over $1$ and well under $2.1$.
Anything outside that is arithmetic, not calculus.

**Four cheap invariants.**

| Invariant | On this question |
| :--- | :--- |
| The bounds are in the right order and the width is $(M-m)(b-a)$ | $2.0943951024 - 1.0471975512 = 1.0471975512 = \pi/3$ |
| The ratio of the bounds equals $M/m$ when $m>0$ | $\dfrac{2\pi/3}{\pi/3} = 2 = \dfrac{M}{m}$ |
| A midpoint-rule estimate must land inside | $\sec(\pi/6)\cdot\pi/3 = 1.2091995762$, inside |
| $\sec x \ge 1$ everywhere, so $I \ge b-a$ | $1.3169578969 \ge 1.0471975512$ |

**The one number worth memorising.**
$\ln(2+\sqrt3) = 1.3169578969$.
Its position inside $[\pi/3, 2\pi/3]$ is $25.76\%$ of the way up, which is the same as saying the average value of $\sec$ on this interval is $1.2576021548$, closer to $m$ than to $M$.
That is a property of every convex increasing integrand and it is why the crude interval always looks lopsided.

**The mean value point, which is the number that makes section 9 make sense.**
By the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals there is a $c$ in $(0,\pi/3)$ with $\sec c$ equal to the average value.
Here $c = \arccos\!\left(\tfrac{b-a}{I}\right) = 0.6515182915$, which is $62.22\%$ of the way along the interval.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as a STRUCTURE, not as a list of facts.
Across all of Family 0 there are only two distinct pieces of work: decide which end is the minimum, then multiply both extreme values by the same width.
Every sign in these tables is generated by one rule, namely whether $f$ increases or decreases, and every magnitude is generated by one rule, namely $(\text{extreme value}) \times (b-a)$.

### Family 0 - monotone integrand: $m$ and $M$ are the two endpoint values

**Trigger.** You can see $f' > 0$ or $f' < 0$ on the whole interval without solving anything.
**Tool.** Evaluate $f$ at $a$ and at $b$; the smaller is $m$, the larger is $M$.
**Worked shape.** Increasing $\Rightarrow$ $m = f(a)$, $M = f(b)$. Decreasing $\Rightarrow$ $m = f(b)$, $M = f(a)$.

### Family 3 - comparison against a non-constant dominating function

**Trigger.** The wording is "show that $I < N$" or "prove the integral is at most", rather than "find an interval".
**Tool.** Monotonicity of the integral: find $g$ with $f \le g$ on $[a,b]$ and $\int g$ easy.
**Worked shape.** Verify the inequality on the whole interval FIRST, then integrate $g$.

| # | $f$ on $[a,b]$ | dominating $g$ | $\int g$ | crude $M(b-a)$ | true value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3.1 | $\sin x$ on $[0,1]$ | $x$ | $0.5$ | $0.8414709848$ | $0.4596976941$ |
| 3.2 | $e^{-x^2}$ on $[0,1]$, from below by $e^{-x}$ | $e^{-x}$ | $0.6321205588$ | $m(b-a) = 0.3678794412$ | $0.7468241328$ |
| 3.3 | $\sec x$ on $[0,\pi/3]$ | $1+x^2$ | $1.4299910905$ | $2.0943951024$ | $1.3169578969$ |

Row 3.2 is the mirror image: a dominating function used from BELOW to raise the floor.
Row 3.3 uses the question's own integrand and shows how much slack the crude ceiling carries.
**Verify the comparison before you use it.**
$\sec x \le 1+x^2$ is true on $[0,\pi/3]$ but false beyond $x = 1.1025058244$, so the same comparison on $[0,1.2]$ would be a false statement dressed as a proof.

### Family 4 - additivity: splitting sharpens the bound

**Trigger.** "Make the estimate as accurate as you like", or a follow-up asking for a bound of a given width.
**Tool.** $\int_a^b = \sum_i \int_{x_i}^{x_{i+1}}$, bounding each piece with its own $m_i$ and $M_i$.
**Worked shape.** For $k$ equal pieces of a monotone $f$, width of the sandwich $= (M-m)(b-a)/k$, so $k = \lceil (M-m)(b-a)/w \rceil$ pieces achieve width $w$.

The table in route D is this family's instance table.
The width formula is exact: at $k=3$ the width is $0.3490658504 = (2-1)(\pi/3)/3$, and $k=105$ is the first $k$ giving a width under $0.01$.

### Family 5 - orientation and sign

**Trigger.** The limits are the wrong way round, or the integrand is negative somewhere.
**Tool.** $\int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f$. Negate BOTH bounds and swap them.
**Worked shape.** If $[L,U]$ bounds $\int_a^b f$, then $[-U,-L]$ bounds $\int_b^a f$.

| # | integral | $m$ | $M$ | bound | true value |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| 5.1 | $\int_{\pi/3}^{0}\sec x\,dx$ | - | - | $[-2.0943951024,\ -1.0471975512]$ | $-1.3169578969$ |
| 5.2 | $\int_0^{\pi/3}(-\sec x)\,dx$ | $-2$ | $-1$ | $[-2.0943951024,\ -1.0471975512]$ | $-1.3169578969$ |
| 5.3 | $\int_{\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx$ | - | - | $[0,\ 0]$ | $0$ |

Row 5.2 is the one that catches people.
Multiplying $f$ by $-1$ makes the MINIMUM come from where the maximum used to be.
$-\sec$ has $m = -2$ at $x = \pi/3$ and $M = -1$ at $x = 0$.
The rule "$m$ is at the left end" is not a rule; it is a consequence of increasing.

### Family 6 - when the property does NOT apply

**Trigger.** The integrand is undefined or unbounded somewhere in $[a,b]$.
**Tool.** Say so, and say why. There is no finite $M$, so there is no upper bound to give.
**Worked shape.** Locate the bad point, show the one-sided limit is infinite, conclude the integral is improper.

| # | integral | the bad point | what happens |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 6.1 | $\int_0^{2\pi/3}\sec x\,dx$ | $x = \pi/2$ is inside | $\int_0^{\pi/2-\varepsilon}$ grows without bound: $5.2983$, $7.6009$, $9.9035$, $12.2061$, $14.5087$ for $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}\ldots10^{-6}$ |
| 6.2 | $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x}$ | $x = 0$ is inside | no finite $m$ or $M$ exists |
| 6.3 | $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sec x\,dx$ | $x = \pi/2$ is an endpoint | still improper; the endpoint counts |

**This is the family that makes Q17.1's interval $[0,\pi/3]$ a deliberate choice.**
$\pi/3$ is the largest of the friendly angles that stays clear of the pole.
Had the examiner written $[0, 2\pi/3]$ the question would have no answer, and had he written $[0,\pi/4]$ the arithmetic would involve $\sqrt2$ instead of $2$.
Screen for poles even when the interval looks harmless.

### Family 7 - the three verbs: "interval containing", "estimate", "bound"

**Trigger.** Read the verb before you start.
**Tool.** Different verbs want different objects.

| verb (and which question uses it) | what it wants | on Q17.1 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "find an interval where the value is located" (**Q17.1**) | BOTH bounds, presented as an interval | $[\pi/3,\ 2\pi/3]$ |
| "estimate the value" (**Q17.2**) | a single number, usually the sandwich stated as an inequality, sometimes its midpoint | $1 \le \pi/2 \le 2$ |
| "bound the value" (**Q17.3**) | an inequality; one side may be enough if the wording says "at most" | $0 \le I \le 30.411$ |

Three estimates you might be asked for, all inside the interval:

| estimate | formula | value | error against $1.3169578969$ |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: |
| midpoint of the bounds | $\tfrac{m+M}{2}(b-a)$ | $1.5707963268$ | $0.2538384299$ |
| midpoint rule | $f\!\left(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right)(b-a)$ | $1.2091995762$ | $0.1077583208$ |
| average value (exact by definition) | $\tfrac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f \cdot (b-a)$ | $1.3169578969$ | $0$ |

The midpoint RULE beats the midpoint OF THE BOUNDS on this integrand, and it is one extra evaluation.
Neither is what Q17.1 asks for.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks.

**1. Forgetting the $(b-a)$ factor, and getting away with it.**
This is the defining error of the whole category, and on Q17.1 it does not announce itself.
Dropping the factor gives $[1,2]$, and the true value $1.3169578969$ is inside $[1,2]$.
So a self-check by "does my interval contain the answer" cannot catch it here, and neither can a plausibility check, because $[1,2]$ looks like a perfectly sensible interval.
On both of the other questions in this category the same slip IS caught: Q17.2 gives $[\tfrac12,1]$ which excludes $\pi/2 = 1.5707963268$, and Q17.3 gives $[0, 6.0822019956]$ which excludes $10.6736300365$.
**Q17.1 is the one question in the category where this mistake is invisible.**
The only defence is procedural: write $b-a$ on its own line at step 3, before you assemble anything.

**2. Reading the minimum off the wrong end.**
$\sec$ increases, so $m$ is on the left; $\cos$ decreases, so $m$ is on the right.
If you memorise "left end is the minimum" you will get Family 0 row 0.9 wrong, and you will get every negated integrand in Family 5 wrong.
Decide from the sign of $f'$, every time.

**3. Guessing $m$ and $M$ instead of finding them, and getting away with THAT too.**
A wrong $m$ or $M$ still produces a technically-correct interval far more often than you would expect.
Out of an 18-row census of plausible mistakes, 6 rows were both valid and containing, 4 rows used an INVALID $m$ or $M$ and still produced an interval containing the true value, and only 8 rows were actually caught.
The reason is structural: **loosening a correct bound can never break containment.**
Any $m' \le 1$ and any $M' \ge 2$ still work; that was checked over 4002 samples with zero failures.
Only tightening past the truth is detectable.

The exact tipping point is beautiful and worth knowing.
If you take $m = \sec(x_0)$ for some $x_0$ in the interval, the resulting interval still contains the true value if and only if $x_0 \le c$, where $c = 0.6515182915$ is the Mean Value Theorem point, at $62.22\%$ of the way along.
A scan over $1001$ choices of $x_0$ found $62.24\%$ of them still "work", matching $c/(b-a)$ to within one grid step.
So taking the midpoint $\pi/6 = 0.5236$ as your minimum HIDES, and taking $\pi/4 = 0.7854$ as your minimum is CAUGHT.
**A correct-looking interval is not evidence of a correct method.**

**4. Not checking the domain.**
$\sec$ has poles, and a bound argument across a pole is meaningless rather than merely loose.
One sentence costs nothing: "no point of $[0,\pi/3]$ has $\cos x = 0$, since the nearest such point is $\pi/2 > \pi/3$."

**5. Answering with a number instead of an interval.**
The verb is "find an interval where the value is located".
An answer of "$1.317$" is a different question's answer, however true it is.

**6. Treating looseness as an error.**
A bound can be very loose and completely correct.
Q17.3's honest answer is $[0, 30.411]$ for a value near $10.67$, a factor of nearly three at the top end.
That is not a mistake and it does not need apologising for or "improving".
If the examiner wanted tightness he would have asked for a specific accuracy, and then Family 4 is the tool.

**7. Writing the bounds in the wrong order, or as a set.**
$[\pi/3, 2\pi/3]$, lower first.
$[2\pi/3, \pi/3]$ describes the empty set and scores zero.

**8. Möbius: `2pi/3`.**
Covered in section 7. It is a keystroke, and it costs the whole mark.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
"Use the properties of the integral to ..."   <- read the VERB first
        |
        +-- is the integrand DEFINED and BOUNDED on all of [a,b]?
        |      no  -> say so; the integral is improper; the property does not apply.   [Family 6]
        |      yes -> continue
        |
        +-- does the question have a part (a) asking for absolute extremes?
        |      yes -> part (a)'s max is M, part (a)'s min is m. Go straight to ASSEMBLE. [Family 2]
        |      no  -> continue
        |
        +-- compute f'.  Is f' of one sign on the whole interval?
        |      yes -> monotone.  increasing: m=f(a), M=f(b).  decreasing: m=f(b), M=f(a). [Family 0]
        |      no  -> full Q15 recipe: zeros of f', points where f' is undefined,
        |             plus BOTH endpoints; evaluate f at all of them.                     [Family 1]
        |
        +-- ASSEMBLE:   write  b - a  =  ______   on its own line     <- the step people skip
        |               then   m(b-a) <= I <= M(b-a)
        |
        +-- which verb?
        |      "interval"  -> present [m(b-a), M(b-a)]
        |      "estimate"  -> present the inequality, or its midpoint if a number is demanded
        |      "bound"     -> present the side asked for; both sides is never wrong
        |      "show I < N"-> you may need a dominating function instead              [Family 3]
        |      "sharpen"   -> split into k pieces; width = (M-m)(b-a)/k               [Family 4]
        |
        +-- limits reversed or integrand negated?  negate both bounds and SWAP them.  [Family 5]
        |
        +-- CHECK: bounds ordered, width = (M-m)(b-a), a midpoint estimate lands inside,
        |          and remember a correct interval is NOT proof of a correct m and M.
        |
        +-- PREVIEW in Mobius:  pi/3   and   2*pi/3   (never 2pi/3)
```
