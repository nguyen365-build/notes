## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2.1 The two derivative facts

| $f$ | $f'$ | $f''$ | concave on $(0,\pi)$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | down, so tangent **above** |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | $-\cos x$ | down on $(0,\pi/2)$, up after |

These hold **only when $x$ is in radians**.
$\dfrac{d}{dt}\sin(t^\circ) = \dfrac{\pi}{180}\cos(t^\circ)$, which is a different function.
That $\pi/180$ is not a decoration; it is the price of working in degrees, and section 4.4 shows you can pay it honestly.

### 2.2 The special angles, both coordinates

| degrees | radians | $\sin$ | $\cos$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ |
| $30$ | $\pi/6$ | $1/2$ | $\sqrt3/2$ |
| $45$ | $\pi/4$ | $\sqrt2/2$ | $\sqrt2/2$ |
| $60$ | $\pi/3$ | $\sqrt3/2$ | $1/2$ |
| $90$ | $\pi/2$ | $1$ | $0$ |

You need **both columns** at the anchor, because $f(a)$ comes from one and $f'(a)$ from the other.
A student who remembers only $\sin 60^\circ$ cannot start this question.

### 2.3 The conversion

$$1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180}\ \text{rad}, \qquad 2^\circ = \frac{\pi}{90}\ \text{rad} \approx 0.0349$$

Read that number.
It is about **one thirtieth**, and a step of one thirtieth is what makes the estimate good.
A step of $2$ is what makes it nonsense.

### 2.4 The two decimals worth memorising

| quantity | value |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt3/2$ | $0.8660254$ |
| $\pi/180$ | $0.0174533$ |

Their sum is $0.8834787$, and that is the answer.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Name $f$.** The thing you are asked for is $f$ evaluated somewhere.
> 2. **Name the anchor $a$.** The nearest point where $f(a)$ and $f'(a)$ are both exactly known. For trig that means a special angle.
> 3. **Convert.** Write $a$ in radians and write $dx$ in radians. Do this on its own line, before differentiating.
> 4. **Differentiate once.** Evaluate $f'(a)$ exactly.
> 5. **Build $dy = f'(a)\,dx$.**
> 6. **Add: $f(a+dx) \approx f(a) + dy$.**
> 7. **Read the concavity** and say over or under.

| The cue in the stem | What it forces |
| :--- | :--- |
| a degree sign anywhere | step 3 is mandatory and is worth marks |
| "linearization" | write $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ |
| "differentials" | write $dy = f'(a)\,dx$ |
| "estimate the error in" | you want $dy$ **alone**, not $f(a) + dy$ |
| "to four decimal places" | write four places (see section 6.3) |
| "over- or underestimate" | you need $f''$, not just $f'$ |

Steps 3 and 4 are the two places marks are lost, and they are lost in different ways: step 3 is a blunder, step 4 is a slip.

## 4. Q12.2 worked four ways

### 4.1 The differentials route

$$f(x) = \sin x, \quad a = \frac{\pi}{3}, \quad dx = 2^\circ = \frac{\pi}{90}$$
$$f(a) = \sin\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad f'(a) = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac12$$
$$dy = f'(a)\,dx = \frac12 \cdot \frac{\pi}{90} = \frac{\pi}{180}$$
$$\sin 62^\circ \approx \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.8834787$$

Notice the small pleasure in the arithmetic: $\cos(\pi/3) = 1/2$ **halves** the $\pi/90$ into $\pi/180$, so the correction term is exactly "one degree in radians".

### 4.2 The linearization route

$$L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac12\left(x - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$$
$$L\!\left(\frac{31\pi}{90}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac12\cdot\frac{\pi}{90} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$$

Identical, as Q12.1 established.
This route makes you write the target in radians too, which is one more chance to get the conversion right and one more chance to get it wrong.

### 4.3 The angle-sum route, which explains why it works

$$\sin(60^\circ + 2^\circ) = \sin 60^\circ \cos 2^\circ + \cos 60^\circ \sin 2^\circ$$

Now approximate the two small-angle factors: $\cos 2^\circ \approx 1$ and $\sin 2^\circ \approx \pi/90$.
$$\approx \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 1 + \frac12 \cdot \frac{\pi}{90} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$$

Same number, bit for bit.
This is not a separate method; it is the linearization with its two approximations made visible.
It also tells you exactly what you threw away: $\cos 2^\circ$ is really $0.99939$, not $1$, and that missing $0.00061$ is where the error comes from.

### 4.4 The degree-function route, which is the honest way to stay in degrees

Define $g(t) = \sin(t^\circ) = \sin\!\left(\dfrac{\pi t}{180}\right)$ with $t$ measured in degrees.
Then the chain rule gives
$$g'(t) = \frac{\pi}{180}\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi t}{180}\right)$$
$$g(62) \approx g(60) + 2\,g'(60) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}\cdot\frac12 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$$

Bit-identical to the other three.
**You may work in degrees, provided you differentiate the degree function.**
The $\pi/180$ does not disappear; it moves from the step into the derivative.
Verified: all four routes land on the same double, `0.8834786963043819`.

### Route comparison

| Route | Lines of writing | Where it can go wrong | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 4.1 differentials | 4 | forgetting to convert $dx$ | **use this one under pressure** |
| 4.2 linearization | 5 | converting the target as well, twice the exposure |
| 4.3 angle sum | 6 | needs the identity recalled correctly | best for *understanding* |
| 4.4 degree function | 5 | the $\pi/180$ hides inside $g'$ and gets dropped | use only if degrees feel safer |

The stem says "use linearization", so write the words $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$ somewhere even if you compute by 4.1.
It costs one line and it is the phrase the marker is looking for.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Six points, so roughly six things are being looked for.
There is no published marking scheme for this course, so the split below is inference and not an authority.

> Let $f(x) = \sin x$, with $x$ in radians.
> Take $a = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$, since $60^\circ$ is the nearest angle whose sine and cosine are exact.
> Then $dx = 62^\circ - 60^\circ = 2^\circ = \dfrac{2\pi}{180} = \dfrac{\pi}{90}$ radians.
> $f'(x) = \cos x$, so $f'(a) = \cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac12$.
> The linearization is $L(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a)$, so
> $$\sin 62^\circ \approx \sin\frac{\pi}{3} + \cos\frac{\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{\pi}{90} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180} \approx 0.8835.$$
> Since $f''(x) = -\sin x < 0$ on $(0,\pi)$, $f$ is concave down there and the tangent line lies above the curve, so this is an **overestimate**.

The sentence "$dx = 2^\circ = \pi/90$ radians" is the one sentence that cannot be omitted.
Everything else in the write-up is recoverable from a correct final number; that line is the only place the examiner can see that you knew.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### 6.1 The two-second checks

| Check | Why it works |
| :--- | :--- |
| Is the answer in $[-1, 1]$? | it is a sine; **this alone kills the degrees blunder** |
| Is it just above $\sqrt3/2 = 0.866$? | you stepped up from $60^\circ$ by a little |
| Is the correction about $0.017$? | $\pi/180$, one degree in radians |
| Is $62^\circ$ still in the first quadrant? | so the sine is positive and rising |

The first line is the highest-value check in the whole of category 12.
The blunder it catches produces $\sqrt3/2 + 1 = 1.8660$, and no sine is ever $1.87$.

### 6.2 The measured error

Do not assert that the method works. Here is what it actually did.

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Estimate | $0.8834786963043819$ |
| True $\sin 62^\circ$ | $0.8829475928589269$ |
| Error (estimate $-$ true) | $+5.31103445455 \times 10^{-4}$ |
| Relative error | $6.0151 \times 10^{-4}$, i.e. $0.060$ percent |
| Direction | **over**, as concavity predicts |

The true value was computed to 60 significant digits, confirmed a second way through the angle-sum identity, and confirmed a third against `math.sin`.

### 6.3 How many decimal places this is actually worth

The estimate rounds to $0.8835$; the truth rounds to $0.8829$.
**Three correct decimal places, not four.**

Across a twelve-entry trigonometric catalog (section 8), the correct-place counts are
$$[3,\ 3,\ 3,\ 2,\ 3,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 3]$$
and **not one of the twelve reaches four correct decimal places**.
Q12.1 found the same thing for roots, where 5 of 21 managed four.
So when Q12.3 says "estimate each of the values below to four decimal places", it can only mean *write* four decimal places.
The marker's answer is the differential value, and writing $0.4698$ for $\cos 62^\circ$ is correct even though the true value is $0.4695$.
Say so on the page if you have room; it costs nothing and it shows you know what the estimate is worth.

### 6.4 The error bound, and the endpoint it must be taken at

The quadratic remainder is $\dfrac{|f''(\xi)|}{2}dx^2$ for some $\xi$ between $a$ and $a+dx$.
For $\sin$ that is $\dfrac{|\sin \xi|}{2}dx^2$, and $|\sin|$ is **rising** on $(60^\circ, 62^\circ)$.

| Bound taken at | Value | Holds? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the anchor, $\sin 60^\circ$ | $5.2761\times10^{-4}$ | **no** - the actual error is $5.3110\times10^{-4}$ |
| the target, $\sin 62^\circ$ | $5.3792\times10^{-4}$ | yes |

This is Q12.1's finding recurring with the inequality pointing the other way.
There, $|f''|$ *fell* as $x$ grew, so an anchor bound failed on downward steps.
Here it *rises*, so an anchor bound fails on upward steps.
**The rule is the same either way: take the maximum of $|f''|$ over the whole interval, not at the anchor.**

### 6.5 If you want a better answer

Add the next Taylor term:
$$\sin 62^\circ \approx \frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180} - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{(\pi/90)^2}{2} = 0.8829510834564408$$
Error $3.49\times10^{-6}$, which is **152 times better**.
That is not what this question asks for, and writing it does not earn the linearization marks, but it is worth knowing that the extra accuracy costs one line.

### 6.6 Two things that name a point inside the interval

Both of these are theorems about $(60^\circ, 62^\circ)$, and both were recovered by root-finding rather than assumed.

| Theorem | The point it names |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mean value theorem: $\cos\xi$ = the secant slope | $\xi = 61.0016^\circ$ |
| Taylor with Lagrange remainder: $-\sin\eta \cdot h^2/2$ = the remainder | $\eta = 60.6632^\circ$ |

They are **different points**, 0.34 of a degree apart.
The MVT point sits almost exactly at the midpoint, which is the usual heuristic, but "almost" is doing real work: it is $0.0016^\circ$ above it, not on it.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
Every entry in this whole catalog is the same five moves; what changes between families is only which of $f$, $a$ or $dx$ the examiner made awkward.
Every value below was recomputed from its closed form and independently re-derived formula-blind, and every direction was taken from the sign of $f''$ rather than from the comparison itself.

### Family 0 - a sine at a special angle, small degree offset

**Trigger:** $\sin$ of a whole number of degrees within a few degrees of $0, 30, 45, 60, 90$.
**Tool:** convert $dx$, then $L = \sin a + \cos a\,dx$.

| Question | Anchor | Exact estimate | Decimal | Error | Direction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin 62^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$ | $0.8834787$ | $+5.3110\text{e-}4$ | over |
| $\sin 59^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $\frac{\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\pi}{360}$ | $0.8572988$ | $+1.3146\text{e-}4$ | over |
| $\sin 31^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $\frac12 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}$ | $0.5151150$ | $+7.6920\text{e-}5$ | over |

Three entries, three different pairs of exact values, one method.
The signs are all positive because $\sin$ is concave down across the whole of $(0^\circ, 180^\circ)$.

### Family 1 - the other trigonometric functions

**Trigger:** $\cos$, $\tan$, $\sec$ instead of $\sin$.
**Tool:** the right derivative, and a fresh look at the concavity, **which is not always the same direction**.

| Question | Anchor | $f'(a)$ | Exact estimate | Decimal | Direction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cos 62^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | $\frac12 - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{90}$ | $0.4697700$ | over |
| $\cos 28^\circ$ | $30^\circ$ | $-\frac12$ | $\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac{\pi}{180}$ | $0.8834787$ | over |
| $\tan 46^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $2$ | $1 + \frac{\pi}{90}$ | $1.0349066$ | **under** |
| $\sec 61^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $2\sqrt3$ | $2 + 2\sqrt3\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}$ | $2.0604600$ | **under** |

Two things to see here.

**The second row is the first row of Family 0.**
$\cos 28^\circ$ from $30^\circ$ produces *the same double* as $\sin 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$, because reflecting through $45^\circ$ maps one problem onto the other.
That is the sharpest self-check in this whole document: if a $\cos$ question and a $\sin$ question are mirror images, their estimates must agree exactly.

**The direction flips for $\tan$ and $\sec$.**
$\tan$ is concave **up** at $45^\circ$ and $\sec$ is concave up at $60^\circ$, so those two are underestimates.
A student who has learned "linearization overestimates" from three sine problems will get both wrong.

### Family 2 - the argument is already in radians

**Trigger:** no degree sign. $\sin(1.6)$, $\cos(0.05)$.
**Tool:** no conversion at all, and often a *flat* anchor.

| Question | Anchor | $f'(a)$ | Estimate | Error |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin 1.6$ | $\pi/2$ | $0$ | $1$ | $+4.2640\text{e-}4$ |
| $\cos 0.05$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1$ | $+1.2497\text{e-}3$ |
| $\sin 0.1$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0.1$ | $+1.6658\text{e-}4$ |

The first two are the interesting shape.
$f'(a) = 0$ makes the linearization a **constant**, so the estimate does not move at all.
That is not an error; it is what a horizontal tangent means.
It is also why those two are among the least accurate entries in the catalog: with the linear term gone, the whole error sits in the quadratic one.

### Family 3 - the anchor is 0, and the answer is a small-angle rule

**Trigger:** an argument near zero.
**Tool:** recognise that you already know the answer.

| Linearize at $0$ | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | $\sin x \approx x$ |
| $\tan x$ | $\tan x \approx x$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\cos x \approx 1$ |

**These are the small-angle formulas, and they are just this question with $a = 0$.**
Note carefully that linearizing $\cos$ gives $1$, **not** $1 - x^2/2$.
The familiar quadratic rule is a second-order Taylor result and is not what this method produces; writing it would be answering a different question.

$\sin$ and $\tan$ do unusually well here because $f''(0) = 0$, so their error is *cubic*: shrinking the step by 10 cuts the error by about 1000, not by 100.
$\cos$ does not get that gift, which is why $\cos 0.05$ manages only two correct decimal places.

### Family 4 - a trigonometric function inside a composite

**Trigger:** $x\sin x$, $\sin 2x$, $\sin(x^2)$.
**Tool:** product or chain rule for $f'$, then the identical five moves.

| Question | $f'$ | $f'(a)$ | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(x) = x\sin x$ at $x = 0.55$, $a = \pi/6$ | $\sin x + x\cos x$ | $\frac12 + \frac{\pi}{6}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ | product rule |
| $f(x) = \sin 2x$ at $x = 31^\circ$, $a = 30^\circ$ | $2\cos 2x$ | $1$ | chain factor 2 |

The second one is worth staring at.
$\sin(2x)$ at $x = 31^\circ$ **is** $\sin 62^\circ$, and the method reproduces the same double from the other side: the chain factor $2$ doubles the correction, while the halved step halves it back.
Drop the chain factor and you halve the correction, which is exactly the error that MT1 items 7, 8 and 9 all test.

### Family 5 - inverse trigonometric functions

**Trigger:** $\arctan(1.02)$, $\arcsin(0.51)$.
**Tool:** the inverse-trig derivative table, and a concavity that is usually the opposite of the direct function's.

| Question | Anchor | $f'(a)$ | Estimate | Direction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\arctan 1.02$ | $1$ | $\frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac12$ | $\frac{\pi}{4} + 0.01 = 0.7953982$ | over |
| $\arcsin 0.51$ | $0.5$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3}$ | $\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{0.02}{\sqrt3} = 0.5351458$ | **under** |

The $\arctan$ entry has $f'(a) = 1/2$, the same slope as the headline question, arrived at by a completely unrelated route.
Note also that the argument of an inverse trig function is a **number**, not an angle, so there is nothing to convert; the answer, however, comes out in radians.

### Family 6 - error propagation with a measured angle

**Trigger:** "a possible error of $\pm 0.5^\circ$", "estimate the error in".
**Tool:** $dy = f'(a)\,dx$ **alone**, with $dx$ the measurement error in radians.
This is the companion question in the bank: one leg of a right triangle is exactly 25 cm, the opposite angle is measured as $60^\circ \pm 0.5^\circ$.

| Quantity | Formula | Value | Differential | Error (cm) | Percentage |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| adjacent | $25\cot\theta$ | $25/\sqrt3 = 14.4338$ | $-25\csc^2\theta\,d\theta = -\frac{100}{3}d\theta$ | $-0.29089$ | $2.0153\%$ |
| hypotenuse | $25\csc\theta$ | $50/\sqrt3 = 28.8675$ | $-25\csc\theta\cot\theta\,d\theta = -\frac{50}{3}d\theta$ | $-0.14544$ | $0.5038\%$ |

with $d\theta = 0.5^\circ = 0.0087266$ rad.

Three things to take from this family.

**Do not add $f(a)$.** The question asks for the error, which is $dy$ by itself. Adding the side length is the single most common wrong answer in this family.

**The percentage errors differ by a factor of exactly 4.** And $4 = \sec^2 60^\circ$. The same half-degree of uncertainty costs four times as much on the adjacent side as on the hypotenuse, and the ratio is a clean trigonometric quantity rather than an accident.

**The conversion matters here too, and more visibly.** Using $0.5$ raw instead of $0.0087266$ gives an "error" of $16.67$ cm on a side that is only $14.43$ cm long, which is impossible.

### Family 7 - the direction of the error, asked directly

**Trigger:** "is your estimate an over- or underestimate?"
**Tool:** the sign of $f''$ on the interval. Nothing else.

| $f$ at the anchor | $f''$ | Tangent | Estimate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin$ at $60^\circ$ | $-0.866$ | above | over |
| $\cos$ at $60^\circ$ | $-0.500$ | above | over |
| $\cos$ at $30^\circ$ | $-0.866$ | above | over |
| $\tan$ at $45^\circ$ | $+4.000$ | below | **under** |
| $\sec$ at $60^\circ$ | $+14.000$ | below | **under** |
| $\arctan$ at $1$ | $-0.500$ | above | over |
| $\arcsin$ at $0.5$ | $+0.770$ | below | **under** |
| $\sqrt{\ }$ at $9$ | $-0.0046$ | above | over |

Five over and three under.
The direction is a property of $f$ **and** the anchor, never of the method.
The last row is Q12.1's question, included so the two documents can be read as one table.

**The exception that proves the rule.** At an inflection point $f''(a) = 0$ and the concavity *changes*, so the direction depends on which way you step. Linearizing $\sin$ at $0$ gives an overestimate for $dx > 0$ and an underestimate for $dx < 0$. If the anchor is $0$ or $180^\circ$, check the side.

### Family 8 - how good is it

**Trigger:** "to four decimal places", "estimate the error in your estimate", "how accurate".
**Tool:** $\dfrac{\max|f''|}{2}dx^2$ over the interval, plus honesty about rounding.

| Entry | $|f''|$ at anchor | Correct places |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin 31^\circ$ from $30^\circ$ | $0.500$ | 3 |
| $\sin 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | $0.866$ | 3 |
| $\cos 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | $0.500$ | 2 |
| $\sec 61^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ | $14.000$ | 2 |
| $\tan 46^\circ$ from $45^\circ$ | $4.000$ | 1 |

The ordering is not simply by $|f''|$, because rounding boundaries interfere: a value can agree at four places and disagree at three.
$\sqrt{16.4} \approx 4.05$ is the extreme case, agreeing with the truth to $3\times10^{-4}$ and yet reporting **zero** correct decimal places, because $4.05$ rounds to $4.1$ where the truth rounds to $4.0$.
Quantize before you claim a place count; do not infer it from the size of the error.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often it actually costs marks.

1. **Not converting degrees to radians.**
   Produces $\sqrt3/2 + 1 = 1.8660$, an error of $0.983$, which is **1851 times** the correct method's error and $111$ percent in relative terms.
   It is the only error in this document that a two-second check catches for free, because no sine exceeds 1.
   Worse, **a smaller step makes it relatively worse, not better**: on a one-degree step the ratio is $3713$, because the blunder's error is linear in the step while the method's is quadratic.
   And it has essentially no hiding place: across the whole first quadrant it never coincides with the truth, except at $90^\circ$ where $\cos a = 0$ kills the term it corrupted.

2. **Spot-checking at the anchor.**
   Of eight plausible wrong formulas, **six agree with the truth exactly when $dx = 0$**, because they differ from it only in the $dy$ term.
   Setting $dx = 0$ to "check the setup" is a test that cannot fail.
   The two survivors are the two that corrupt the $f(a)$ term instead: using the whole target as $dx$, and reporting $dy$ alone.

3. **Dropping $f'(a)$, or evaluating it at the wrong point.**
   The classic hiding points, each sitting where the factor you got wrong happens to equal 1:
   - forgetting $f'(a)$ entirely is invisible at $a = 0$, where $\cos a = 1$;
   - using $\sin a$ as the slope is invisible at $45^\circ$, where $\sin a = \cos a$;
   - a wrong sign on $dy$ is invisible at $90^\circ$, where $\cos a = 0$.
   Compute where your error would hide **before** choosing a check point. At $60^\circ$ with $dx = 2^\circ$ none of the eight hides, so the exam's own numbers are a safe place to check.

4. **Evaluating $f'$ at the target instead of the anchor.**
   This one is nastier than the rest because it is **asymptotically invisible**: its error shrinks with $dx$ exactly as the correct answer's does, so making the step smaller cannot expose it.
   At the exam's own numbers it is wrong by $1.8\times10^{-4}$ in the opposite direction.
   Pleasant consequence worth knowing: the two choices **bracket** the true value, and their average is within $10^{-5}$ of it.

5. **Reporting the true value instead of the estimate.**
   If you check yourself with a calculator, resist correcting $0.8835$ to $0.8829$.
   The question asked for the linearization.

6. **Answering the error question with $f(a) + dy$.**
   In Family 6, "estimate the error" wants $dy$ alone.

7. **Assuming linearization always overestimates.**
   True for $\sin$ and $\cos$ near $60^\circ$; false for $\tan$, $\sec$ and $\arcsin$.
   Look at $f''$ every time.

8. **Choosing an exact anchor that is not the nearest one.**
   $180^\circ$ is a perfectly legal exact anchor and is 2215 times worse than $60^\circ$.

9. **Claiming four correct decimal places.**
   Not one of the twelve trigonometric catalog entries delivers four.

10. **Using a bound taken only at the anchor.**
    For $\sin 62^\circ$ from $60^\circ$ the anchor bound is $5.2761\times10^{-4}$ and the actual error is $5.3110\times10^{-4}$, so the bound is violated.

### The anchor census, since trap 8 deserves numbers

Every one of these is a legitimate exact angle, and the error is measured, not predicted.

| Anchor | Distance to $62^\circ$ | Error | Times worse than $60^\circ$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $60^\circ$ | $2^\circ$ | $5.311\text{e-}4$ | $1.0$ |
| $45^\circ$ | $17^\circ$ | $3.396\text{e-}2$ | $63.9$ |
| $30^\circ$ | $32^\circ$ | $1.007\text{e-}1$ | $189.7$ |
| $90^\circ$ | $28^\circ$ | $1.171\text{e-}1$ | $220.4$ |
| $0^\circ$ | $62^\circ$ | $1.992\text{e-}1$ | $375.0$ |
| $120^\circ$ | $58^\circ$ | $4.892\text{e-}1$ | $921.1$ |
| $135^\circ$ | $73^\circ$ | $7.251\text{e-}1$ | $1365.2$ |
| $150^\circ$ | $88^\circ$ | $9.472\text{e-}1$ | $1783.4$ |
| $180^\circ$ | $118^\circ$ | $1.177$ | $2215.3$ |

**Read the third and fourth rows carefully, because they refute the obvious rule.**
The $30^\circ$ anchor is four degrees *further* from the target than the $90^\circ$ anchor and is still $1.16$ times *better*.
The reason is that the error is $\frac{|f''(a)|}{2}dx^2 = \frac{|\sin a|}{2}dx^2$, and $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$ against $\sin 90^\circ = 1$: the smaller second derivative buys back the extra distance.
The same effect taken to its limit explains why $0^\circ$ beats $120^\circ$ despite being four degrees further: $\sin 0^\circ = 0$, so the quadratic term vanishes entirely and the error there is third order.

So the working rule is **"nearest exact angle"**, and it is right almost always, but the honest rule is **"smallest $|f''(a)|\,dx^2$"**.
Under exam pressure use the first; if two anchors are nearly equidistant, use the second to break the tie.
And note that "nearest" means nearest *among the anchors you can actually find*: a hypothetical exact angle at $61^\circ$ would be four times better than $60^\circ$, so $60^\circ$ is not magic, it is merely the best available.
