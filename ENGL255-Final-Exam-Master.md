### Overview - How to Use This Set

A complete worked set for all four parts of the Rev. 11 final exam, built 2026-08-27 from the
exported course content, the tutor's own exam-prep guidance, and the marked returns for
Assignments 1, 2 and 3.

#### What is here

| Folder | Contents |
|---|---|
| `Part A - Essays/` | 17 complete model essays, one for every prompt in Practice 16.2 to 16.5 and 17.2, covering all five modes of organization |
| `Part B - Summaries/` | 12 complete model summaries, one for each Unit 2 article |
| `Part C - Excerpt Explanations/` | The Leduc memorisation kit, plus 7 worked model answers covering every excerpt role |
| `Part D - Grammar Practice/` | 100 multiple-choice questions, 5 for each of the 20 common errors, with a full answer key |
| `_verification/` | The gate scripts that check every file against the exam rules, and the Part D source data |

#### How to use this, and how not to

These are **model answers written for study**. The exam is invigilated and permits no notes, so
nothing here can be carried into the room. Use them the way a worked solution in a mathematics
text is used: read one, then close it and produce your own under the clock.

Do not memorise an essay and reproduce it. Quite apart from academic integrity, the tutor has
said that he checks whether the tone and style of exam writing match previous submissions, and
prose that does not sound like your assignments would raise exactly that flag. The value of these
files is in their **structure**: five paragraphs, sentence counts inside the limits, a thesis as
the last sentence of the introduction, topic sentences, and consistent person.

A reasonable order of work:

1. Read `00-Exam-Rules-and-Requirements.md` once. It is the specification everything else meets.
2. Part D, twenty minutes a night, working the errors your tutor keeps flagging first.
3. Part C: learn the Leduc kit, then write your own answer and compare it with a worked one.
4. Part B: summarise one article cold in 40 minutes, then compare with the model here.
5. Part A: write one essay in 75 minutes from a prompt, then compare with the model here.

#### What is verified, and how

Every file in Parts A, B and C passes an automated gate. Run them yourself:

```
cd "Final Exam Prep"
python _verification/check_part_a.py "Part A - Essays"
python _verification/check_part_b.py "Part B - Summaries" <folder-with-the-12-source-texts>
python _verification/check_part_c.py "Part C - Excerpt Explanations/C01-worked-model-answers.md" <leduc-source-text>
python _verification/build_part_d.py
```

- **Part A** is checked for 500 to 600 words, exactly five paragraphs, 5 to 12 sentences in every
  paragraph, no first person, consistent person, no contractions. 17/17 pass.
- **Part B** is checked for 150 to 200 words, one paragraph, third person only, at most 30 quoted
  words, an attribution verb, and, most importantly, **no unquoted three-word run that also
  appears in the source article**. That is the tutor's "never more than two unquoted words in a
  row" rule, enforced mechanically against the actual article text. 12/12 pass. The first run
  found 63 genuine lifts across ten summaries, all of which were rewritten.
- **Part C** is checked for 10 to 12 sentences, the title and author in the first sentence, at
  least one quotation, and the same three-word-run rule. 7/7 pass.
- **Part D** is generated from one data file, so the questions and the answer key cannot drift
  apart. The build refuses to write unless there are exactly 100 items, 5 for each of the 20
  errors, with 4 distinct options each. Answer letters are rotated so the correct choice is not
  always in the same slot: A=30, B=24, C=23, D=23.

### Exam Rules and Requirements

Two sources, and where they disagree the stricter one wins.

- **The Study Guide / Course Information (Rev. 11)** gives the format, the marks, and the minutes.
- **The tutor's `Reminders and Additional Guidance + Exam-Prep Information.pdf`** gives the rules
  that decide the marks. Several of them appear nowhere else.

#### The exam

Three hours, online, invigilated, 20% of the course grade. You must score at least 50% on this
exam to pass the course regardless of your assignment marks.

| Part | Task | Marks | Minutes |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Essay, 500-600 words, one of five given topics | 50 | 90 |
| B | Summary of an unfamiliar article supplied with the exam | 20 | 40 |
| C | Explanation of one excerpt chosen from twelve | 10 | 20 |
| D | 20 multiple-choice questions on the 20 common errors | 20 | 20 |

Plus 10 minutes for a final revision pass.

#### Part A rules

From the Study Guide:

- 500 to 600 words.
- One of five modes: process, classification, comparison, cause-effect, or argumentative.
- A title; an introduction; two or three body paragraphs, each opening on a topic sentence; a
  conclusion that sums up and restates the thesis.
- Marked on content, organization, mechanics, grammar, and style.

From the tutor, and these are the ones that cost marks:

- **Write five paragraphs**: an introduction, three body paragraphs, and a conclusion. He states
  this explicitly for the final-exam essay. Four paragraphs is allowed but loses marks for
  undeveloped points.
- **No paragraph below 5 sentences or above 12.** Marks are lost at both ends. The ideal for the
  introduction and the conclusion is 5 or 6 sentences.
- **The thesis is the last sentence of the introduction.**
- **A restatement must use significantly different language** from the original thesis. Repeating
  the sentence does not count as restating it.
- **Person must be consistent**: either third person plural throughout, or second person
  throughout. Do not use the first person. Second person is legitimate in a process essay,
  because a how-to essay is the exception the course names.
- **No citations and no references anywhere in the exam.**
- Sentence fragments, run-ons, and quotation-integration problems are "especially deleterious":
  they reduce content, organisation and style marks as well as grammar.
- For a process essay, apply his test before committing: **if step 3 could come before step 2
  without harming the process, they are not steps.** This is what sent Assignment 3 back for
  revision.

Every essay in `Part A - Essays/` meets all of the above and is machine-checked against it.

#### Part B rules

From the Study Guide:

- One paragraph, roughly 150 to 200 words, on an unfamiliar article supplied with the exam.
- Open with the article title, the author's name, and a restatement of the thesis.
- Follow the ten steps from Chapter 15 of *Read, Think, Write*.

From the tutor:

- **At least 150 and at most 200 words.**
- **Never use more than two unquoted words from the original in a row.** This is the hard line.
- **Quote as the exception, not the rule**: at most 30 quoted words in the whole summary, never
  indented. Aim for none.
- **Brevity must not cost completeness.** In a short article, treat nearly every specific
  supporting point as essential and get it in. This is where Assignment 2 lost its content mark.
- **No opinion, explanation, or analysis of your own.**
- **Signal the author continuously**: "According to...", "Leduc points out that...", "she
  explains that...".
- **Third person only.** No I, me, my, we, us, our. No you, your.
- **Present tense** throughout when describing what a text does.

Every summary in `Part B - Summaries/` meets all of the above, and the two-unquoted-words rule is
enforced mechanically against the actual article text rather than by eye.

#### Part C rules

From the Study Guide:

- Twelve excerpts, one from each Unit 2 essay. Choose one.
- Identify the essay title and the author, then examine the role and significance of the excerpt
  and how it functions in relation to the author's thesis.
- Prepare **one** essay. Do not try to learn all twelve, and do not memorise even one.

From the tutor:

- **Ten to twelve sentences**, one paragraph.
- Place the excerpt in context: where in the essay it falls, and how it relates to the thesis.
- **Exploit most of the essay's supporting points**, because that is what the mark rewards.
- **Choose the article carefully.** He warns against Didion's "On Keeping a Notebook" and both
  Orwell essays as carrying too many supporting points for twenty minutes.
- Memorise the exact title, the author, a thesis restatement in your own words, the main
  supporting points, any named experts, and two or three short quotable phrases.
- The two-unquoted-words rule applies here as well.

#### Part D rules

- 20 multiple-choice questions, one for each error in the "20 Common Errors" document. All twenty
  are represented.
- You are not asked to name the error, only to identify a correct or an incorrect sentence.
- One mark each. If a question stalls you, guess, then move on; your time is better spent
  proofreading the essay.

#### In the room

- One print dictionary. It may be bilingual. The tutor also permits a print thesaurus; the Study
  Guide says a dictionary and no other books, so the dictionary is the safe thing to carry.
- **No notes of any kind.** Everything for Part C has to be in your head.
- No spell-check in the exam software. Reserve the last ten minutes to proofread.
- Formatting is limited: no double-spacing needed, mark a paragraph with a blank line rather than
  an indent, and use quotation marks where you would normally italicise a title.
- Use normal capitalisation. The tutor calls out all-caps and all-lowercase writing specifically.
- Do not retype the prompts. Go straight into the answer.
- Outline on paper, but do not write a full first draft; you will run out of time for the real one.

### A01. Three Kinds of Pop Music

> **Mode:** Classification (Practice 16.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5

Pop music sits near the top of the charts in almost every country. Casual listeners often assume that it is a single style of song. A few minutes with any streaming service proves otherwise. A quiet ballad built around a piano and a club track built around a drum machine are filed under the same heading. What holds the category together is not a shared sound but a shared goal, because every pop song is built to reach the widest possible audience. Pop music divides into three main types: the dance track, the ballad, and the crossover song.

The dance track is the type built for movement rather than for close listening. Its writers begin with a tempo that suits a crowded floor and then fit the melody around that pulse. The rhythm stays loud and steady from the first bar to the last. Lyrics tend to be short, and they often repeat one phrase so that a crowd can shout it back. Producers care more about the drop than about the verse, so the arrangement builds toward a single release of energy. Listeners rarely study these songs for their words, and the writers do not expect them to. The success of a dance track is measured by what a room full of people does when it starts.

The ballad works in the opposite direction, because it slows the tempo and puts one voice at the centre. Its writers build the song around a single situation, usually a loss, a longing, or a promise. Instruments stay sparse in the opening verse so that the words carry the weight. Melodies climb toward a high note near the end, and that climb gives the listener the emotional payoff. Words matter far more here than in a dance track, so writers spend their effort on images and on specific detail. Ballads travel well across generations, because the feelings they describe do not date quickly. A ballad succeeds when a listener sitting alone hears it as a description of a private experience.

The crossover song is the type that begins in another genre and then arrives in the pop charts. Its writers keep the instruments and the vocal habits of the original style, whether that style is country, hip hop, or rock. They then simplify the structure until it fits a radio format, shortening the introduction and tightening the chorus. Artists who record these songs reach two audiences at once, which explains why the type appears so often near the top of the charts. Purists in the original genre frequently complain that the result has been thinned out for strangers. Listeners who discover an artist through a crossover song often work backward into a catalogue they would never have found otherwise. The type therefore serves as a door between musical worlds rather than as a world of its own.

Sorting pop music into these three groups gives listeners a vocabulary for something they already hear. One group exists to move a crowd, a second exists to hold a single listener still, and a third exists to carry an artist across a border. None of the three is more serious than the others, because each solves a different problem for the people who make it. A listener who knows which type a song belongs to can explain a preference instead of simply naming a favourite. The one broad label on the charts conceals three distinct kinds of writing, each with its own purpose and its own measure of success.

### A02. Three Kinds of Post-Secondary Institution

> **Mode:** Classification (Practice 16.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5

Students leaving high school are told to apply to college, as though every campus offered the same thing. The buildings look alike in the brochures, and the promises sound alike as well. Behind that surface, post-secondary institutions differ in what they teach, in how long they take, and in what a graduate can do afterward. A student who chooses the wrong kind of school loses years and money before discovering the mistake. Sorting the options by purpose rather than by reputation makes the decision much clearer. Post-secondary institutions fall into three main types: the research university, the technical college, and the open university.

The research university is the type organized around the production of new knowledge. Its faculty members are hired for what they publish rather than for how they teach, and their graduate students carry much of the undergraduate instruction. Programs run for four years or longer, and the early courses are broad by design. Students spend a first year on subjects far outside their intended field, which frustrates anyone in a hurry. The reward for that patience is a credential that opens doors to graduate study and to professions with licensing requirements. Class sizes in the first two years are large, so students who need attention must go looking for it. This type suits people who want depth, credentials, and time to change their minds.

The technical college is the type organized around a specific job. Its instructors have usually worked in the trade they teach, and their courses are built from tasks rather than from theory. Programs run for one or two years, and almost every course connects directly to work a graduate will perform. Employers often help design the curriculum, so the equipment in the shop matches the equipment in the industry. Graduates enter the labour market quickly and carry very little debt by comparison with university graduates. The narrowness that makes this type efficient also makes it risky, because a trade can shrink. This type suits people who know what work they want and want to start it soon.

The open university is the type organized around access rather than around a campus. Its courses arrive online or by mail, its terms start throughout the year, and its students study from wherever they live. Admission requirements are minimal, so adults who left school early can begin without first repairing an old transcript. Students set their own pace, which helps people with jobs and families and defeats people who need a schedule imposed on them. Instruction comes through written materials and a tutor rather than through a lecture hall. Completion rates are lower here than at the other two types, largely because this type admits people whose lives are complicated. It suits people who need a degree to fit around obligations that will not move.

These three kinds of institution answer three different questions. One asks what is true, another asks how a job is done, and the third asks how a working adult can study at all. A student who names the question that matters most has effectively made the choice already. Comparing schools by prestige hides that question instead of answering it. The single word college covers three distinct arrangements, and knowing which one is on offer matters more than knowing whose name is better known.

### A03. Three Kinds of Profile on a Dating Site

> **Mode:** Classification (Practice 16.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5

Anyone who has spent an evening scrolling through a dating application knows the strange feeling of repetition. The photographs change and the names change, yet the profiles sort themselves into a small number of familiar shapes. That pattern is not an accident, because people join these sites for very different reasons and those reasons show in the writing. A user who learns to read the shapes wastes far less time on conversations that were never going to lead anywhere. The categories become easy to recognize once someone has been told to look for them. Profiles on a dating site fall into three main types: the browser, the auditioner, and the seeker.

The browser is the type who joined out of curiosity rather than out of intent. Profiles of this kind are short, often three lines or fewer, and the photographs are casual to the point of carelessness. Browsers answer messages slowly and let conversations die without explanation, because nothing in their week depends on the outcome. Their descriptions avoid any statement that could be held against them later. Questions about what they want produce answers such as seeing what happens or open to anything. Browsers are not dishonest, and many of them would say plainly that they are undecided if anyone asked. Their profiles waste other time only because other users assume that everyone on the site shares one purpose.

The auditioner is the type who treats a profile as a performance. Photographs of this kind are numerous and carefully staged, usually including travel, exercise, and one shot with a borrowed dog. The writing lists accomplishments and hobbies at length, and it reads like a summary of qualifications. Auditioners respond quickly and pleasantly, but they steer every conversation toward their own material rather than toward the other person. What they want from the site is confirmation that the performance works. Some of them are genuinely looking for a partner and have simply confused visibility with connection. Their profiles are the easiest to admire and the hardest to hold a conversation with.

The seeker is the type who joined with a specific outcome in mind and says so. Profiles of this kind name a few concrete things, such as a city, a stage of life, and what the person hopes to build. Photographs are ordinary and few, because the writing is doing the work. Seekers reply with questions rather than with statements, and they move toward a meeting fairly early. They also end conversations directly when the fit is wrong, which reads as coldness and is in fact a courtesy. Their profiles are the least glamorous on any site and the most likely to lead somewhere. Users who want a relationship rather than an audience learn to look for this type first.

These three kinds of profile explain why a dating site can feel both crowded and empty. One group is passing the time, a second is looking for applause, and only the third is looking for a person. Nobody is behaving badly in this arrangement, because all three purposes are legitimate and none of them is announced. Frustration on these sites usually comes from assuming that everyone shares a single goal. Reading a profile for its purpose rather than for its photographs turns an exhausting search into a manageable one.

### A04. Three Kinds of Parent at the Rink

> **Mode:** Classification (Practice 16.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5

A youth hockey rink at seven in the morning is one of the best places to watch adults reveal themselves. The children on the ice are doing much the same thing, but the parents in the stands are not. Over a single season the same figures appear in the same seats, and their behaviour follows patterns clear enough to name. Those patterns matter, because a child learns what winning and losing mean mainly from the adult who drives them home. What looks like a crowd of individuals is really a small set of recognizable positions. Parents in the stands fall into three main types: the coach, the agent, and the witness.

The coach is the type who cannot stop instructing. Parents of this kind stand at the glass, call out corrections during play, and continue the lesson in the parking lot afterward. Their instructions are usually accurate, because many of them played the game and know it well. The difficulty is one of volume rather than of content, since a child on the ice already has a coach and cannot serve two. Children of these parents often develop sound technique and an anxious habit of looking to the stands after every mistake. The behaviour comes from love and from a wish to be useful. It also teaches a child that performance is something owed to an audience.

The agent is the type who is managing a career that does not yet exist. Parents of this kind track ice time, compare their child with other children by name, and speak to officials about fairness. Their conversations circle around scouts, divisions, and the year when selection begins to matter. They treat every game as evidence in a longer case rather than as an event of its own. Children of these parents learn early that the score is attached to their future, which is a heavy thing to carry at nine years old. Some of these parents are responding to a real opportunity, and a few of their children do go far. The cost is that the game stops being a game long before the child is ready for that.

The witness is the type who came to watch. Parents of this kind sit down, follow the play, cheer for goals at both ends of the rink, and say very little on the drive home unless they are asked. They know the rules and the standings, so their quiet is a choice rather than ignorance. Their questions afterward tend to be about whether the child enjoyed the morning. Children of these parents make the same mistakes as everyone else and recover from them faster, because nothing at home depends on the result. This type is the least visible in the stands and the most obvious in the players. Watching without commentary turns out to be much harder than it looks.

These three kinds of parent are watching the same game and seeing three different things. One sees a lesson, a second sees an audition, and the third sees a child playing. All three care, which is why the differences are worth naming rather than mocking. The type a parent belongs to is not fixed either, and most people move between them depending on the morning. What a child carries home from the rink depends far less on the score than on which of those three drove them there.

### A05. What a Dog Asks and What a Cat Asks

> **Mode:** Comparison (Practice 16.3)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Point-by-point (attention, training, absence)

Households across the country choose between a dog and a cat as though the decision were a matter of taste. Advertising encourages that view by showing both animals as interchangeable sources of comfort. The two species in fact make very different demands on the people who keep them, and those demands last for well over a decade. A household that misjudges the difference ends up with a miserable animal and a resentful owner. The honest comparison is not about which animal is more lovable but about what each one requires. Dogs and cats differ most clearly in the attention they need, in the way they learn, and in what happens when their owners leave.

The first difference lies in the amount of attention each animal requires. Dogs are social animals whose ancestors lived and hunted in groups, and that history still governs their behaviour. A dog expects company for most of the day and shows distress when it does not get any. Owners of dogs therefore arrange their schedules around walks, feeding times, and the simple business of being present. Cats descend from solitary hunters, and their tolerance for company is genuine but limited. A cat will seek contact on its own terms and then withdraw without warning or apology. The practical result is that a dog needs an owner and a cat accepts one.

The second difference lies in how each animal learns. Dogs respond strongly to approval, which makes instruction relatively straightforward for a patient owner. A dog offered praise and food will repeat a behaviour until it becomes reliable, and this is why dogs work as guides, as detectors, and as herders. Training a dog costs time in the first year and returns that time for the rest of the animal life. Cats learn just as quickly but do not treat human approval as a reward worth working for. A cat changes its behaviour when the environment changes, so owners manage cats by arranging the house rather than by giving instructions. Neither animal is more intelligent than the other, but only one of them takes direction.

The third difference appears when the owner leaves. A dog left alone for a working day may bark, chew furniture, or wait by the door without moving. Kennels, walkers, and neighbours exist largely because dogs cannot be left for long, and those services cost money every time an owner travels. A cat left alone with food, water, and a clean box is generally content for a weekend. Owners of cats can accept an unexpected trip without arranging a network of helpers first. This difference decides the question for many households more than temperament ever does. The animal that suits a person best is usually the one that suits that person schedule.

A dog and a cat are not two versions of the same companion. One asks for constant company, learns through approval, and cannot easily be left behind. The other asks for occasional company, learns through its surroundings, and manages well on its own. A household that wants a partner in daily life should choose the first, and a household that wants a quieter presence should choose the second. Matching the animal to the life already being lived prevents most of the unhappiness that follows this decision.

### A06. Two Ways to Take a Course

> **Mode:** Comparison (Practice 16.3)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Point-by-point (schedule, contact with instructors, self-direction)

Universities now offer most subjects in two forms, and students are asked to choose between them at registration. The course code is often identical, the textbook is identical, and the credential at the end is identical as well. Those similarities hide differences that decide whether a student finishes the course or abandons it. A student who selects a format for convenience alone frequently discovers the cost halfway through a term. The two formats suit different lives rather than different levels of ability. In-person learning and online learning differ most in the schedules they impose, in the contact they allow with instructors, and in the amount of self-direction they demand.

The first difference lies in the schedule. An in-person course fixes the hours in advance and expects students to arrange everything else around them. That rigidity is inconvenient for anyone with a job, but it also does a great deal of work on the student behalf. A student who must appear in a room on Tuesday morning studies before Tuesday morning, because the deadline is public and immediate. An online course removes the fixed hours entirely and replaces them with a set of due dates weeks apart. Students who work shifts or care for families can finally fit study into the gaps in their week. The freedom is real, and so is the danger that nothing gets done until a deadline is close.

The second difference lies in contact with instructors. An in-person course produces contact almost by accident, because a question asked at the end of a lecture takes thirty seconds to answer. Students in a classroom also learn from questions they never thought to ask, since someone else asks them first. An online course makes every contact deliberate, requiring an email, a forum post, or a scheduled call. That formality raises the cost of asking, so small confusions often go unspoken until they have grown into large ones. Written answers from a tutor are frequently more careful than a hallway reply, which is a genuine advantage for a student who reads them. The exchange is better in quality and much poorer in frequency.

The third difference lies in how much self-direction the format requires. An in-person course distributes the work of managing a term across an instructor, a timetable, and a room full of classmates. Students in that setting can measure their own progress against the people sitting beside them. An online course transfers all of that management to the student, who must decide each week what to read and when to write. Nobody notices a student who quietly falls behind until the grades arrive, and by then recovery is difficult. Students with strong habits often prefer this arrangement, because it removes hours of travel and of waiting. Students still building those habits find that the format tests exactly the skill they lack.

The two formats are not a strong option and a weak one. One supplies structure, easy contact, and a fixed rhythm at the cost of flexibility. The other supplies flexibility at the cost of structure, and it charges the difference to the student own discipline. A student with an unpredictable week and reliable habits should take the online section, and a student with an open week and uncertain habits should take the classroom section. Choosing on that basis rather than on convenience is what turns a registration decision into a completed course.

> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5

Two companies supply almost every mobile phone in use today, and their products look increasingly alike. Both offer a glass rectangle, a good camera, and a store full of the same applications. Buyers therefore treat the decision as a question of brand loyalty or of price on the day. The real difference lies in two opposing beliefs about who should be in charge of a device. That difference shapes the experience of owning the phone for as long as the owner keeps it. Apple phones and Android phones differ most in how much control they give the owner, in the range of hardware available, and in what the phone costs over its full life.

The first difference lies in control over the device. Apple designs the hardware and the software together and decides in advance how the two will behave. Owners receive a system that works consistently and that resists being altered, which removes both risk and choice. Applications reach the phone through a single store, and that store rejects anything the company considers unsafe or untidy. Android takes the opposite position and treats the phone as equipment belonging to whoever bought it. Owners can replace the launcher, install software from outside the official store, and change defaults that Apple keeps locked. One approach protects users from their own mistakes, and the other trusts them to make some.

The second difference lies in the range of hardware. Apple releases a handful of models each year at a narrow set of prices, all built to a similar standard. A buyer choosing among them compares screen size and camera quality and very little else, which makes the decision quick. Android runs on hardware from dozens of manufacturers, covering everything from inexpensive handsets to folding screens. A buyer in that market can find a rugged phone, a phone with an enormous battery, or a phone that costs less than a pair of shoes. The variety is genuinely useful for anyone with an unusual requirement or a small budget. It also means that the quality of an Android phone depends heavily on which one the buyer chose.

Apple prices its phones high and then supports them with system updates for a long time, so a single device can remain current for many years. Owners also find that the resale value stays comparatively strong, which reduces the true cost of the next upgrade. Android phones usually cost less at purchase, and the cheapest of them cost dramatically less. Support varies a great deal by manufacturer, and many models stop receiving updates while they are still working perfectly well. A buyer who keeps a phone for five years and a buyer who replaces one every two years reach opposite conclusions from the same prices. The cheaper phone is not always the cheaper decision.

These two families of phone are answers to a single question about ownership. One answer promises consistency, a short menu, and a long supported life in exchange for a high price and limited freedom. The other promises choice, a wide range of prices, and control of the device in exchange for uneven quality and shorter support. Buyers who want the phone to simply work should take the first, and buyers who want the phone to do what they tell it should take the second. Naming that preference first turns a confusing purchase into a straightforward one.

### A08. Two Ways to Write a Popular Song

> **Mode:** Comparison (Practice 16.3)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Point-by-point (subject matter, storytelling, relationship with the audience)

Pop music and country music sit side by side on every streaming service and share many of the same producers. Both aim at large audiences, both rely on a strong chorus, and both now use the same recording technology. Listeners who dismiss one of them usually do so on the strength of an accent rather than of an argument. The two styles are in fact built on different assumptions about what a popular song is for. Those assumptions show up in the words long before they show up in the instruments. Pop and country songs differ most in the subjects they choose, in the way they handle a story, and in the relationship they build with an audience.

The first difference lies in subject matter. Pop songs concentrate on states of feeling and describe them in terms broad enough to fit almost any listener. A pop lyric about heartbreak avoids naming a town, a job, or a year, because a detail of that kind would exclude somebody. The result travels easily across borders and languages, which is exactly what the industry behind it needs. Country songs move in the opposite direction and reach for the particular. A country lyric names the truck, the county, the bar, and the month, and it expects those details to do the emotional work. One style aims to be about everyone and the other aims to be about someone.

The second difference lies in the handling of a story. A pop song usually presents a situation rather than a sequence, holding one emotional moment steady while the arrangement builds around it. Verses in that structure repeat the feeling from slightly different angles, and the chorus states it plainly. A country song more often runs on narrative, moving through events in order and saving a change of meaning for the final verse. Listeners of country music are accustomed to a twist, and songwriters in that tradition are judged on how well they deliver one. This is why country lyrics reward attention and pop lyrics survive being half heard in a shop. The two styles ask for different amounts of listening.

The third difference lies in the relationship each style builds with its audience. Pop presents its performers as figures set apart, and the staging, the wardrobe, and the videos all work to maintain that distance. Fans of a pop artist admire someone they are not expected to resemble. Country presents its performers as neighbours who happen to have a record deal, and the interviews, the clothing, and the song subjects all support that impression. Fans of a country artist are invited to believe that the singer grew up somewhere much like their own town. Neither presentation is more honest than the other, since both are managed carefully by people who are paid to manage them. They simply promise different things: one promises glamour and the other promises kinship.

Pop and country are two solutions to the same commercial problem. One reaches a huge audience by removing every detail that might not apply, and the other reaches a loyal audience by supplying details that apply exactly. One offers a feeling held still, and the other offers a story that moves. One asks to be admired from a distance, and the other asks to be recognized as familiar. A listener who understands those choices can enjoy both without pretending that they are doing the same job.

### A09. Why Students Cheat

> **Mode:** Cause and effect (Practice 16.4)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Causes essay (three causes of cheating in schools)

Every school publishes a policy on academic misconduct, and every school catches students breaking it. Administrators respond by tightening the rules, adding detection software, and raising the penalties. Those measures treat cheating as a failure of character that can be corrected by fear. Rates of misconduct nevertheless remain steady from one decade to the next, which suggests that the diagnosis is wrong. Cheating persists because the conditions that produce it have never been addressed. Three causes account for most academic dishonesty: the pressure of consequences attached to grades, the absence of any real instruction in how to use sources, and the low perceived risk of being caught.

The first cause is the weight schools attach to a small number of grades. A single mark can decide entry to a program, the renewal of a scholarship, or the approval of a visa. Students facing that arithmetic stop asking what they have learned and start asking what the transcript will say. A grade that carries consequences of that size converts an assignment into a threat rather than an exercise. Under threat, most people take the shortest route to safety, and copying is the shortest route available. Schools that raise the stakes therefore create the pressure and then punish the response to it. The behaviour becomes far more understandable once the size of the consequence is taken seriously.

The second cause is that students are rarely taught how to use sources properly. Most institutions assume that a first-year student already knows how to paraphrase, when to cite, and what counts as common knowledge. Those skills are technical, and nobody acquires them by reading a policy that lists penalties. A student who does not know how far a paraphrase must move from the original will produce work that matches the source too closely. That student has committed plagiarism by the definition in the calendar, without any intention to deceive. Detection software cannot distinguish between the two situations, so both are recorded as misconduct. A large share of what schools call cheating is simply an untaught skill showing up in a graded assignment.

The third cause is that the risk of being caught looks small from a student desk. Large classes, heavy marking loads, and standard assignment topics all reduce the chance that any single submission is examined closely. Students compare notes and learn quickly that few people they know have ever faced a hearing. That impression persists even at schools with active integrity offices, because the cases are handled quietly. A rational calculation follows: a certain benefit now against an unlikely penalty later. Raising penalties does nothing to this calculation, because the term that matters is the probability rather than the punishment. Schools that increase the chance of detection change behaviour, and schools that increase the severity of sentences generally do not.

These three causes work together, and none of them concerns dishonesty as a personal trait. High stakes create the motive, missing instruction creates the opportunity for accidental offences, and low detection removes the deterrent. A school serious about reducing misconduct would therefore spread assessment across more work, teach source use as a skill in its own right, and vary assignments so that copied answers stop fitting. Tightening the rules alone leaves all three conditions untouched. Cheating is best understood as the predictable result of a system rather than as a flaw in the students who pass through it.

### A10. What Bullying Leaves Behind

> **Mode:** Cause and effect (Practice 16.4)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Effects essay (three effects of bullying)

Adults tend to describe bullying as an unpleasant stage that most children pass through and forget. That description is comforting and it is wrong. Bullying is a sustained experience of being made powerless in a place a child is required by law to attend. Experiences of that kind do not end when the school year ends, because they change how a person expects to be treated. The damage is easiest to see in three areas, and none of them is the bruise that adults look for first. Bullying produces three lasting effects on the people who endure it: a decline in schoolwork, a withdrawal from ordinary social risk, and a lasting distrust of authority.

The first effect appears in schoolwork. A child under threat spends a large share of attention on watching for danger, and attention is the resource that learning consumes. Hallways, changing rooms, and lunch periods become problems to be solved rather than parts of a day. Students in that position begin to avoid the places where the trouble happens, and the avoidance costs them class time. Marks fall, and the falling marks attract exactly the adult attention that the student has learned not to trust. Teachers who see only the record often conclude that the student has stopped caring. The academic damage is therefore doubled, because the symptom is mistaken for the cause.

The second effect appears in social behaviour. Being singled out teaches a precise lesson: attention is dangerous, and standing out invites harm. Children who learn that lesson stop volunteering answers, stop joining teams, and stop putting their names forward for anything. The strategy works, which is why it persists long after the original threat has gone. Adults who were bullied as children frequently describe an unwillingness to speak in meetings or to apply for work they could do well. What looks like shyness or modesty is often a defence that was rational at twelve and expensive at thirty. The habit outlives the danger by decades.

The third effect appears in the relationship with authority. Most bullying happens within sight of adults who could stop it, and many victims report the trouble at least once. When the response is slow, or when the adult advises the child to ignore the problem, a conclusion follows. The child learns that the people who hold the rules will not enforce them on their behalf. That conclusion generalizes far beyond the school and shapes later dealings with managers, police, and institutions of every kind. Adults carrying it are slower to report harassment at work and slower to expect that reporting will help. The failure of one teacher to act can therefore change how a person treats every authority afterward.

These three effects share a single root, which is the lesson that protection cannot be relied on. Schoolwork suffers because attention is spent on safety, social life narrows because visibility has become dangerous, and trust in authority collapses because authority did not intervene. Programs that focus only on separating the children involved leave that lesson intact. The interventions that work are the ones that demonstrate to a child that reporting produces action. Bullying is not a phase to be waited out but an education in powerlessness, and the curriculum sticks.

### A11. Where a Phobia Comes From

> **Mode:** Cause and effect (Practice 16.4)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Causes essay (three causes of a phobia)

A phobia looks irrational from the outside, which is why sufferers are so often told to be sensible. Somebody who cannot enter a lift, cross a bridge, or sit through a flight already knows that the danger is small. Being reminded of that fact changes nothing, because the fear was never built out of an argument. Phobias are learned responses, and they are learned through routes that have nothing to do with reasoning. Understanding those routes explains both why the fear appeared and why logic fails to remove it. A phobia usually develops through three causes: a frightening first experience, the example set by other people, and the avoidance that follows.

The first cause is a single frightening experience attached to a harmless object or situation. A child bitten by a dog, trapped briefly in a lift, or badly frightened during turbulence forms an association between the setting and the alarm. That association is created by a part of the brain built for speed rather than for accuracy, so one episode is enough. The link survives afterward even when the person can explain perfectly well that most dogs do not bite. This is why sufferers can describe the original event in detail and still be unable to reason their way past it. The memory that matters is stored as a physical response rather than as a belief. Arguments aimed at the belief leave the response untouched.

The second cause is learning the fear from other people rather than from an event. Children watch how the adults around them behave and adopt those reactions long before they can evaluate them. A parent who freezes at the sight of a spider teaches a lesson more effectively than any warning could. Fears also travel through description alone, since a vivid story about a snake or a needle can establish the same association without any encounter. Whole families frequently share a phobia that none of them can trace to a first incident. The fear in these cases was inherited socially rather than genetically. Its origin is invisible, which makes it especially difficult for the sufferer to challenge.

The third cause is avoidance, which is what turns a fear into a phobia. Somebody frightened of lifts begins taking the stairs, and the relief that follows is immediate and powerful. That relief rewards the avoidance and strengthens it, so the behaviour repeats and then spreads. Stairs give way to refusing tall buildings, and refusing tall buildings gives way to turning down work in the city. Each act of avoidance also removes the only evidence that could correct the original fear, because nothing safe ever happens in a lift that is never entered. The fear therefore grows in the absence of any new frightening experience. Avoidance is the reason phobias intensify over years rather than fading.

These three causes explain why a phobia resists common sense so completely. An event or an example installs the association, and avoidance then protects it from every piece of contrary evidence. Treatment works when it reverses the third cause, exposing the person gradually to the feared situation until the alarm subsides on its own. That approach succeeds precisely because it supplies the experience that avoidance has been preventing. Telling somebody that the fear is unreasonable addresses none of the three causes, which is why it has never helped anyone.

### A12. How to Plan a First Date

> **Mode:** Process (Practice 16.5)
>
> **Person:** Second person
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **Sequence test:** Stage 2 depends on the time agreed in stage 1; stage 3 depends on the place chosen in stage 2. No stage can be moved.

A first date fails far more often in the planning than in the meeting itself. Two people who would have enjoyed each other end the evening exhausted, because the arrangements worked against them from the start. The usual mistake is to choose a restaurant before settling anything else, which fixes the hardest decision first. Planning in the right order removes most of the awkwardness that people blame on chemistry. The stages below must be followed in sequence, since each one supplies the information the next one needs. Planning a first date well involves three stages: settling the time, choosing the setting, and preparing on the day.

Begin by confirming that the other person wants to go and by agreeing on a time. Ask directly rather than hinting, because a vague invitation forces the other person to guess at what is being proposed. Offer two specific options instead of asking what suits, since an open question puts the whole burden of planning on them. Listen carefully to how the answer is framed, because an evening that has to end early tells you something useful. Agree on a day, a start time, and a rough finish before any other decision is made. Write the arrangement down and confirm it a day or two beforehand. Everything that follows depends on the answers you collect at this stage.

Choose the setting only once the time is fixed, because the time decides what is possible. A weekday evening after work rules out anything that requires travel or daylight, while a Saturday afternoon opens both. Pick somewhere quiet enough for conversation, since the point of the meeting is to find out whether you can talk to each other. Avoid a cinema or a concert for a first meeting, as neither allows a single word for two hours. Choose a place you can leave easily and where staying longer is also possible, so that the evening can follow whatever happens. Check the opening hours and book a table if the place takes bookings. Decide what to wear by matching the setting you chose rather than by dressing up as far as possible. Arrive a few minutes early and put the phone away before the other person appears. These small preparations remove the distractions that would otherwise take your attention during the conversation.

Following these three stages in order turns an anxious evening into a manageable one. Fixing the time first gives you the constraints, choosing the setting second fits the meeting to those constraints, and preparing last removes the practical worries. Reversing any two of these stages creates the problem that the next stage was supposed to solve. None of this guarantees that the two of you will suit each other, and nothing could. What careful planning does guarantee is that the evening gets a fair test, which is all a first date was ever supposed to provide.

### A13. How to Tie a Shoelace

> **Mode:** Process (Practice 16.5)
>
> **Person:** Second person
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **Sequence test:** The starting knot cannot be tied before the slack is removed, and the bow cannot be formed before the starting knot holds. No stage can be moved.

Almost every adult ties a shoelace without thinking, and almost every adult ties it badly. The proof is on any pavement, where laces come undone hundreds of times a day on shoes that were fastened only minutes earlier. The fault is rarely the lace and almost never the shoe. A knot that slips has usually been built on a foundation that was loose before the bow was ever formed. Teaching the task properly means slowing it down into stages that must happen in a fixed order. Tying a shoelace reliably involves three stages: removing the slack, tying the starting knot, and forming the bow.

Start by removing the slack from the whole lace, working from the toe upward. Take hold of the two crossings nearest the toe and pull them apart until the lace sits snugly across the top of the foot. Repeat that movement at each pair of eyelets in turn, moving one crossing at a time toward the ankle. Do not skip ahead to the top pair, because slack left near the toe simply travels upward again as soon as you walk. Finish by drawing the two loose ends until the shoe feels firm but not tight across the instep. Check that the two ends are roughly equal in length, and adjust them now rather than later. A lace that is even at this point will produce a knot that sits straight.

Tie the starting knot next, on the foundation you have just created. Cross the right end over the left so that the two ends form an X directly above the eyelets. Take the end that is now on top, pass it under the crossing, and bring it out on the far side. Pull both ends steadily outward, away from each other, until the crossing tightens against the tongue of the shoe. Keep the tension even in both hands, because pulling harder on one side drags the knot off centre. Hold this knot down with a thumb while you move on, since releasing it now lets the slack return. This half knot carries the whole fastening, and every problem later begins here.

Form the bow last, once the starting knot is holding firm. Make a loop in one end by folding it back on itself, and pinch the base of that loop between finger and thumb. Wrap the other end once around the base of the loop, passing it away from you and around the back. Push a fold of that second end through the small gap you have just created, and take hold of it on the other side. Pull the two loops apart, not the loose ends, until the bow tightens against the starting knot. Check the result by looking at how the bow sits: a bow lying across the shoe is secure, and a bow pointing along the shoe will work loose. If it points the wrong way, undo it and reverse the direction of the wrap.

Working through these three stages in order produces a knot that lasts the day. The slack has to go first, because no knot can hold a lace that is loose beneath it. The starting knot has to come second, because the bow needs something firm to sit against. The bow comes last and takes only a moment once the two earlier stages have been done properly. A lace that keeps coming undone is almost never a mystery, and the fault will be found in whichever of these stages was hurried.

### A14. How to Learn to Give an Effective Speech

> **Mode:** Process (Practice 16.5)
>
> **Person:** Second person
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **Sequence test:** You cannot rehearse a speech that has not been built, and you cannot usefully rehearse in front of listeners before you have rehearsed alone. No stage can be moved.
>
> **Note:** This is the prompt Minh named. Read this one first.

Most people believe that public speaking is a talent, and that belief is the main reason they never improve at it. Confidence in front of an audience is a result rather than a starting point, and it is produced by work done long before anyone is watching. Speakers who appear natural have almost always rehearsed in ways their listeners never see. The work involved is ordinary, and it follows a fixed order that cannot be shuffled. Skipping a stage is what produces the familiar disaster of a speaker reading nervously from a page. Learning to give an effective speech involves three stages: building the speech, rehearsing it alone, and rehearsing it in front of listeners.

Build the speech before you think about delivery at all. Decide first what you want the audience to do or believe differently when you finish, and write that outcome in one sentence. Reduce the whole speech to a single message, because an audience listening once will carry away one idea and no more. Choose three points that support the message, and find a concrete example for each one, since examples are what listeners actually remember. Write the opening and the closing out in full, as those are the two moments where nerves cause the most damage. Leave the middle as an outline of phrases rather than as continuous prose, so that you will speak it rather than recite it. Do not move on until the outline fits on a single page.

Rehearse alone next, out loud and on your feet. Speaking the words aloud is the only way to find the sentences that look fine on paper and collapse in the mouth. Time yourself from the first rehearsal, and cut material rather than speaking faster when you run over. Repeat the opening and the closing until you can deliver both without looking down, as knowing those two passages carries you through the worst of the nerves. Record one run on a phone and watch it, however uncomfortable that is, because it shows you habits that you cannot feel while speaking. Work on the outline itself when a passage keeps failing, since a sentence that will not be spoken is usually a sentence that was badly built. Continue until you can get through the whole speech without stopping.

Rehearse in front of listeners only when the solo run is steady. Ask one or two people to sit through it, and ask them for specific responses rather than for reassurance. A useful question is what they think the single message was, because their answer tells you whether the structure is working. Watch where their attention drifts, and treat those moments as faults in the speech rather than as rudeness. Practise handling interruptions and questions here, where a mistake costs nothing. Deliver the speech to its real audience once the rehearsal audience can repeat your message back to you. Review the recording afterward, note one thing to change, and carry that note into the next speech you build.

These three stages have to be worked through in order, because each one supplies what the next one needs. Building comes first, since there is nothing to rehearse until the message and the structure exist. Solo rehearsal comes second, as it fixes the problems that would otherwise waste your listeners time. Rehearsal in front of people comes last and tests the only thing you cannot test alone, which is whether the speech lands. Speakers who look gifted have simply been through this sequence often enough that the audience never sees the work.

### A15. Every Degree Should Require a Writing Course

> **Mode:** Argumentative (Practice 17.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Two supporting arguments plus one rebuttal paragraph

Universities require students in every faculty to satisfy a long list of conditions before graduating. Engineers take mathematics, nurses take anatomy, and business students take statistics, and nobody argues about any of it. Writing sits oddly outside that list at many institutions, where it is treated as a remedial subject for students who arrive underprepared. That arrangement misunderstands what writing is and who needs it. Graduates in every field spend their working lives producing documents that other people must act on. Every undergraduate degree should require a course in writing, because writing is the form most professional work takes, because writing is how thinking is tested, and because the objections to requiring it do not survive examination.

The first reason is that writing is the medium in which most graduates actually work. An engineer who cannot write a clear report will have the design misunderstood by the people who approve it. A nurse who records an observation ambiguously creates a risk for the next person on shift. Managers spend more of the working week producing written material than doing anything else, and that material is what their decisions rest on. Employers say so repeatedly when they are asked what recent graduates lack. A degree that certifies technical competence and ignores the skill through which that competence is communicated has certified only half the job. Requiring one course is a small correction to a large gap.

The second reason is that writing exposes thinking in a way no other assessment does. A multiple-choice examination can be passed by recognition, and a calculation can be completed correctly without any grasp of why the method works. Prose does not permit that. A student who cannot explain a relationship in a sentence usually cannot explain it at all, and the sentence is where the confusion becomes visible. Writing an argument forces a student to decide what follows from what, and then to defend that order to a reader. This is precisely the discipline that a university claims to teach in every discipline. A required writing course is therefore not an addition to a degree but a test of it.

Critics answer that writing should be taught within each discipline rather than as a separate course. That objection has real force, since a laboratory report and a legal memorandum follow different conventions. The argument nevertheless fails on two counts. Discipline-specific writing instruction has been recommended for decades and remains rare, because subject specialists are neither trained nor rewarded to provide it. More importantly, the conventions differ but the underlying skills do not, as every field requires a controlling idea, ordered support, and sentences that survive a careful reading. A required course teaches those transferable skills, and the discipline can then add its own conventions on top. The choice is not between a general course and a specialist one, but between a general course and nothing.

The case for requiring a writing course rests on what a degree is supposed to certify. Graduates work through documents, so a degree that ignores documents leaves its holders unprepared for the ordinary demands of employment. Prose reveals whether understanding is real, so a programme that never asks for prose has never fully tested its students. The main objection turns out to argue for better teaching rather than for no requirement at all. Institutions that leave writing optional are not protecting space in the timetable; they are declining to check whether their graduates can be understood.

### A16. Employers Should Not Monitor Personal Social Media

> **Mode:** Argumentative (Practice 17.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Two supporting arguments plus one rebuttal paragraph

Companies now routinely search the personal accounts of the people who work for them. Some do it before hiring, some do it continuously, and a growing number pay outside services to do it on their behalf. Managers defend the practice as ordinary caution, arguing that anything posted publicly is fair to read. That defence sounds reasonable until the arrangement is examined from the side of the employee. What is being proposed is that a condition of employment should extend to conduct outside working hours. Employers should not monitor the personal social media accounts of their staff, because the practice punishes lawful private conduct, because it produces unreliable evidence, and because the case made for it does not hold.

The first reason is that monitoring converts private life into a condition of employment. An employment contract buys a defined number of hours and a defined standard of work, and it has never bought the rest of a person week. Someone who attends a political rally, criticizes a public policy, or posts a photograph from a weekend has done nothing that concerns an employer. Monitoring nevertheless creates a permanent audience for those activities and a permanent risk attached to them. Employees who know they are watched begin to censor lawful behaviour, which is a real loss of freedom imposed without any wrongdoing. The imbalance of power makes the situation worse, since few people can afford to object. A practice that penalizes conduct nobody has agreed to submit for review cannot be justified as ordinary caution.

The second reason is that the evidence such monitoring produces is unreliable. A social media post is a fragment stripped of its context, its audience, and often its date. Sarcasm reads as sincerity, a shared article reads as an endorsement, and a photograph from years ago reads as current behaviour. Reviewers scanning hundreds of accounts have neither the time nor the information to interpret any of this correctly. Decisions taken on that basis therefore fall hardest on people whose humour, language, or community is unfamiliar to the reviewer. Employers who would never accept such thin material in a disciplinary hearing accept it readily when it arrives through a monitoring report. A method that generates confident conclusions from poor evidence damages the innocent more often than it catches anyone.

Supporters answer that companies must protect their reputation, and that an employee who publicly disgraces the organization causes real harm. That concern is legitimate, and nothing here defends genuine misconduct. The argument still fails, because the harm described is already covered by ordinary conduct policies that apply when a complaint is made. Those policies respond to specific incidents, allow the employee to explain, and produce a record that can be reviewed. Continuous monitoring replaces that fair process with an open-ended search for something to object to. Protecting a reputation does not require watching everyone all the time; it requires acting properly when a real problem is reported.

The argument against monitoring rests on the boundary between work and the rest of a life. Employers who cross that boundary punish lawful private conduct, rely on evidence too thin to interpret, and do so in pursuit of a goal that existing policies already serve. The practice survives mainly because it is cheap and because the people it harms are rarely in a position to complain. Companies serious about conduct should improve the processes they already have rather than watching their staff at home. An employment relationship is a contract for work, and it should not quietly become a contract for a life.

### A17. High Schools Should Start Later in the Morning

> **Mode:** Argumentative (Practice 17.2)
>
> **Person:** Third person plural
>
> **Paragraphs:** 5
>
> **structure:** Two supporting arguments plus one rebuttal paragraph

Most high schools open their doors before eight in the morning, and most of the students walking through those doors are exhausted. Teachers describe first period as a room of sleeping teenagers, and parents describe the half hour before the bus as the worst part of the day. The usual explanation is that adolescents stay up too late and lack discipline. That explanation ignores what happens to the body during adolescence and what an early start therefore costs. The timetable is one of the few things in this argument that anybody can actually change. High schools should begin the day later, because adolescent sleep patterns make early starts biologically unworkable, because the shortfall damages both learning and safety, and because the practical objections can be answered.

The first reason is that adolescence shifts the body clock later, and no amount of discipline reverses it. Teenagers do not become sleepy at the hour that suits their parents, because the timing of their sleep hormone moves back by roughly two hours during puberty. A student sent to bed at ten will often lie awake, not out of defiance but because the body is not ready. Waking that student at six therefore removes sleep from the only part of the night when it was available. The recommended amount for that age group is between eight and ten hours, and an early bell makes that quantity impossible for most households. The shortfall is not a failure of parenting but a scheduling conflict imposed by the school. Asking families to fix it privately asks them to solve a problem they did not create.

The second reason is that the resulting shortage damages both schoolwork and safety. Sleep is when the brain consolidates what was learned during the day, so a chronically short night undoes part of the teaching it follows. Students in that condition show poorer attention, weaker memory, and higher rates of anxiety and low mood. The risks extend beyond the classroom, because the oldest students in a high school drive themselves to it while badly rested. Districts that moved their start times report better attendance, better marks, and fewer collisions involving teenage drivers. Those results appear without any change to teachers, curriculum, or funding. Few reforms available to a board produce that much improvement for so little money.

Opponents reply that a later start disrupts bus schedules, after-school sports, and the working day of parents. Those difficulties are genuine and they are also solvable, as districts that made the change have demonstrated. Bus fleets can be rescheduled by swapping the elementary and high school runs, since younger children naturally wake earlier. Sports and activities move later in the afternoon, and the additional daylight in most seasons makes that straightforward. Parents who leave early already rely on arrangements for younger children and can extend them, while older students are old enough to leave the house on their own. Every one of these objections concerns adult convenience, and none of them concerns whether students learn.

The case for a later start is unusually clear because the biology is not in dispute. Adolescents fall asleep later for reasons outside their control, early bells remove the hours they need, and the cost shows up in marks, in mood, and in collisions. The obstacles are logistical, and districts that took them seriously have worked through them without much difficulty. A school system that knows when its students can learn and schedules the day against that knowledge is choosing convenience over education. Moving the bell is the cheapest improvement available to any board willing to make it.

### D01. Sentence Fragment

**1.** Which of the following is a sentence fragment?

- **A.** The train arrived on time.
- **B.** The train arrived on time, and the passengers boarded quickly.
- **C.** When the train arrived, the passengers boarded quickly.
- **D.** Although the train arrived on time.

> **Answer: D** - Although the train arrived on time.
>
> The subordinating conjunction 'although' makes the clause dependent, so it cannot stand alone.

**2.** Which of the following is a complete sentence?

- **A.** The library closed early.
- **B.** And the students went home.
- **C.** Because the library closed early.
- **D.** Running along the river at dawn.

> **Answer: A** - The library closed early.
>
> It has a subject and a conjugated verb and expresses a complete thought. The others are dependent or lack a conjugated verb.

**3.** Which of the following is a sentence fragment?

- **A.** Marta worked all summer, and George travelled abroad.
- **B.** Marta worked all summer.
- **C.** George travelled abroad.
- **D.** And George travelled abroad.

> **Answer: D** - And George travelled abroad.
>
> Beginning with the coordinating conjunction 'and' leaves the clause dependent on an independent clause that is missing.

**4.** Which sentence needs an independent clause added to it?

- **A.** Although George found his wallet.
- **B.** George found his wallet.
- **C.** George found his wallet, but the money was gone.
- **D.** The money was gone.

> **Answer: A** - Although George found his wallet.
>
> 'Although' makes this a dependent clause. Adding an independent clause repairs it: 'Although George found his wallet, the money was gone.'

**5.** Which of the following is NOT a complete sentence?

- **A.** Which the committee approved last week.
- **B.** The committee approved the budget.
- **C.** The committee, which met on Thursday, approved the budget.
- **D.** The committee meets on Thursdays.

> **Answer: A** - Which the committee approved last week.
>
> A relative clause beginning with 'which' cannot stand on its own; the main subject and verb are missing.

### D02. Fused Sentence

**6.** Which sentence is fused, that is, run together with no punctuation at all?

- **A.** The audience agreed the movie was terrible, so they told all their friends to avoid it.
- **B.** The audience agreed the movie was terrible. They told all their friends to avoid it.
- **C.** The audience agreed the movie was terrible they told all their friends to avoid it.
- **D.** The audience agreed the movie was terrible; they told all their friends to avoid it.

> **Answer: C** - The audience agreed the movie was terrible they told all their friends to avoid it.
>
> Two independent clauses are joined with nothing between them.

**7.** Which sentence is correctly punctuated?

- **A.** Passengers on the plane were upset they had to sit, on the tarmac for three hours.
- **B.** Passengers on the plane were upset they had to sit on the tarmac for three hours.
- **C.** Passengers on the plane were upset since they had to sit on the tarmac for three hours.
- **D.** Passengers on the plane were upset, they had to sit on the tarmac for three hours.

> **Answer: C** - Passengers on the plane were upset since they had to sit on the tarmac for three hours.
>
> The connecting word 'since' subordinates the second clause, so no comma is needed and no run-on remains.

**8.** Which sentence is a fused sentence?

- **A.** Sanjay never finished paneling his basement, so he hired a carpenter.
- **B.** Sanjay never finished paneling his basement he hired a carpenter.
- **C.** Sanjay never finished paneling his basement. He hired a carpenter.
- **D.** Because Sanjay never finished paneling his basement, he hired a carpenter.

> **Answer: B** - Sanjay never finished paneling his basement he hired a carpenter.
>
> Two complete sentences are jammed together with no punctuation and no joining word.

**9.** In which sentence are two independent clauses joined with no punctuation?

- **A.** I love books, and they inspire me.
- **B.** I love books they inspire me.

- **C.** The 1960's saw an explosion in folk music.
- **D.** The 1960s saw an explosion in folk music.

> **Answer: D** - The 1960s saw an explosion in folk music.
>
> A decade is a plural, not a possessive, so it takes no apostrophe.

### D15. Parallelism

**71.** Which sentence is parallel?

- **A.** That teacher is passionate, she is dedicated, and inspiring.
- **B.** That teacher is passionate, dedicated, and inspires her students.
- **C.** That teacher is passionate, dedicated, and inspiring.
- **D.** That teacher is passionate, dedication, and inspiring.

> **Answer: C** - That teacher is passionate, dedicated, and inspiring.
>
> All three items are adjectives. Mixing an adjective list with a verb breaks the pattern.

**72.** Which sentence is NOT parallel?

- **A.** I love to write, to read, and to knit.
- **B.** I love to write, to read, and knitting.
- **C.** I love writing, reading, and knitting.
- **D.** I love to write, read, and knit.

> **Answer: B** - I love to write, to read, and knitting.
>
> Two infinitives followed by a gerund breaks the verb form.

**73.** Which sentence is parallel?

- **A.** She likes cooking, jogging, and reading.
- **B.** She likes to cook, jogging, and reading.
- **C.** She likes cooking, to jog, and reading.
- **D.** She likes cooking, jogging, and to read.

> **Answer: A** - She likes cooking, jogging, and reading.
>
> All three items are gerunds.

**74.** Which comparison is parallel?

- **A.** He prefers the green socks to brown ones that are socks.
- **B.** He prefers green socks to the socks which are brown.
- **C.** He prefers the green socks to the socks that are brown.
- **D.** He prefers the green socks to the brown socks.

> **Answer: D** - He prefers the green socks to the brown socks.
>
> Both halves of the comparison take the same form: adjective plus noun.

**75.** Which sentence contains a parallelism error?

- **A.** She reviewed her notes, sipped her coffee, and hoped she would pass.
- **B.** While waiting for the exam, she reviewed her notes, sipped her coffee, and hoped she would pass.
- **C.** While waiting for the exam, she reviewed her notes, sipped her coffee, and was hoping she would pass.
- **D.** While waiting for the exam, she reviewed, sipped, and hoped.

> **Answer: C** - While waiting for the exam, she reviewed her notes, sipped her coffee, and was hoping she would pass.
>
> Two simple past verbs followed by a past progressive breaks the pattern.

### D16. Mixed Construction

**76.** Which sentence contains a mixed construction?

- **A.** The most valued trait in a nurse is compassion.
- **B.** The most valued trait in a nurse is a person who is compassionate.
- **C.** Nurses are valued for their compassion.
- **D.** Compassion is the most valued trait in a nurse.

> **Answer: B** - The most valued trait in a nurse is a person who is compassionate.
>
> A trait cannot be a person. The two halves of the sentence do not match logically.

**77.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** Arachnophobia is an extreme fear of spiders.
- **B.** Arachnophobia is where someone fears spiders.
- **C.** Arachnophobia is because people fear spiders.
- **D.** Arachnophobia is when you are extremely afraid of spiders.

> **Answer: A** - Arachnophobia is an extreme fear of spiders.
>
> A definition needs a noun after 'is', not a clause beginning with 'when', 'where', or 'because'.

**78.** Which sentence contains a mixed construction?

- **A.** The statistics show that more research is needed.
- **B.** Researchers conclude that more research is needed.
- **C.** An analysis of the statistics reveals that more research is needed.
- **D.** These statistics propose that more research is needed.

> **Answer: D** - These statistics propose that more research is needed.
>
> Statistics cannot propose anything; only a person can.

**79.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** A practical nurse becoming an RN it requires bridging.
- **B.** For a practical nurse to become an RN it requires the bridging program.
- **C.** A practical nurse who wants to become an RN should take the bridging program.
- **D.** For a practical nurse to become an RN is requiring the bridging program.

> **Answer: C** - A practical nurse who wants to become an RN should take the bridging program.
>
> The original begins with a prepositional phrase and then adds a second subject, 'it', leaving the grammar unresolved.

**80.** Which sentence contains a mixed construction?

- **A.** I like horror movies because they frighten me.
- **B.** The reason I like horror movies is because they frighten me.
- **C.** Horror movies appeal to me because they are frightening.
- **D.** The reason I like horror movies is that they frighten me.

> **Answer: B** - The reason I like horror movies is because they frighten me.
>
> 'The reason is because' states the same idea twice; use 'the reason is that' or drop the opening entirely.

### D17. Overuse of Passive Voice

**81.** Which sentence is in the active voice?

- **A.** The girl kicked the ball.
- **B.** The ball had been kicked.
- **C.** The ball was being kicked by the girl.
- **D.** The ball was kicked by the girl.

> **Answer: A** - The girl kicked the ball.
>
> Active voice runs subject, verb, object: the doer comes first.

**82.** Which sentence is the clearest and most concise?

- **A.** The CEO implemented the new regulations.
- **B.** The implementation of the new regulations was carried out by the CEO.
- **C.** The new regulations were implemented.
- **D.** The new regulations were implemented by the CEO.

> **Answer: A** - The CEO implemented the new regulations.
>
> Active voice names the doer and removes the extra words that the passive requires.

**83.** Which revision removes the unnecessary passive?

- **A.** Beautifully, the song was sung by her.
- **B.** The song was sung beautifully by her.
- **C.** She sang the song beautifully.
- **D.** The song had been sung beautifully by her.

> **Answer: C** - She sang the song beautifully.
>
> There is no reason to hide the singer, so the active voice is shorter and clearer.

**84.** In which sentence is the passive voice a good choice?

- **A.** The jewels were stolen.
- **B.** The ball was kicked by the girl.
- **C.** The report was written by me.
- **D.** A standing ovation was given to the speaker by the audience.

> **Answer: A** - The jewels were stolen.
>
> The thief is unknown and the action matters more than the doer, which is one of the three cases where the passive is appropriate.

**85.** Which sentence is active?

- **A.** The audience gave the speaker a standing ovation.
- **B.** A standing ovation was received by the speaker.
- **C.** The speaker was given a standing ovation.
- **D.** At the end of the presentation, a standing ovation was given to the speaker.

> **Answer: A** - The audience gave the speaker a standing ovation.
>
> The audience is the doer and comes first, so no form of 'to be' plus 'by' is needed.

### D18. Overuse of the Verb To Be

**86.** Which sentence is more concise?

- **A.** It is the case that carbon dioxide is trapping heat.
- **B.** Carbon dioxide is a gas that is trapping heat.
- **C.** It is carbon dioxide that is trapping heat in the atmosphere.
- **D.** Carbon dioxide traps heat in the atmosphere.

> **Answer: D** - Carbon dioxide traps heat in the atmosphere.
>
> Removing 'it is' and 'that is' promotes the real verb, 'traps', to centre stage.

**87.** Which sentence is more concise?

- **A.** There are many environmental problems requiring action that is immediate.
- **B.** There are many environmental problems that are requiring immediate action.
- **C.** Many environmental problems require immediate action.
- **D.** It is many environmental problems that are requiring action.

> **Answer: C** - Many environmental problems require immediate action.
>
> 'There are' and 'that are' add words without adding meaning, and they bury the main verb.

**88.** Which sentence overuses the verb 'to be'?

- **A.** Everyone loves the sweet cake.
- **B.** The cake is sweet and is loved by everyone.
- **C.** The sweet cake pleases everyone.
- **D.** Everyone enjoys the cake.

> **Answer: B** - The cake is sweet and is loved by everyone.
>
> 'Is' appears twice, and the second instance also produces an unnecessary passive.

**89.** Which sentence is strongest?

- **A.** Choosing words carefully matters in an essay.
- **B.** There is importance in the words that are used.
- **C.** It is the words that are used that are important.
- **D.** It is important to be mindful of the words that are used in an essay.

> **Answer: A** - Choosing words carefully matters in an essay.
>
> One precise verb replaces three weak forms of 'to be'.

**90.** Which sentence buries its main verb?

- **A.** The committee approved the proposal quickly.
- **B.** The proposal won the committee's approval.
- **C.** The committee approved the proposal.
- **D.** It was the committee that was responsible for the approval of the proposal.

> **Answer: D** - It was the committee that was responsible for the approval of the proposal.
>
> The real action, approving, has been turned into a noun and pushed to the end behind two forms of 'to be'.

### D19. Misuse of Which/That and Who/Whom and Where

**91.** Which sentence correctly punctuates a restrictive clause?

- **A.** The Canadian law, which legalizes cannabis was passed in 2018.
- **B.** The Canadian law, that legalizes cannabis, was passed in 2018.
- **C.** The Canadian law that legalizes cannabis was passed in 2018.
- **D.** The Canadian law who legalizes cannabis was passed in 2018.

> **Answer: C** - The Canadian law that legalizes cannabis was passed in 2018.
>
> The clause identifies which law is meant, so it is restrictive: it uses 'that' and takes no commas.

**92.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** The man that is wearing a red hat is my uncle.
- **B.** The man who is wearing a red hat is my uncle.
- **C.** The man whom is wearing a red hat is my uncle.
- **D.** The man which is wearing a red hat is my uncle.

> **Answer: B** - The man who is wearing a red hat is my uncle.
>
> Use 'who' for people, and use the subject form because the pronoun performs the verb.

**93.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** The 1980s were a decade when big hair ruled.
- **B.** The 1980s were a decade which big hair ruled.
- **C.** The 1980s were a decade that big hair ruled in where.
- **D.** The 1980s were a decade where big hair ruled.

> **Answer: A** - The 1980s were a decade when big hair ruled.
>
> 'Where' refers to places. A period of time takes 'when'.

**94.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** Did you read which the prime minister is speaking in Iqaluit?
- **B.** Did you read where is the prime minister speaking in Iqaluit?
- **C.** Did you read where the prime minister is speaking in Iqaluit?
- **D.** Did you read that the prime minister is speaking in Iqaluit?

> **Answer: D** - Did you read that the prime minister is speaking in Iqaluit?
>
> 'Where' has crossed over from informal speech; the correct word for reporting a fact is 'that'.

**95.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** Who did Kal buy a ticket for whom?
- **B.** For who did Kal buy a ticket?
- **C.** For whom did Kal buy a ticket?
- **D.** For whoever did Kal buy a ticket?

> **Answer: C** - For whom did Kal buy a ticket?
>
> The pronoun is the object of the preposition 'for', so the object form 'whom' is required.

### D20. Problems in Attributive Tags

**96.** Which sentence punctuates the attributive tag correctly?

- **A.** Jenkins states that, carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **B.** Jenkins states that carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **C.** Jenkins states, that carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **D.** Jenkins, states that carbon dioxide traps heat.

> **Answer: B** - Jenkins states that carbon dioxide traps heat.
>
> An attributive tag containing 'that' takes no comma at all.

**97.** Which sentence is correct?

- **A.** Jenkins states that 'carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **B.** Jenkins states that; 'carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **C.** Jenkins, states that 'carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **D.** Jenkins states that, 'carbon dioxide traps heat.'

> **Answer: A** - Jenkins states that 'carbon dioxide traps heat.'
>
> The rule holds whether the material is quoted or paraphrased: 'that' takes no comma.

**98.** Which sentence correctly treats the quotation as dialogue?

- **A.** Jenkins states 'Carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **B.** Jenkins states: that 'Carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **C.** Jenkins states that, 'Carbon dioxide traps heat.'
- **D.** Jenkins states, 'Carbon dioxide traps heat.'

> **Answer: D** - Jenkins states, 'Carbon dioxide traps heat.'
>
> When a quotation is handled as dialogue, include the comma and drop 'that'.

**99.** Which verb choice is incorrect?

- **A.** Jenkins states that carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **B.** Jenkins explains how carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **C.** Jenkins states how carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **D.** Jenkins explains that carbon dioxide traps heat.

> **Answer: C** - Jenkins states how carbon dioxide traps heat.
>
> Some verbs, including 'states' and 'claims', can be followed by 'that' but not by 'how'.

**100.** A writer wants to signal that the author is presenting a fact rather than describing a process. Which sentence does that?

- **A.** Jenkins explains how carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **B.** Jenkins explains that carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **C.** Jenkins explains where carbon dioxide traps heat.
- **D.** Jenkins explains when carbon dioxide traps heat.

> **Answer: B** - Jenkins explains that carbon dioxide traps heat.
>
> 'That' promises a statement; 'how' promises the steps of a process.
