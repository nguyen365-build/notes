**Do not invent a name.**

### 2.2 The granularity question: does one "substitution" cover a substitution inside a rearrangement?

This is the only genuinely ambiguous thing about the naming instruction, so it is worth settling now rather than in the exam room.

Part (d) is the sharp case.
The printed work is: rewrite $\sec^3 x\tan x$ as $\sec^2 x\,(\sec x\tan x)$, then substitute $u=\sec x$.
That is two moves, and there are three defensible ways to write it.

| granularity | what you would write for (d) | the risk |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **coarse** | "substitution" | if the marker wants the rewrite acknowledged, it understates the work by half |
| **per step** | "rewrite using the hint, then substitution with $u=\sec x$, $du=\sec x\tan x\,dx$" | none that I can see |
| **exhaustive** | as above, plus "power rule on $\int u^2\,du$" | none, other than time |

**Write the middle one.**
The reason is a cost asymmetry, not a piece of knowledge, and it is the same argument Q8.4 makes for derivatives.
Naming a step too finely costs a few seconds of writing.
Naming it too coarsely costs marks if, and only if, the marker was counting steps.
You cannot know which marker you have, and the extra precision is nearly free, so the fine-grained list dominates.

**The one piece of the wording that is not inference.**
The instruction is *"Indicate the technique you are using"* - singular "technique", unlike item 2's *"State which rule(s)"* with its printed plural.
That singular is worth noticing but should not be over-read.
It most plausibly means "for each integral, say which technique", not "you are forbidden to mention two steps".
Naming the rewrite *and* the substitution on part (d) answers the singular reading as well, because the technique is the substitution and the rewrite is how you got to it.

**The rule that resolves every case:**

> A **substitution** is one technique no matter how much algebra surrounds it.
> Name the substitution, and name the $u$.
> Then say in one clause what you had to do to the integrand *before* the substitution was visible.

On part (a) that clause is "after writing $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \sqrt2\,du$".
On part (d) it is "after the hint's rewrite".
On part (b) there is no clause, because $u$ and $du$ are already sitting there.
On part (c) there is no substitution to name at all.

### 2.3 The technique list describes YOUR work, not the integrand

This is Q16.2's signature idea, inherited in form from Q8.4 and sharper here.

> **Two students can hand in the same correct antiderivative and owe two different technique lists, because they did two different things.**
> The name is a description of a process, not a property of the integrand.

That claim is easy to state and easy to doubt, so it was verified.
Four pairs of routes were checked, each pair over seven sample points, comparing the two antiderivatives as functions.

| integrand | route 1, and its name list | route 2, and its name list | measured relationship |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sec^3 x\tan x$ **(part d)** | hint rewrite, then substitution $u=\sec x$: $\frac{\sec^3x}{3}$ | rewrite as $\frac{\sin x}{\cos^4 x}$, then substitution $u=\cos x$: $\frac{1}{3\cos^3 x}$ | **identical functions**, max difference $4.441\times10^{-16}$ |
| $\dfrac{\cos\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt x}$ **(part a)** | substitution $u=\sqrt{2x}$ | substitution $u=\sqrt{x}$ | **identical functions**, max difference $6.661\times10^{-16}$ |
| $\tan x\sec^2 x$ **(part b)** | substitution $u=\tan x$: $\frac{\tan^2x}{2}$ | substitution $u=\sec x$: $\frac{\sec^2x}{2}$ | differ by a **constant**, spread over 7 points $3.109\times10^{-15}$; the constant is exactly $\frac12$ |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{5x}}{x^2}$ **(inside part c)** | divide through, power rule | split off the term, then substitution $u=5x$ | **identical functions**, max difference $0$ |

**Row 4 is the one to study, and it is this question's version of Q8.4's Family 3.**
Part (c) needs no technique.
But a student who splits the fraction and then substitutes $u=5x$ on the middle term gets *exactly the same answer* and owes a name list that includes "substitution".
Neither list is wrong.
The one that matches the work is the one that is right, and the one that does not match is a naming error even though the calculus is flawless.

**Row 1 is the cleanest demonstration in the document**, because the two routes do not merely differ by a constant - they produce the *same function*, character for character once simplified, from two genuinely different technique lists.
So "same answer" carries no information at all about "same technique".

**Row 3 shows the opposite failure mode.**
Two correct substitutions on part (b) give antiderivatives that differ by $\frac12$, and both are correct because $\sec^2 x - \tan^2 x = 1$.
If you check your work against a friend's and see $\frac{\tan^2 x}{2}$ against $\frac{\sec^2 x}{2}$, neither of you has made a mistake.
On the definite integral they agree exactly, both giving $\frac32$, which is verified.

**The practical instruction that falls out:**

> Write the name **while you are doing the step**, not when you look back at the finished answer.
> Looking back at the answer cannot tell you which route produced it, and this section is the proof.

### 2.4 The four facts you actually need to have memorised

Everything in Q16.2 is one of these, and there is nothing else.

| fact | form |
| :--- | :--- |
| the power rule, backwards | $\displaystyle\int x^n\,dx = \frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$, for $n\ne-1$ |
| $\cos$ integrates to $\sin$ | $\displaystyle\int \cos u\,du = \sin u + C$ |
| the substitution rule | $\displaystyle\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int f(u)\,du$, with $u=g(x)$ |
| the FTC, in the form you use | $\displaystyle\int_a^b f = F(b)-F(a)$, upper minus lower |

Plus three rewrites that are algebra, not technique, and which the exam relies on:

- $\sqrt{5x} = \sqrt5\,x^{1/2}$, so $\dfrac{\sqrt{5x}}{x^2} = \sqrt5\,x^{-3/2}$.
  The most-dropped step on part (c) is the exponent arithmetic $\frac12-2=-\frac32$.
- $\dfrac{a+b+c}{d} = \dfrac ad + \dfrac bd + \dfrac cd$.
  This is the whole of part (c).
- $\sec x = \dfrac{1}{\cos x}$, and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x$.
  This is what makes part (d)'s hint usable.

## 3. The universal procedure, with names attached

> **Step 0 - Read the whole instruction and underline the naming demand.**
> On this item it is the second of two sentences, and it is the easiest thing on the page to skip.
>
> **Step 1 - Classify the answer type.**
> Limits on the integral sign means the answer is a number and takes no $+C$.
> No limits means the answer is a function and must carry $+C$.
> Part (b) is the only definite one here.
>
> **Step 2 - Try algebra, and if it works, say that it worked.**
> Divide through, expand, rewrite roots as fractional powers.
> If the result is a sum of powers of $x$, apply the power rule term by term.
> **Write down "no technique required - algebraic simplification, then the power rule".**
> Do not leave the technique line blank because there was no technique; a blank line is indistinguishable from having skipped the instruction.
>
> **Step 3 - Look for $u$ and $du$.**
> Pick a candidate inner function $u$, compute $du/dx$, and ask whether $du/dx$ is present in the rest of the integrand up to a constant factor.
> If yes, substitute, and **write "substitution, $u=\ldots$, $du=\ldots$" as you decide it**, not afterwards.
> If no, try another $u$.
>
> **Step 4 - If nothing fits, rewrite first, then re-enter at step 2.**
> A trig product that no $u$ fits needs an identity.
> On this question you never have to find that rewrite yourself, because part (d) prints it.
>
> **Step 5 - Finish.**
> Indefinite: $+C$, and state the domain if the answer contains a root or a reciprocal.
> Definite: $F(b)-F(a)$, upper limit first.
>
> **Step 6 - Differentiate back, then audit the names separately.**
> The two audits are different jobs and section 6 explains why one of them has no instrument.

### The classification table, extended with the name

Q16.1's classification table said which rung.
This one says which rung *and* what to write.

| what you see after multiplying out | rung | tool | **what to write on the technique line** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a sum of powers of $x$, fractional or negative included | 1 | power rule, term by term | "no technique - algebraic simplification, then the power rule" |
| a fraction with a single term downstairs | 1 | divide through first | "split the fraction, then the power rule" |
| a bracket to a small power | 1 | expand | "expand, then the power rule" |
| $f(ax+b)$ for a known $f$ | 2 | the $1/a$ factor | "substitution, $u=ax+b$" (the $1/a$ rule is a substitution) |

## 4. Q16.2 worked, part by part

### Part (a) - $\displaystyle\int \frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$

**Step 2, algebra.** There is nothing to divide or expand. Algebra does not kill this one.

**Step 3, look for $u$ and $du$.**
The composition is $\cos$ of $\sqrt{2x}$, so try $u=\sqrt{2x}$.

$$u = \sqrt{2x} = (2x)^{1/2}, \qquad \frac{du}{dx} = \tfrac12(2x)^{-1/2}\cdot 2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2x}}, \qquad du = \frac{dx}{\sqrt{2x}}.$$

Now the question that decides everything: **is $\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2x}}$ present in the integrand?**
The integrand carries $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}$, which is not quite it, but the two differ by a constant:

$$\frac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \frac{\sqrt2\,dx}{\sqrt2\,\sqrt x} = \frac{\sqrt2\,dx}{\sqrt{2x}} = \sqrt2\,du.$$

That single line is the whole of part (a).
Substituting,

$$\int \frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)}{\sqrt x}\,dx = \int \cos u \cdot \sqrt2\,du = \sqrt2\int\cos u\,du = \sqrt2\,\sin u + C.$$

$$\boxed{\;\int \frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx = \sqrt2\,\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right) + C, \qquad x>0.\;}$$

**The technique to write:** *substitution*, with $u=\sqrt{2x}$ and $\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \sqrt2\,du$.

**The domain matters and should be stated.**
$\sqrt x$ in the denominator needs $x>0$, not $x\ge0$.
The antiderivative $\sqrt2\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)$ is perfectly well defined at $x=0$, which is exactly why the restriction is easy to forget: nothing in the answer looks wrong.

**Check.** Differentiating back,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt2\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)\right] = \sqrt2\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2x}} = \frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt2\sqrt x}\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right) = \frac{\cos\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)}{\sqrt x}.\;\checkmark$$

Verified numerically at twelve points from $x=0.05$ to $x=12.5$, and independently by quadrature: $F(2)-F(0.5) = 0.095921074189$, matching tanh-sinh quadrature of the integrand to $1.1\times10^{-16}$.

**The alternative route, and the name it does not change.**
Take $u=\sqrt x$ instead, so $du = \frac{dx}{2\sqrt x}$ and $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}=2\,du$.
The integrand becomes $2\cos\left(\sqrt2\,u\right)du$, giving $\frac{2}{\sqrt2}\sin\left(\sqrt2\sqrt x\right) = \sqrt2\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)$.
**The same function**, verified to $6.661\times10^{-16}$ over seven points.
The name is still "substitution", but the $u$ you write is different, and the $u$ you write should be the one you used.

### Part (b) - $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3} \tan x\,\sec^2 x\,dx$

**This is the easiest part on the paper and the one with the most ways to lose a mark**, because it is the only definite one.

**Step 3.** Try $u=\tan x$. Then $du=\sec^2 x\,dx$, and $\sec^2 x\,dx$ is sitting right there.

$$\int \tan x\,\sec^2 x\,dx = \int u\,du = \frac{u^2}{2} + C = \frac{\tan^2 x}{2} + C.$$

**Step 5, the limits.** There are two correct ways to finish, and it is worth knowing both.

*Back-substitute and use the original limits:*
$$\int_0^{\pi/3}\tan x\sec^2x\,dx = \left[\frac{\tan^2x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/3} = \frac{\left(\sqrt3\right)^2}{2} - \frac{0^2}{2} = \frac32.$$

*Or change the limits with the variable, and never go back:*
when $x=0$, $u=\tan0=0$; when $x=\pi/3$, $u=\tan\frac\pi3=\sqrt3$.
$$\int_0^{\sqrt3} u\,du = \left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_0^{\sqrt3} = \frac32.$$

$$\boxed{\;\int_0^{\pi/3}\tan x\,\sec^2 x\,dx = \frac32 = 1.5\;}$$

**The technique to write:** *substitution*, $u=\tan x$, $du=\sec^2x\,dx$; limits change to $u$ from $0$ to $\sqrt3$.

Note there is **no $+C$** here.
A definite integral is a number.

**Check.** Independent tanh-sinh quadrature of $\tan x\sec^2 x$ over $[0,\pi/3]$ gives $1.500000000000$, agreeing with the closed form to $2.2\times10^{-16}$.
Simpson's rule on the same interval was measured converging at orders $3.822, 3.949, 3.987, 3.997$, so the digit lock inherits a genuinely fourth-order method and the twelve digits are real.

**The other substitution, which is equally correct.**
Take $u=\sec x$ instead.
Write $\tan x\sec^2 x\,dx = \sec x\cdot(\sec x\tan x\,dx) = u\,du$, giving $\frac{\sec^2x}{2}+C$.
That is a *different antiderivative*: it exceeds $\frac{\tan^2 x}{2}$ by exactly $\frac12$, measured constant across seven points to $3.109\times10^{-15}$.
Both give $\frac32$ on this interval, verified.
Both name "substitution", and each should name its own $u$.

### Part (c) - $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^3 + \sqrt{5x} - 4}{x^2}\,dx$

**This is the part the naming instruction exists for.**
There is no technique here at all, and saying so is the correct answer to the second sentence of the question.

**Step 2, algebra, and it kills the problem outright.**
Split the fraction over its single-term denominator and rewrite the root as a power.

$$\frac{x^3 + \sqrt{5x} - 4}{x^2} = \frac{x^3}{x^2} + \frac{\sqrt5\,x^{1/2}}{x^2} - \frac{4}{x^2} = x + \sqrt5\,x^{-3/2} - 4x^{-2}.$$

**The exponent step is where marks go.**
$\frac{x^{1/2}}{x^2} = x^{1/2-2} = x^{-3/2}$, and the arithmetic $\frac12-2=-\frac32$ is done wrong more often than anything else on this question.

Now the power rule three times:

$$\int x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}, \qquad \int \sqrt5\,x^{-3/2}\,dx = \sqrt5\cdot\frac{x^{-1/2}}{-\frac12} = -\frac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt x}, \qquad \int -4x^{-2}\,dx = -4\cdot\frac{x^{-1}}{-1} = \frac4x.$$

$$\boxed{\;\int \frac{x^3+\sqrt{5x}-4}{x^2}\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt x} + \frac4x + C, \qquad x>0.\;}$$

**The technique to write:** *none*.
Say it explicitly: "no integration technique needed - algebraic simplification (split the fraction, rewrite $\sqrt{5x}=\sqrt5\,x^{1/2}$), then the power rule term by term".

**Both signs flip, and that is the trap.**
The middle term's $-\frac12$ in the denominator makes the coefficient negative, and the last term's $-1$ makes an originally negative term positive.
Two sign flips in the same line, in opposite directions.
Both were seeded into the census and both were caught by differentiating back, at relative gaps of $3.690$ and $6.959$ - not marginal errors.

**The domain.**
$\sqrt{5x}$ needs $x\ge0$ and the $x^2$ downstairs needs $x\ne0$, so the integrand lives on $x>0$ and so does the answer.

**Check.** $F(4)-F(1) = 6.736067977500$, matching independent quadrature to $1.8\times10^{-15}$.
Differentiating back was verified at twelve points from $x=0.05$ to $x=11$.

**The route that adds a technique to the list.**
Split the three terms first and integrate the middle one by substituting $u=5x$, so $x=u/5$ and $dx=du/5$:

$$\int\frac{\sqrt{5x}}{x^2}\,dx = \int \frac{\sqrt u}{u^2/25}\cdot\frac{du}{5} = 5\int u^{-3/2}\,du = -\frac{10}{\sqrt u} = -\frac{10}{\sqrt{5x}} = -\frac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt x}.$$

Measured identical to the direct route, difference exactly $0$ across seven points.
**That student has done nothing wrong and owes a longer name list**: "split the fraction, then substitution with $u=5x$ on the middle term, then the power rule".
Writing "no technique" after doing that substitution would be a naming error.

### Part (d) - $\displaystyle\int \sec^3 x\,\tan x\,dx$

**The exam prints the rewrite, so take it.**
The hint says $\sec^3 x\tan x = \sec^2 x\,\sec x\tan x$, and the reason that helps is that $\sec x\tan x$ is exactly $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x$.

**Step 3.** Let $u=\sec x$, so $du = \sec x\tan x\,dx$.

$$\int\sec^3x\tan x\,dx = \int \underbrace{\sec^2 x}_{u^2}\underbrace{\left(\sec x\tan x\,dx\right)}_{du} = \int u^2\,du = \frac{u^3}{3}+C.$$

$$\boxed{\;\int \sec^3 x\,\tan x\,dx = \frac{\sec^3 x}{3} + C, \qquad \cos x\ne0.\;}$$

**The technique to write:** *substitution*, $u=\sec x$, $du=\sec x\tan x\,dx$, after the rewrite the hint supplies.

**Check.** Differentiating back, $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\sec^3x}{3} = \frac{3\sec^2x\cdot\sec x\tan x}{3} = \sec^3x\tan x$.
Verified at twelve points across $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, and $F(\pi/4)-F(0) = 0.609475708249$ against independent quadrature to $8.9\times10^{-16}$.
That value is exactly $\frac{2\sqrt2-1}{3}$.

**The route that changes the name list.**
Write everything in sines and cosines: $\sec^3x\tan x = \frac{1}{\cos^3x}\cdot\frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^4 x}$.
Now substitute $u=\cos x$, $du=-\sin x\,dx$:

$$\int\frac{\sin x}{\cos^4x}\,dx = -\int u^{-4}\,du = \frac{u^{-3}}{3} = \frac{1}{3\cos^3 x}.$$

**That is the identical function** to $\frac{\sec^3x}{3}$, measured to $4.441\times10^{-16}$ over seven points - not merely a constant apart, the same function.
And the name list is different: "rewrite with $\sec = 1/\cos$ and $\tan=\sin/\cos$, then substitution with $u=\cos x$".
This is the cleanest demonstration in the document that the name describes the work.

### Route comparison

| part | route to use under pressure | why the alternative loses |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | $u=\sqrt{2x}$ | $u=\sqrt x$ works and lands identically, but leaves a $\sqrt2$ inside the sine that you then have to recognise |
| b | $u=\tan x$ | $u=\sec x$ is equally fast; use whichever you see first, and name the one you used |
| c | divide through, power rule | substituting $u=5x$ on the middle term is correct and costs a step and a name |
| d | the hint's rewrite, $u=\sec x$ | the $\sin/\cos^4$ route is correct but needs a sign you can drop and an $u^{-4}$ you can mis-power |

**Under pressure, the whole question is: algebra on (c), and one substitution each on (a), (b), (d).**
Three of the four parts carry the same technique name.
That symmetry is the point of the item, and it is why part (c) is the one that discriminates.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up, and what counts as naming a technique

### The policy

Q16.1's policy stands: get the antiderivative, add $+C$, do not gold-plate the algebra.
Q16.2 adds a second policy beside it, and it points the other way.

> **Simplify as little as you can. Name as much as you can.**
>
> Algebra past a correct antiderivative is unpaid risk.
> Naming past the minimum is paid, or at worst free, because the question asked for it in writing.

That asymmetry is the whole of this section.

### What counts as naming a technique

| this counts | this is weaker | this does not count |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "By **substitution**, with $u=\sqrt{2x}$, so $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}=\sqrt2\,du$" | "substitution" written in the margin | the correct antiderivative with no words at all |
| "**No technique** - split the fraction, then the power rule term by term" | "algebra" | leaving the technique line blank because there was no technique |
| "**Substitution**, $u=\sec x$, after the hint's rewrite" | "$u$-sub" | naming a technique you did not use |
| "**Substitution**, $u=\tan x$; limits become $0$ to $\sqrt3$" | "sub, changed limits" | naming *integration by parts*, which this course never teaches |

The middle column is not wrong and is what to fall back on when time is short.
The right column has two entries worth dwelling on.

**"Leaving the line blank because there was no technique" is the specific trap of part (c).**
A blank technique line and a skipped instruction look identical on the page.
Writing "none required" takes four seconds and removes the ambiguity.

**"Naming a technique you did not use" is worse than naming nothing**, because it is a positive claim about your method that a marker can see is false.
The census in section 6 confirms that no check you can run will catch it, so it has to be prevented rather than detected.

### The short form, which is what to write if time is tight

> **a.** By **substitution**, $u=\sqrt{2x}$, $du=\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2x}}$, so $\frac{dx}{\sqrt x}=\sqrt2\,du$:
> $$\int\frac{\cos\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt x}\,dx = \sqrt2\int\cos u\,du = \sqrt2\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)+C, \quad x>0.$$
>
> **b.** By **substitution**, $u=\tan x$, $du=\sec^2x\,dx$; limits become $u=0$ to $u=\sqrt3$:
> $$\int_0^{\pi/3}\tan x\sec^2x\,dx = \int_0^{\sqrt3}u\,du = \left[\tfrac{u^2}{2}\right]_0^{\sqrt3} = \frac32.$$
>
> **c.** **No technique required** - split the fraction and use the power rule, since $\frac{x^3+\sqrt{5x}-4}{x^2} = x+\sqrt5\,x^{-3/2}-4x^{-2}$:
> $$\int = \frac{x^2}{2}-\frac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt x}+\frac4x+C, \quad x>0.$$
>
> **d.** By the hint's rewrite and then **substitution**, $u=\sec x$, $du=\sec x\tan x\,dx$:
> $$\int\sec^3x\tan x\,dx = \int u^2\,du = \frac{\sec^3x}{3}+C, \quad \cos x\ne0.$$

That is about two minutes of writing and it answers both sentences of the question on all four parts.

### The two-way naming audit

Before you move on, read the answer and the technique line against each other in both directions.
It takes ten seconds and it is the only instrument that exists for this failure mode.

| direction | the question to ask | what a failure looks like |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **name to work** | for the technique I named, where in my working is it visible? | you wrote "substitution" on part (c) and there is no $u$ anywhere on the page |
| **work to name** | for every step my working took, is it named? | your part (d) working starts with a rewrite and your technique line says only "substitution" |

The second direction catches omissions, which is the more common failure.
The first direction catches the invented name, which is the more expensive one.

## 6. Verify before you commit - and know that the name has no check

### The one instrument you have

Differentiate your answer back and compare it to the integrand.
It is fast, it needs no new technique, and on this question it is decisive - for the mathematics.

### What it catches, with measured evidence

Twelve wrong answers that change the function were seeded across the four parts and **all twelve were caught**.

| error family | seeded | detected | example, and its measured relative gap |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| dropped constant factor | 4 | **4** | $\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)$ without the $\sqrt2$ on (a): $2.929\times10^{-1}$ |
| missed chain factor | 3 | **3** | $\sqrt2\sin(2x)$ on (a), stopping after $u=2x$: $3.270$ |
| power-rule slip | 3 | **3** | reading $\frac{\sqrt{5x}}{x^2}$ as $\sqrt5\,x^{-1/2}$ on (c): $4.967\times10^{-1}$ |
| algebra slip | 2 | **2** | sign flip on (b)'s $\frac{\sec^2x}{2}$: $2.000$ |
| **total** | **12** | **12** | |

**The smallest relative gap anywhere in the detected set is $2.929\times10^{-1}$.**
Nothing is marginal.
A dropped constant factor or a missed chain factor is a thirty-percent-of-scale disagreement, and symbolically it is a whole missing factor - you do not need a calculator to see it.

### What it cannot catch

Fourteen more errors were seeded, and **none of them was detected**.

| error family | seeded | detected | why it hides |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| omitted or altered $+C$ | 3 | **0** | a constant differentiates to zero |
| domain restriction omitted | 3 | **0** | a domain is not a pointwise property, so no derivative can see it |
| swapped limits on (b) | 1 | **0** | the antiderivative behind the wrong answer is flawless; only the subtraction is backwards |
| **naming errors** | **7** | **0** | **the name is not part of the function** |
| **total** | **14** | **0** | |

Over all 26 seeded cases: **12 detected, 14 hidden**, and the split is exact rather than approximate.

> **Differentiating back detects every error that changes the function, and is structurally blind to every error that does not.**
> A wrong technique name does not change the function, so it is invisible.

### The naming blind spot, measured rather than asserted

This is Q16.2's headline and it deserved a real measurement rather than an argument.

Seven naming errors were seeded, each one a **correct antiderivative carrying a wrong technique name**:

| part | what was actually done | what was claimed | why a student writes it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| c | algebra, then the power rule | "substitution" | reciting the technique list instead of reading your own work |
| c | algebra, then the power rule | nothing written | the instruction is the second sentence and gets skipped |
| a | substitution, $u=\sqrt{2x}$ | "integration by parts" | naming a technique that appears **zero times** in any course file |
| a | substitution, $u=\sqrt{2x}$ | "the chain rule" | naming a *differentiation* rule for an integration step |
| b | substitution, $u=\tan x$ | "substitution, $u=\sec^2 x$" | right technique, wrong $u$ - the $u$ recorded is not the one used |
| d | rewrite, then substitution $u=\sec x$ | "trigonometric identity only" | naming the rewrite and dropping the substitution that did the work |
| d | rewrite, then substitution $u=\sec x$ | "integration by parts" | a technique this course never introduces |

The detector was run on all seven.
**Detected: zero of seven.**
And the measurement is stronger than a threshold comparison: for every one of the seven, the derivative values the detector computed were **bit-identical** to the ones it computes for the correct answer.
That is the real statement.
The detector never sees the name at all, so its input is literally unchanged, and no tolerance anywhere in the check could alter the verdict.

**The blind-spot arm needs a control, or it proves nothing.**
"Adding a wrong label is undetected" and "my detector is broken" produce identical output.
Two controls were run:

- A **non-constant perturbation of the same magnitude** as the seeded $+7$ constant, namely $F + 0.007x$, which the same detector **does** catch, at a relative gap of $4.932\times10^{-3}$.
- An answer that is **both misnamed and mathematically wrong**, $\sec^3 x$ for part (d), caught at a relative gap of $2.000$ - by the mathematics, not by the name.

So the detector is alive, and the naming blind spot is a property of the check rather than a defect in this run's instrument.

### What to do instead, since there is no instrument

Four habits, in order of how much they are worth.

1. **Write the name at the moment you take the step.**
   Section 2.3 proves that the finished answer cannot tell you which route produced it.
2. **Run the two-way audit from section 5.**
   It is the only check that exists, it is not automatic, and it takes ten seconds.
3. **Never write a technique name that is not in section 2.1's list.**
   The list is one entry long plus "none", so this is easy to obey.
4. **Sanity-check the definite answer's sign and magnitude.**
   $\tan x\sec^2 x$ is positive throughout $[0,\pi/3]$, so the integral must be positive; the swapped-limit answer $-\frac32$ fails that in one glance, and it is the only way to catch it.

### The cheap magnitude checks

| part | check | what it rules out |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| a | the answer is bounded by $\sqrt2 \approx 1.414$ for every $x$, because it is $\sqrt2$ times a sine | a lost or duplicated constant |
| b | the integrand is positive on $[0,\pi/3]$, so the answer is positive | swapped limits |
| b | $\tan\frac\pi3 = \sqrt3$, so $\frac{\tan^2}{2} = \frac32$ exactly - a whole number over 2 | an arithmetic slip at the last step |
| c | as $x\to\infty$ the $\frac{x^2}{2}$ term dominates, so the answer grows like $\frac{x^2}{2}$ | a wrong leading term |
| d | at $x=0$, $\sec0=1$, so $F(0)=\frac13$ | a lost $\frac13$ |

**Numbers worth holding.**
$\sqrt2 = 1.414213562373$, $2\sqrt5 = 4.472135955000$, and part (d) on $[0,\pi/4]$ is $\frac{2\sqrt2-1}{3} = 0.609475708249$.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both exams are Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so a correct method with a bad keystroke scores zero.

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| `+ C` on parts (a), (c), (d) | omit it, or put one on part (b), which is a number |
| `sqrt(2)*sin(sqrt(2*x))` | `sqrt2 sin sqrt2x`, which will not parse |
| `2*sqrt(5)/sqrt(x)` or `2*sqrt(5)*x^(-1/2)` | `2sqrt5/sqrtx` |
| `x^(-3/2)` with brackets round the exponent | `x^-3/2`, which parses as $\frac{x^{-3}}{2}$ |
| `(sec(x))^3/3` | `sec^3(x)/3`, which most parsers reject |
| `3/2` | `1.5` when the exact form was asked for, and never `1,5` |
| bracket the whole numerator and denominator | rely on precedence in a typed fraction |

**The single most expensive typo on this question is `x^-3/2`.**
Part (c)'s middle term carries a negative fractional exponent, and written without brackets it becomes $\frac{x^{-3}}{2}$, which is a different function entirely.
Bracket every exponent that is not a single digit.

**Second most expensive: `sec^3(x)`.**
The parser wants the function applied first and then raised, so `(sec(x))^3`.
This bites on part (d), and the same shape bites on part (b) if you enter the antiderivative rather than the number.

**Third: the missing `+ C`.**
Three of the four parts are indefinite, and section 6 proves that no check you run will remind you.

**A note about the naming half.**
The instruction "Indicate the technique you are using" is a written instruction on a written paper.
Where this item is delivered answer-only, there may be no box in which a technique name can be typed at all.
That is worth knowing and worth not guessing about: **no marking scheme exists in any course file**, so how - or whether - the naming half is captured in the electronic delivery is not something these files answer.
Write the name anyway wherever there is room, since it costs seconds and the printed instruction asks for it.

**Preview every answer before submitting.**
Möbius renders what it parsed, so if the preview shows $\frac{x^{-3}}{2}$ where you meant $x^{-3/2}$, you have found the error for free.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
Across all seven families below there are only **two distinct technique names**: "substitution, $u=\ldots$" and "none, algebra and the power rule".
Everything else is which $u$, and how much rearranging you had to do before the $u$ was visible.

Every row was verified two ways: the antiderivative was differentiated back by complex-step at five interior points, and the definite value was compared against tanh-sinh quadrature of the integrand, which never sees the closed form.
**22 catalog rows, 0 failures.**

### Family 0 - no technique at all: algebra, then the power rule

**Trigger:** a single-term denominator, or a bracket to a small power, or anything that becomes a sum of powers after one line of algebra.
**Tool:** the power rule, term by term.
**Name to write:** *none required - algebraic simplification, then the power rule*.

| integrand | antiderivative | verified definite value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{x^3+\sqrt{5x}-4}{x^2}$ **(the exam, part c)** | $\dfrac{x^2}{2}-\dfrac{2\sqrt5}{\sqrt x}+\dfrac4x+C$ | on $[1,4]$: $6.736067977500$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2+3}{\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac25 x^{5/2}+6\sqrt x+C$ | on $[1,4]$: $18.400000000000$ |
| $\dfrac{(x-1)^2}{x}$ | $\dfrac{x^2}{2}-2x+\ln x+C$ | on $[1,3]$: $1.098612288668$ |
| $\dfrac{2x^3-\sqrt x}{x^3}$ | $2x+\dfrac{2}{3}x^{-3/2}+C$ | on $[1,4]$: $5.416666666667$ |
| $\left(x^2-4\right)^2$ **(Q16.1 part d)** | $\dfrac{x^5}{5}-\dfrac{8x^3}{3}+16x+C$ | on $[0,3]$: $24.600000000000$ |

**How to read this family.**
Every row is the same move: get rid of the fraction or the bracket, then apply one rule.
The only thing that varies is the exponent arithmetic, and that is where every lost mark in this family comes from.

### Family 1 - substitution with $u$ under a root

**Trigger:** a root of a linear expression, with the reciprocal of a matching root elsewhere in the integrand.
**Tool:** $u$ = the root itself.
**Name to write:** *substitution*, $u=\sqrt{\ldots}$.

| integrand | antiderivative | verified definite value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{\cos\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt x}$ **(the exam, part a)** | $\sqrt2\sin\left(\sqrt{2x}\right)+C$ | on $[0.5,2]$: $0.095921074189$ |
| $\dfrac{\sin\sqrt x}{\sqrt x}$ | $-2\cos\left(\sqrt x\right)+C$ | on $[0.5,4]$: $2.352782867246$ |
| $\dfrac{\cos\sqrt{3x}}{\sqrt x}$ | $\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\sin\left(\sqrt{3x}\right)+C$ | on $[0.5,2]$: $-0.349368156258$ |
| $x\sqrt{x-1}$ **(Q16.1 part e)** | $\dfrac25(x-1)^{5/2}+\dfrac23(x-1)^{3/2}+C$ | on $[2,4]$: $8.632817855719$ |

**The structure worth naming.**
Rows 1 to 3 are the same integral with the constant under the root changed, and the constant that comes out front is $\frac{2}{\sqrt k}$ where $k$ is that constant.
For $k=2$ that is $\frac{2}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2$, which is why part (a)'s answer looks like it does.
For $k=1$ it is $2$, and for $k=3$ it is $\frac{2}{\sqrt3}$.
**One formula generates all three, and knowing it turns any reworded version of part (a) into a ten-second problem.**

### Family 2 - substitution with $u$ a trig function

**Trigger:** a power of $\sec$ or $\tan$ or $\cos$ sitting beside that function's own derivative.
**Tool:** $u$ = the trig function whose derivative you can see.
**Name to write:** *substitution*, $u=\sec x$ or $u=\tan x$ or $u=\cos x$.

| integrand | the $du$ you are looking for | antiderivative | verified definite value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\tan x\sec^2 x$ **(the exam, part b)** | $\sec^2 x\,dx$, so $u=\tan x$ | $\dfrac{\tan^2x}{2}+C$ | on $[0,\pi/3]$: $1.500000000000$ |
| $\sec^3 x\tan x$ **(the exam, part d)** | $\sec x\tan x\,dx$, so $u=\sec x$ | $\dfrac{\sec^3x}{3}+C$ | on $[0,\pi/4]$: $0.609475708249$ |
| $\sec^4 x\tan x$ | $\sec x\tan x\,dx$, so $u=\sec x$ | $\dfrac{\sec^4x}{4}+C$ | on $[0,\pi/4]$: $0.750000000000$ |
| $\tan^2 x\sec^2 x$ | $\sec^2 x\,dx$, so $u=\tan x$ | $\dfrac{\tan^3x}{3}+C$ | on $[0,\pi/3]$: $1.732050807569$ |
| $\sin x\cos^3 x$ | $-\sin x\,dx$, so $u=\cos x$ | $-\dfrac{\cos^4x}{4}+C$ | on $[0,\pi/3]$: $0.234375000000$ |

**The whole family is one decision: which trig function's derivative can you see?**
If you see $\sec^2 x\,dx$, take $u=\tan x$.
If you see $\sec x\tan x\,dx$, take $u=\sec x$.
If you see $\sin x\,dx$, take $u=\cos x$ and carry the minus.
Rows 2 and 3 differ only in the power, and the answer's denominator is always the new power - which is the power rule doing the work after the substitution, not anything trigonometric.

### Family 3 - one answer, two technique lists

**This is Q16.2's signature family and it exists nowhere else in the category.**
**Trigger:** an integrand you can attack more than one way.
**Tool:** a decision, and the point is that the name list must match the route you took.

| integrand | route 1, and its name list | route 2, and its name list | measured relationship |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sec^3x\tan x$ | hint rewrite, substitution $u=\sec x$ | rewrite as $\frac{\sin x}{\cos^4x}$, substitution $u=\cos x$ | identical function, $4.441\times10^{-16}$ |
| $\dfrac{\cos\sqrt{2x}}{\sqrt x}$ | substitution $u=\sqrt{2x}$ | substitution $u=\sqrt x$ | identical function, $6.661\times10^{-16}$ |
| $\tan x\sec^2 x$ | substitution $u=\tan x$ | substitution $u=\sec x$ | differ by exactly $\frac12$ |
| $\dfrac{\sqrt{5x}}{x^2}$ | divide through, power rule: **no technique** | substitution $u=5x$: **substitution** | identical function, difference $0$ |
| $(2x+1)^3$ | expand, power rule: **no technique** | the $1/a$ rule, $u=2x+1$: **substitution** | both give $78$ on $[0,2]$ |

**Rows 4 and 5 are the ones to study**, because they are the two where the *name changes*.
On rows 1 to 3 both routes are substitutions and only the $u$ differs.
On rows 4 and 5 one route needs no technique and the other needs one, from the same integrand, both correct.

**Row 5 is worth doing both ways once with a pen.**
$(2x+1)^3$ expands to $8x^3+12x^2+6x+1$, whose antiderivative is $2x^4+4x^3+3x^2+x$.
The $1/a$ route gives $\frac{(2x+1)^4}{8}$.
These differ by the constant $\frac18$, and on $[0,2]$ both give $78.000000000000$, verified.
The expansion route is longer and needs no technique; the $1/a$ route is faster and needs one.
**Neither is wrong, and each owes its own name.**

### Family 4 - definite integrals, where the limits carry the marks

**Trigger:** limits on the integral sign.
**Tool:** either change the limits with the variable, or back-substitute and use the originals.
**Name to write:** *substitution*, plus the changed limits if you changed them.

| integral | technique line | verified value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3}\tan x\sec^2x\,dx$ **(the exam, part b)** | substitution $u=\tan x$; limits $0\to\sqrt3$ | $1.500000000000$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan x\sec^2x\,dx$ | substitution $u=\tan x$; limits $0\to1$ | $0.500000000000$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\tan x\,dx$ | none - this is a recognised antiderivative | $1.000000000000$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^{2}\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}\,dx$ | substitution $u=x^2+1$; limits $1\to5$ | $1.236067977500$ |

**Row 3 is the trap in this family.**
$\sec x\tan x$ is the derivative of $\sec x$, so there is nothing to substitute - you already know the antiderivative.
Writing "substitution, $u=\sec x$" there is a naming error of exactly the kind section 6 shows is undetectable.

**Three rules for the limits, and each has cost someone marks.**
Upper limit first, always: $F(b)-F(a)$.
If you change the variable, change the limits, and then never go back to $x$.
If you back-substitute instead, use the original $x$ limits, and never mix the two.

### Family 5 - when the exam hands you the rewrite

**Trigger:** a printed hint.
**Tool:** whatever the hint says.
**Name to write:** the rewrite, then the technique it exposes.

Part (d) is the only hinted item on Final Exam 2, and the hint is doing something specific: it converts an integrand with no visible $u$ into one where $du$ is bracketed for you.

| what the hint gives you | what it exposes |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\sec^3x\tan x = \sec^2 x\,(\sec x\tan x)$ | $\sec x\tan x\,dx$ is $d(\sec x)$, so $u=\sec x$ |

**Take the hint.**
It is not a shortcut around the technique; it *is* the technique's setup step, printed because the examiner wants the substitution rather than the sine-and-cosine detour.
Name both halves: "using the hint's rewrite, then substitution with $u=\sec x$".

**And note what the hint does not do**: it does not tell you the answer, name the technique, or supply the $+C$.
All three are still yours.

### Family 6 - where naming goes wrong while the calculus goes right

**Trigger:** a correct antiderivative and a wrong label.
This family has no mathematics in it, which is exactly why it is easy to skip and expensive to skip.

| the labelling error | what it looks like | why it happens |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| naming a technique you did not use | "substitution" written over part (c)'s pure algebra | reciting the technique list instead of reading your own work |
| naming nothing at all | four correct antiderivatives, no words | the instruction is the second sentence of two |
| naming the wrong $u$ | "substitution, $u=\sec^2x$" over work that used $u=\tan x$ | the technique line was written after the algebra, from memory |
| naming only the rewrite | "trig identity" over part (d) | the rewrite was the interesting bit, so it eclipsed the substitution |
| naming a technique this course never taught | "integration by parts" anywhere on this item | panic, plus a half-remembered later course |
| naming a differentiation rule | "chain rule" for a substitution | substitution *is* the reverse chain rule, so the confusion is honest |

**The last row deserves a caveat rather than a flat prohibition.**
The Review file itself glosses substitution as the "reverse chain rule", once, so the connection is real and the course does state it.
But "the chain rule" alone is a differentiation rule, and writing it as your integration technique names the wrong direction.
If you want to say it, say "substitution, which is the chain rule in reverse, with $u=\ldots$" - the technique is still named and the insight is free.

**Every one of these six was seeded into the census and none was detected.**
That is not a defect in the check; it is the definition of the failure mode.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

1. **Not naming the technique at all.**
   The instruction is the second of two sentences and it is the easiest thing on the paper to read past.
   It is also half of what makes this item different from Q16.1.
   Cost is unknown because no rubric exists, but it is not zero on a question that asks in writing.
2. **Leaving part (c)'s technique line blank because there was no technique.**
   A blank line and a skipped instruction are indistinguishable.

## 12. Closing category 16 - one procedure for both questions

Category 16 has two questions and they are now both written.
They divide the subject cleanly, and the division is worth stating because it tells you what to revise where.

### The single procedure that covers both

> **1. Classify.** Limits present? Then a number and no $+C$. Absent? Then a function and $+C$.
> **2. Algebra first.** Divide through, expand, roots to powers. A sum of powers is a solved problem.
> **3. Then $u$ and $du$.** Pick $u$, compute $du$, and check $du$ is present up to a constant.
> **4. Then an identity.** Rewrite, and re-enter at step 2.
> **5. Finish.** $+C$, or $F(b)-F(a)$ upper first. State the domain if the answer has a root or a reciprocal.
> **6. Differentiate back.** It catches every error that changes the function and nothing else.
> **7. If the question asked, name what you did** - and audit the names in both directions, because step 6 cannot.

Steps 1 to 6 are Q16.1's.
Step 7 is Q16.2's, and it is the only step in the whole category that has no automatic check.

### Which question teaches what

| | Q16.1 - Five integrals | Q16.2 - Four integrals, naming the technique |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| paper | Final Exam 1, item 8 | Final Exam 2, item 6 |
| points | 20 over five parts | 6 over four parts |
| the ladder | **establishes it** - the five parts are rungs 1, 4, 2, 1, 3 | uses it - the four parts are rungs 3, 3, 1, 3 |
| what it drills | *doing* the technique | *describing* the technique |
| its hard part | recognising which rung you are on | recognising when the rung is "none" |
| the check | establishes that differentiating back partitions the errors exactly | shows the partition has a fourth blind family, and it is naming |
| the trap it punishes | reaching for substitution when algebra would do | reaching for a technique *name* when no technique was used |
| what to revise from it | the substitution mechanics, and $+C$ | the vocabulary, the granularity, and the two-way audit |

**The two questions share their trap and invert their emphasis.**
Q16.1's part (a) and part (d) need no technique, and Q16.2's part (c) needs no technique.
On Q16.1 the cost of missing that is doing extra work.
On Q16.2 the cost is naming a technique you did not use, which is a positive false claim.

**The rung distribution is the fastest way to see the difference.**
Q16.1 spreads its five parts across four rungs, so it is a survey.
Q16.2 puts three of its four parts on the same rung - substitution - and uses the fourth to ask whether you noticed that the third was not a technique at all.
It is not a survey; it is one discrimination, asked four times.

**Where to spend revision time, given the point split.**
Q16.1 is worth 20 points and Q16.2 is worth 6.
But Q16.2 sits on the same paper as Q8.4, which asks the identical favour for derivatives, and between them they are 10 of Final Exam 2's 81 points.
**The naming habit is therefore worth practising once, properly, and then it applies to two items on the same paper.**
That is the best return in this category.
