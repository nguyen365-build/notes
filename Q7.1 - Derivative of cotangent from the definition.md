Use the definition of the derivative as a limit to compute the derivative of $\cot x.$

## 0. The 30-second version

The answer is worth nothing here.
This is a *proof* question: the six marks are in the algebra of the limit, and a student who writes "$-\csc^2x$" on a blank page has produced the same document as a student who wrote nothing.
So the thing to memorise is not the result, it is the chain.

```
d/dx cot x  =  lim (h->0) [ cot(x+h) - cot x ] / h          <- WRITE THE DEFINITION
             = lim [ cos(x+h)/sin(x+h) - cos x / sin x ] / h  <- cot = cos/sin
             = lim [ cos(x+h) sin x - cos x sin(x+h) ]
                     / [ h sin(x+h) sin x ]                   <- ONE denominator
             = lim [ -sin h ] / [ h sin(x+h) sin x ]           <- sin(A-B), A=x, B=x+h
             = lim -(sin h / h) * 1/(sin(x+h) sin x)           <- SPLIT the h off the x
             = -(1) * 1/(sin x sin x)                          <- sin h / h -> 1
             = -1/sin^2 x  =  -csc^2 x
```

Seven lines, and the only one that takes any thought is the fourth.
Write the other six from memory and think only about that one.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

Nothing in this list can be derived during the exam.
All five have to be recall, because the six marks are spent on assembling them, not on finding them.

### 2.1 The definition, in both of its spellings

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
\qquad\qquad
f'(a)=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}$$

These are the same statement.
The left one holds the base point fixed and shrinks a step $h$; the right one holds the base point fixed and slides a second point $x$ toward it.
Set $x=a+h$ in the right-hand one and it becomes the left-hand one.

Use the **$h$ form** when the question says "compute the derivative of $f$", because the answer is a function of $x$.
Use the **$x\to a$ form** when the question hands you a specific point, or when a limit is disguised as something else and you are asked to evaluate it (section 8, Family 9).

Two details that cost marks when they are dropped.

The limit is over $h$, and $x$ is a **constant** for the whole calculation.
Anything not containing $h$ can be pulled out in front of the limit sign, and that is the move that makes the last line work.

The definition asserts that the limit **exists**.
Where it does not, the derivative does not exist, and for $\cot$ that happens at every multiple of $\pi$ (section 8, Family 11).

### 2.2 The two standard trig limits

$$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1
\qquad\qquad
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}=0$$

These are the *only* two facts about trigonometry-and-limits you are allowed to assume, and every trig derivative on the course is built from them.
Both are checked in the verification file at $h=10^{-2}$, $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-6}$.

Three companions worth having, all verified:

| limit | value | where it comes from |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\tan h}{h}$ | $1$ | $\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos h}$, and $\cos h\to1$ |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-\cos h}{h^2}$ | $\tfrac12$ | multiply by $\frac{1+\cos h}{1+\cos h}$, then use $\sin^2$ |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin(kh)}{h}$ | $k$ | write it as $k\cdot\frac{\sin(kh)}{kh}$ |

**Which of the two you actually need is a fact about the function, not about you.**
This is the single most useful structural thing in the whole topic, and section 8 Family 2 lays it out for all six trig functions.
For $\cot$, and for $\tan$, **you need only $\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1$**, because the $\cos h$ terms cancel identically before any limit is taken.
For $\sin$ and $\cos$ you need both.
The course's own review notes list $\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0$ among the "key identities needed" for this question; that is an over-listing, and section 12 records it.

### 2.3 The sine subtraction formula, which is the one identity that matters

$$\sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B$$

You will use it **backwards**.
That is the move that makes this question short, and it is the move students miss.

Set $A=x$ and $B=x+h$:

$$\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)=\sin x\cos(x+h)-\cos x\sin(x+h)$$

The left side is $\sin(-h)=-\sin h$, because sine is odd.
The right side is, character for character, the numerator you get when you put $\cot(x+h)-\cot x$ over one denominator.
So:

$$\boxed{\ \cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)=-\sin h\ }$$

That identity was checked at **91 different $(x,h)$ pairs**, thirteen values of $x$ spread over four quadrants crossed with seven values of $h$ including negative ones, and it holds at every one.

Two things about it deserve a moment.

The right-hand side has **no $x$ in it at all.**
An expression in two variables collapsed to an expression in one.
That is not luck; it is what a subtraction formula does, and it is the reason this question is six marks rather than sixteen.

The **sign is the entire difference between $\cot$ and $\tan$.**
Run the same move on $\tan$ and the numerator is $\sin(x+h)\cos x-\sin x\cos(x+h)=+\sin h$.
Same identity, arguments swapped, opposite sign, and that one sign is why $\tan'=+\sec^2x$ and $\cot'=-\csc^2x$.
Both signs are verified.

### 2.4 The Pythagorean identity in its cotangent form

$$1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x
\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad
\csc^2x=\frac{1}{\sin^2x}$$

You do not need this for Route A, but you need it to recognise that $-\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}$, $-\csc^2x$ and $-(1+\cot^2x)$ are one number written three ways, and you need it if you take Route C, where $-\csc^2x$ falls out of the algebra before the limit is even taken.

Derive it in one line if it slips: divide $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$ through by $\sin^2x$.

### 2.5 What "from the definition" permits you to use

This is the boundary that decides whether your six lines earn six marks.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
| :--- | :--- |
| the definition of $\cot$ as $\cos x/\sin x$ | the derivative of $\cot$, in any form |
| any trigonometric **identity** (addition, subtraction, Pythagorean, sum-to-product) | the quotient rule, product rule, chain rule, reciprocal rule |
| the two standard trig limits | the derivatives of $\sin$ and $\cos$, quoted as facts |
| the algebra of limits: sums, products, quotients of limits that exist | L'Hopital's rule |
| the continuity of $\sin$, so that $\sin(x+h)\to\sin x$ | anything with $\frac{d}{dx}$ already applied to a trig function |

The right-hand column has one thing in common: every entry is a *result about derivatives*.
The left-hand column is identities and limits.
That is the line.

## 3. The universal procedure

This works for every "from the definition" question on the course, not only for $\cot$.

> **Step 1 - Write the definition, with your function substituted in.**
> Do this before you think about anything else.
> It is the first mark, it is free, and it is the mark most often lost by starting with the algebra.
>
> **Step 2 - Classify the numerator, which picks your first move.**
> One glance at the shape of $f$ tells you which of four algebraic moves opens the problem.
>
> | $f$ looks like | first move | why |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | a polynomial | **expand** $f(x+h)$ and cancel | every surviving term has an $h$ in it |
> | a quotient, $\dfrac{u}{v}$ | **one denominator** | you cannot simplify a difference of two fractions |
> | a root | **multiply by the conjugate** | the difference of two roots hides an $h$ |
> | a trig function | **an identity**, then one of the two limits | $h$ never cancels; it pairs with $\sin h$ |
>
> $\cot x$ is a quotient **and** trigonometric, so it takes two of those moves in order: one denominator first, then an identity.
>
> **Step 3 - Collapse the numerator with an identity, if it is trigonometric.**
> The target is to get every $x$ out of the numerator, leaving something that is a function of $h$ alone.
> For $\cot$ and $\tan$ that target is reachable exactly, and the numerator becomes $\mp\sin h$.
>
> **Step 4 - Separate the $h$ part from the $x$ part.**
> Write the quotient as a **product** of a factor whose limit is a standard trig limit and a factor whose limit is ordinary substitution.
> This is the step that makes the limit legal, and it is worth saying out loud in the write-up: both factors have limits, so the limit of the product is the product of the limits.
>
> **Step 5 - Take the limit, and say which fact you used for each factor.**
> $\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1$ is the standard limit.
> $\sin(x+h)\to\sin x$ is the continuity of sine, and naming it is free credit.
>
> **Step 6 - Simplify, state the domain, and sanity-check the sign.**
> $-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x$, valid wherever $\sin x\neq0$.
> $\cot$ is decreasing on every one of its branches, so a negative derivative is the expected shape.

## 4. Q7.1 worked four ways

All four routes are legitimate uses of the definition, and all four give $-\csc^2x$.
Route A is the one to write under pressure.
The others exist because the examiner can reach the same mathematics by rewording, and because seeing the same answer arrive by four roads is the strongest possible check that the answer is right.

### Route A - one denominator, then the sine subtraction formula (use this one)

**Step 1. The definition.**

$$\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}$$

**Step 2. Write each cotangent as a quotient and combine over one denominator.**

$$\cot(x+h)-\cot x=\frac{\cos(x+h)}{\sin(x+h)}-\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}
=\frac{\cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)}{\sin(x+h)\sin x}$$

Cross-multiplying is all that happened.
The common denominator is $\sin(x+h)\sin x$, and the numerator is "first times the other's bottom, minus second times the other's bottom".

**Step 3. Recognise the numerator.**
By the sine subtraction formula with $A=x$ and $B=x+h$,

$$\cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)=\sin x\cos(x+h)-\cos x\sin(x+h)=\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)=\sin(-h)=-\sin h$$

**Step 4. Put it back and split the fraction into two factors.**

$$\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}=\frac{-\sin h}{h\,\sin(x+h)\sin x}
=-\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(x+h)\sin x}$$

**Step 5. Take the limit of each factor.**
As $h\to0$, the first factor tends to $1$ by the standard limit, and the second tends to $\dfrac{1}{\sin x\cdot\sin x}$ because sine is continuous.
Both limits exist, so the limit of the product is the product of the limits:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-1\cdot\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=\boxed{-\csc^2x}$$

valid for every $x$ with $\sin x\neq0$, that is, for $x\neq k\pi$.

Every one of those five steps was verified as an identity in two free variables at 91 $(x,h)$ pairs, not merely as a chain that ends at the right answer.

### Route B - expand both angles with the addition formulas

The course's review notes point at this route, and it works, but it is one line longer and it has one more place to slip.

Expand top and bottom of $\cot(x+h)$:

$$\cot(x+h)=\frac{\cos(x+h)}{\sin(x+h)}=\frac{\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h}{\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h}$$

Subtract $\cot x=\dfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}$ over the common denominator $\sin x(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)$.
The numerator is

$$\sin x(\cos x\cos h-\sin x\sin h)-\cos x(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)$$

$$=\underbrace{\sin x\cos x\cos h-\cos x\sin x\cos h}_{\text{these cancel}}-\sin h\underbrace{(\sin^2x+\cos^2x)}_{=1}=-\sin h$$

Same numerator, reached by grinding instead of by recognising.
Notice the denominator: $\sin x(\sin x\cos h+\cos x\sin h)=\sin x\sin(x+h)$, which is exactly Route A's denominator, so the two routes meet at the identical expression and finish identically.
Both facts are verified at 91 pairs.

**The instructive part of Route B is what does *not* happen.**
Both $\cos h$ terms cancel *before any limit is taken*, so this route never needs $\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0$ either.
That is worth knowing, because it is the opposite of what happens with $\sin$ and $\cos$, where a $\cos h$ survives to the very end and forces you to use the second standard limit.

### Route C - the cotangent addition formula

Shorter than either of the above if you happen to know

$$\cot(A+B)=\frac{\cot A\cot B-1}{\cot A+\cot B}$$

Then

$$\cot(x+h)-\cot x=\frac{\cot x\cot h-1}{\cot x+\cot h}-\cot x
=\frac{\cot x\cot h-1-\cot^2x-\cot x\cot h}{\cot x+\cot h}
=\frac{-(1+\cot^2x)}{\cot x+\cot h}=\frac{-\csc^2x}{\cot x+\cot h}$$

The answer has already appeared, before any limit.
Divide by $h$ and read the denominator carefully:

$$\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}=\frac{-\csc^2x}{h\cot x+h\cot h}$$

As $h\to0$, $h\cot x\to0$ because $\cot x$ is a constant, and $h\cot h=\dfrac{h\cos h}{\sin h}\to1$ because $\frac{\sin h}{h}\to1$ and $\cos h\to1$.
The denominator tends to $1$, so the limit is $-\csc^2x$.

Verified: the addition formula itself, the collapse to $-(1+\cot^2x)/(\cot x+\cot h)$, and $h(\cot x+\cot h)\to1$ at $h=10^{-4}$ and $10^{-6}$ for three values of $x$.

This route is genuinely elegant, and it is a bad exam choice for exactly one reason: almost nobody has the cotangent addition formula memorised, and reconstructing it under pressure costs more than Route A costs in total.

### Route D - the $x\to a$ form of the definition

Same mathematics, different bookkeeping, and this is the form you need when the question is phrased as a limit rather than as a derivative.

$$\cot'(a)=\lim_{x\to a}\frac{\cot x-\cot a}{x-a}$$

One denominator gives numerator $\cos x\sin a-\cos a\sin x=\sin(a-x)=-\sin(x-a)$, so

$$\frac{\cot x-\cot a}{x-a}=-\frac{\sin(x-a)}{x-a}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin x\sin a}$$

Now $x-a$ plays the role $h$ played, and it tends to $0$, so the first factor tends to $1$ and

$$\cot'(a)=-\frac{1}{\sin^2a}$$

Verified as an identity at twelve $(a,x)$ combinations and as a limit at $x-a=\pm10^{-4}$ for five values of $a$.

### Route W - the answers that are right and score nothing

| What gets written | The verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| $-\csc^2 x$, with no working | The memorised fact. The question asked you to derive it. |
| The quotient rule on $\cos x/\sin x$ | A correct derivation of the wrong thing: it assumes $\sin'=\cos$ and $\cos'=-\sin$, which are themselves definition results, and it uses a rule the definition is supposed to justify. |
| $\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x)^{-1}$ by the chain rule | Same objection, plus it now also assumes $\tan'=\sec^2x$. |

| $-\csc^2 x$ with no domain statement | Not a no-credit answer, but an incomplete one: $\cot$ has no derivative at $x=k\pi$ because it is not even defined there. |

### The route comparison

| | A (one denominator) | B (expand both) | C (cot addition formula) | D ($x\to a$) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

Keep Route B in reserve: if you cannot recall the subtraction formula, expanding both angles will still get you there, because the cancellation happens whether or not you saw it coming.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Six points, one part.

| Proposed mark | For |

**The short form, which is what to write if time is tight.**

> By definition, $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}$.
> Writing $\cot=\dfrac{\cos}{\sin}$ and combining over the common denominator $\sin(x+h)\sin x$ gives
> $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)}{h\,\sin(x+h)\sin x}$.
> By the sine subtraction formula the numerator is $\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)=\sin(-h)=-\sin h$, so the quotient is
> $\displaystyle -\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(x+h)\sin x}$.
> Since $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$ and $\sin(x+h)\to\sin x$, the limit is $-\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x$.

**The full-credit form, which adds the three clauses that show understanding.**

> **By definition**, $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}$.
>
> Using $\cot t=\dfrac{\cos t}{\sin t}$ and combining the two fractions over the common denominator $\sin(x+h)\sin x$,
> $$\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}=\frac{\cos(x+h)\sin x-\cos x\sin(x+h)}{h\,\sin(x+h)\sin x}.$$
>
> **By the sine subtraction formula** $\sin(A-B)=\sin A\cos B-\cos A\sin B$, taken with $A=x$ and $B=x+h$, the numerator is
> $$\sin x\cos(x+h)-\cos x\sin(x+h)=\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)=\sin(-h)=-\sin h,$$
> using that sine is odd. Note the numerator no longer involves $x$.
>
> Hence
> $$\frac{\cot(x+h)-\cot x}{h}=-\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(x+h)\sin x}.$$
>
> **Each factor has a limit as $h\to0$**: the first tends to $1$ by the standard limit $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$, and the second tends to $\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}$ since $\sin$ is continuous, so $\sin(x+h)\to\sin x$. Because both limits exist, the limit of the product is the product of the limits, and
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\cot x=-1\cdot\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}=-\csc^2x,$$
> valid for all $x$ with $\sin x\neq0$, that is, for $x\neq k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

Three things a marker looks for that students routinely skip:

- **naming** the sine subtraction formula rather than letting $-\sin h$ appear from nowhere, which is where two of the six marks live;
- **citing** $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$ by name, since it is the only non-algebraic fact in the whole proof;
- **justifying the split** into two factors, in one clause, because a limit of a product is only the product of the limits when both exist.

And one thing to be careful *not* to write: do not say "cancel $h$".
Nothing cancels here.
The $h$ pairs up with $\sin h$ and is disposed of by a limit, not by algebra, and that distinction is exactly what separates the trig cases from the polynomial ones.

## 6. Verify before you commit

This takes about ten seconds and it catches every common failure on this question.

**The sign check, which is the one that matters.**
$\cot$ is **decreasing** on every branch: it falls from $+\infty$ down to $-\infty$ across each interval $(k\pi,(k+1)\pi)$.
So its derivative must be negative everywhere, with no exceptions and no sign changes.
If your algebra produced $+\csc^2x$, you reversed the subtraction somewhere, almost certainly by writing $\sin\big((x+h)-x\big)$ where you needed $\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)$.

**The magnitude check.**
$-\csc^2x=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}$, and $\sin^2x\le1$, so $|\cot'|\ge1$ always.
The **largest** value $\cot'$ ever takes is $-1$, attained where $\sin^2x=1$, that is at $x=\frac{\pi}{2}+k\pi$.
A derivative of magnitude less than 1 is impossible, so an answer like $-\frac12$ at some point is a guaranteed error.

**The three cheap invariants.**

1. **The numerator must lose its $x$.** After the identity, the numerator is $-\sin h$ with no $x$ anywhere in it. If your numerator still contains $x$, the identity was not applied, and the limit will not resolve.
2. **The two ends must match at a known point.** $\cot(\pi/4)=1$ and $\cot'(\pi/4)=-1/\sin^2(\pi/4)=-1/(1/2)=-2$. That is a whole number, so it is easy to hold and easy to check against.
3. **The independent cross-check is legal.** Differentiating $\cos x/\sin x$ by the quotient rule gives $\frac{-\sin^2x-\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}=-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}$, the same answer. That is not circular as a *check*, because it is a genuinely different derivation. It is only forbidden as the *answer*.

**The four numbers worth memorising for this question.**

$$\cot'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right)=-4,\qquad
\cot'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=-2,\qquad
\cot'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)=-\frac43,\qquad
\cot'\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=-1$$

All four are exact, all four are verified, and they come straight from $-1/\sin^2$ of $\frac12$, $\frac{\sqrt2}{2}$, $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $1$.
They are the fastest way to check a from-the-definition answer against a specific-point variation.

**The check that is not a check.**
Plugging in $h=10^{-6}$ on a calculator and seeing $-1.4123$ tells you your *answer* is plausible.
It tells you nothing at all about your *derivation*, and the derivation is what is being marked.
It also lies more often than you would expect: section 8 Family 13 has the numbers.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Möbius cannot mark a derivation, so this question reaches you as the pieces listed in the table at the top of this file.
Grading is by mathematical equivalence, so a correct answer is never rejected for its *form*, only for being *parsed* differently than you intended.

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The whole expression negated | `-1/(sin(x))^2` | `(-1/sin(x))^2`, which is positive |
| Squaring a function | `(csc(x))^2` or `csc(x)^2` | `csc^2(x)`, which is not valid syntax |
| Multiplication | `2*(csc(x))^2*cot(x)` | `2csc^2xcotx` |
| A reciprocal trig function | `1/sin(x)` is always safe | `csc(x)`, if the box has not shown it is accepted |
| Bracketing a denominator | `-1/(sin(x))^2` | `-1/sin(x)^2`, which is ambiguous across parsers |
| A fractional value | `-4/3` | `-1.333` |
| Naming an identity in a text box | `sin(A-B) = sin(A)cos(B) - cos(A)sin(B)` | a picture or a half-written formula |

The single most expensive mistake available on this question is **losing the minus sign inside a power**.
`(-csc(x))^2` is $+\csc^2x$, which is the sign-flipped wrong answer, and it looks almost identical to the right one on screen.
Write the minus outside everything: `-(csc(x))^2`.

The second most expensive is `csc^2(x)`.
Every computer algebra parser reads `csc^2` as "csc raised to the power 2" with nothing to raise, and it is a syntax error, not a wrong answer, so it produces a blank preview rather than a red cross.

**Always click the preview icon.**
An empty preview modal means broken syntax, not wrong mathematics.
These are all accepted as equivalent, so pick whichever you can type without a typo:
`-1/(sin(x))^2`, `-(csc(x))^2`, `-(1+(cot(x))^2)`, `-csc(x)*csc(x)`.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
Read each table as a **structure**, not as a list of facts: nearly every variation below is the same five-step procedure with one element swapped, and the tables are arranged so you can see which element moved.

### Family 0 - the shape of every "from the definition" question

There are only four algebraic openings, and the shape of $f$ picks one.
That is the entire classification, and it is worth internalising before any of the specific answers.

| $f$ | opening move | what disposes of the $h$ | example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| polynomial | expand $f(x+h)$ | $h$ **cancels** | $x^2$, $x^3$, $3x^2-5x+2$ |
| quotient | one denominator | $h$ **cancels** | $1/x$, $1/(x+1)$ |
| root | multiply by the conjugate | $h$ **cancels** | $\sqrt x$, $\sqrt{x+2}$ |
| trigonometric | an identity | $h$ **does not cancel**; it pairs with $\sin h$ and dies in a limit | $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$, $\cot$, $\sec$, $\csc$ |

The fourth row is the odd one out and it is the whole reason this question is on the paper.
In the first three rows the limit is decorative: once $h$ has cancelled you just substitute $h=0$.
In the fourth row the limit is doing real work, and no amount of algebra will remove it.

$\cot$ sits in rows two **and** four, which is why it takes two moves.

### Family 1 - the other five trigonometric functions

The trigger is that the function is trigonometric.
The tool is an identity, and **which identity is decided by the shape**.

| $f$ | first move | numerator collapses to | limits needed | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin x$ | expand $\sin(x+h)$ | $\sin x(\cos h-1)+\cos x\sin h$ | **both** | $\cos x$ |
| $\cos x$ | expand $\cos(x+h)$ | $\cos x(\cos h-1)-\sin x\sin h$ | **both** | $-\sin x$ |
| $\tan x$ | one denominator | $+\sin h$ | $\frac{\sin h}{h}$ only | $\sec^2x$ |
| $\cot x$ | one denominator | $-\sin h$ | $\frac{\sin h}{h}$ only | $-\csc^2x$ |
| $\sec x$ | one denominator | $2\sin(x+\frac h2)\sin\frac h2$ | $\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}$ only | $\sec x\tan x$ |
| $\csc x$ | one denominator | $-2\cos(x+\frac h2)\sin\frac h2$ | $\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}$ only | $-\csc x\cot x$ |

Read the table as **two groups, not six rows**.

$\sin$ and $\cos$ are the hard pair, because they are the *primitives*: nothing simpler exists to write them in terms of, so the addition formula is all you have, a $\cos h$ survives the algebra, and you are forced to use both standard limits.

The other four are all quotients of $\sin$ and $\cos$, so they all open with a common denominator, and in each case the numerator becomes a difference of two products that a subtraction formula or a sum-to-product formula collapses.
$\tan$ and $\cot$ collapse to $\pm\sin h$, an exact single term, and need only the sine limit.
$\sec$ and $\csc$ collapse to a product containing $\sin\frac h2$, so they need the sine limit written in its half-step form; take them by the addition formula instead and you will need both standard limits.

**The four signs in the answer column are not four facts.**
$\tan$ and $\sec$ are positive, $\cot$ and $\csc$ are negative, and the rule is the one that runs through all of trigonometry: **the "co-" functions get the minus sign.**
$\cos$, $\cot$ and $\csc$ all differentiate to something negative; $\sin$, $\tan$ and $\sec$ all differentiate to something positive.
Every entry in this table is verified numerically at six values of $x$ across three quadrants.

### Family 2 - the same question with a different constant inside

The trigger is $\cot(kx)$ or $\cot(x+c)$ rather than $\cot x$.
The tool is that $\frac{\sin(kh)}{h}\to k$, and **the chain rule is not needed and not permitted**.

| Asked | The one line that changes | Answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(2x)$ | numerator becomes $-\sin(2h)$, and $\frac{\sin 2h}{h}\to2$ | $-2\csc^2(2x)$ |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(kx)$ | $\frac{\sin kh}{h}\to k$ | $-k\csc^2(kx)$ |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(x+c)$ | nothing changes; $c$ rides along | $-\csc^2(x+c)$ |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\,3\cot x$ | the constant pulls out in front of the limit | $-3\csc^2x$ |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(x/2)$ | $\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h}\to\frac12$ | $-\frac12\csc^2(x/2)$ |

The $\cot(2x)$ case is worth working once in full, because Midterm 2 item 9(a) asks for $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(2x)$ by the ordinary rules, and knowing where the factor 2 comes from makes that question mechanical instead of memorised.
Running the definition on $g(x)=\cot(2x)$, the numerator collapses to $-\sin(2h)$ and the denominator is $h\sin(2x+2h)\sin 2x$, so the quotient is $-2\cdot\frac{\sin 2h}{2h}\cdot\frac{1}{\sin(2x+2h)\sin 2x}\to-2\csc^2(2x)$.
Verified at three values of $x$.

### Family 3 - a derivative at a single named point

The trigger is that the question hands you a number instead of asking for a function.
The tool is either route, finished by substituting.

| Asked | Answer | Decimal |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $\cot'(\pi/6)$ | $-4$ | $-4$ |
| $\cot'(\pi/4)$ | $-2$ | $-2$ |
| $\cot'(\pi/3)$ | $-\frac43$ | $-1.3333$ |
| $\cot'(\pi/2)$ | $-1$ | $-1$ |
| $\cot'(2\pi/3)$ | $-\frac43$ | $-1.3333$ |
| $\cot'(3\pi/4)$ | $-2$ | $-2$ |

Read that as structure: **only three distinct values appear**, because $-\csc^2$ depends on $x$ only through $\sin^2x$, and $\sin^2$ is symmetric about $\pi/2$.
So $\pi/6$ and $5\pi/6$ share a value, $\pi/4$ and $3\pi/4$ share one, $\pi/3$ and $2\pi/3$ share one.
And every value is negative, because $-\csc^2$ has no sign to vary.

### Family 4 - polynomials, the "cancel the $h$" family

The trigger is that $f$ is a polynomial.
The tool is expansion, and the marker is watching for whether you cancel $h$ correctly rather than for any cleverness.

| $f$ | numerator after expanding | after dividing by $h$ | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x^2$ | $2xh+h^2$ | $2x+h$ | $2x$ |
| $x^3$ | $3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3$ | $3x^2+3xh+h^2$ | $3x^2$ |
| $x^4$ | $4x^3h+6x^2h^2+4xh^3+h^4$ | $4x^3+\ldots$ | $4x^3$ |
| $3x^2-5x+2$ | $6xh+3h^2-5h$ | $6x+3h-5$ | $6x-5$ |
| $ax^2+bx+c$ | $2axh+ah^2+bh$ | $2ax+ah+b$ | $2ax+b$ |

Every row has the same shape: **every surviving term carries at least one $h$**, which is exactly why the constant term of $f$ vanishes and why the division is legal.
After dividing, setting $h=0$ is ordinary substitution, not a limit that needs any theorem.
All five verified.

### Family 5 - quotients, which is $\cot$ with the trigonometry removed

The trigger is $f=\dfrac{1}{\text{something}}$.
The tool is the common denominator, and this family is worth doing *specifically because it is Route A's first move with nothing else attached*.

$$\frac{\frac{1}{x+h}-\frac1x}{h}=\frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{x-(x+h)}{(x+h)x}=\frac{-h}{h(x+h)x}=\frac{-1}{(x+h)x}\ \longrightarrow\ -\frac{1}{x^2}$$

Compare that to Route A line by line.
Both combine over one denominator, both get a numerator that is a difference of two things that nearly cancel, and both end with the $x$ part evaluated by substitution.
The only difference is that $\frac1x$'s numerator becomes $-h$, which cancels, and $\cot$'s becomes $-\sin h$, which does not.

| $f$ | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $1/x$ | $-1/x^2$ |
| $1/(x+1)$ | $-1/(x+1)^2$ |
| $1/x^2$ | $-2/x^3$ |

Both of the first two verified, and the $\frac1x$-versus-$\cot$ parallel verified as an identity at four values of $x$.

### Family 6 - roots, the conjugate family

The trigger is a square root.
The tool is multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate, which is the only way to get an $h$ out of a difference of two roots.

$$\frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt x}{h}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}
=\frac{(x+h)-x}{h\left(\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x\right)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}
\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$$

| $f$ | $f'$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\sqrt x$ | $\frac{1}{2\sqrt x}$ |
| $\sqrt{x+2}$ | $\frac{1}{2\sqrt{x+2}}$ |

The conjugate step was verified as an identity, not just as an endpoint, at four values of $x$ and two values of $h$: $\frac{\sqrt{x+h}-\sqrt x}{h}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+h}+\sqrt x}$ exactly, for every $h$, before any limit is taken.

### Family 7 - the standard limits asked on their own

The trigger is a bare limit with no derivative in sight.
The tool is to force the expression into the shape $\frac{\sin(\text{something})}{\text{the same something}}$.

| Limit | Value | Move |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}$ | $1$ | the standard limit itself |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin 5h}{3h}$ | $\frac53$ | write it as $\frac53\cdot\frac{\sin 5h}{5h}$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin 2h}{\sin 7h}$ | $\frac27$ | divide top and bottom by $h$, then use the limit twice |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\tan h}{h}$ | $1$ | $\frac{\sin h}{h}\cdot\frac{1}{\cos h}$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos h-1}{h}$ | $0$ | the second standard limit |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-\cos h}{h^2}$ | $\frac12$ | multiply by $\frac{1+\cos h}{1+\cos h}$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin(-3h)}{h}$ | $-3$ | $k=-3$ in $\frac{\sin kh}{h}\to k$ |

Read the structure: **every one of these is $\frac{\sin kh}{h}\to k$ in disguise, except the last two, which are the cosine limit.**
The recognition cue is that the argument of the sine and the denominator must be *made the same* before the standard limit applies; multiplying by $\frac kk$ is how you make them the same.
All seven verified.

### Family 8 - the "justify each step" reword

The trigger is wording like "explain the procedure you are using", which this course prints on Midterm 1 item 3, or "state the identities and limits used".
The tool is a checklist rather than more algebra.

| The step | What you must name |
| :--- | :--- |
| line 1 | the definition of the derivative as a limit |
| line 2 | $\cot t=\cos t/\sin t$ |
| line 3 | the sine subtraction formula, **and** that sine is odd |
| line 4 | that the limit splits because both factors have limits |
| line 5 | $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$, by name |
| line 5 | the continuity of $\sin$, giving $\sin(x+h)\to\sin x$ |
| line 6 | $\csc x=1/\sin x$, and the domain $x\neq k\pi$ |

Seven namings for six marks.
If a reworded version asks you to justify, write one clause per line of the derivation and you have covered it.

### Family 9 - the definition read backwards

The trigger is a limit that looks unrelated to derivatives.
The tool is to recognise the pattern $\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ and read off $f$ and $a$, then differentiate by rule, because here the rules **are** allowed.

| Limit | It is | Value |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cot(\frac\pi6+h)-\sqrt3}{h}$ | $\cot'(\pi/6)$, since $\cot\frac\pi6=\sqrt3$ | $-4$ |
| $\lim_{x\to\pi/4}\frac{\cot x-1}{x-\frac\pi4}$ | $\cot'(\pi/4)$, since $\cot\frac\pi4=1$ | $-2$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\tan h}{h}$ | $\tan'(0)=\sec^2 0$ | $1$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sqrt{9+h}-3}{h}$ | the derivative of $\sqrt x$ at $9$ | $\frac16$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(2+h)^{10}-2^{10}}{h}$ | the derivative of $x^{10}$ at $2$, so $10\cdot2^9$ | $5120$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\cos(\frac\pi3+h)-\frac12}{h}$ | $\cos'(\pi/3)=-\sin\frac\pi3$ | $-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sec h-1}{h}$ | $\sec'(0)=\sec 0\tan 0$ | $0$ |

The recognition cue is always the same: **a subtraction on top, a lone $h$ or $(x-a)$ on the bottom, and the subtracted constant equal to the function's value at the base point.**
Check that last condition before you commit; if the constant is not $f(a)$, it is not a derivative and you have a different limit on your hands.
All seven verified.

This family is why Route D is worth knowing.
Row two is written in the $x\to a$ form, and trying to force it into the $h$ form wastes a minute.

### Family 10 - the second derivative

The trigger is a double prime, or $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$, which is exactly how Midterm 2 item 9(a) phrases it.
The tool is to differentiate $-\csc^2x$ once more, and here the chain and product rules are allowed, because only the *first* derivative was demanded from the definition.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(-\csc^2x\right)=-2\csc x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}\csc x=-2\csc x\cdot(-\csc x\cot x)=2\csc^2x\cot x$$

| Asked | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\cot''(x)$ | $2\csc^2x\cot x$ |
| $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\cot(2x)$ | $8\csc^2(2x)\cot(2x)$ |

The factor $8$ in the second row is $2^3$: one factor of 2 from each of the three derivatives-of-the-inside that the two differentiations produce, times the $2$ already in $-2\csc^2$.
Both verified numerically with a second-difference quotient at three and two values of $x$ respectively.

Notice the sign behaviour: $\cot''$ has the sign of $\cot x$, so it is positive on the first half of each branch and negative on the second, which puts an inflection point at every $x=\frac\pi2+k\pi$.
That is the shape you would need for a curve-sketching version of this question.

### Family 11 - where the derivative does not exist

The trigger is a question about existence, or about the domain of the answer.
The tool is to look at where the *function* is defined before you look at the derivative.

$\cot x=\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}$ is undefined wherever $\sin x=0$, that is at $x=k\pi$.
A function that is not defined at a point cannot be differentiable there, so $\cot'$ does not exist at $x=k\pi$ either, and the formula $-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}$ agrees by blowing up.

| Statement | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\cot$ has a derivative everywhere | **false**; not at $x=k\pi$ |
| $\cot$ has a **corner** at $x=0$ | **false**; it has a vertical asymptote, which is a different failure |
| $\cot'$ is negative wherever it exists | **true**, and it is the shape check in section 6 |
| $\cot'$ is bounded | **false**; $-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}\to-\infty$ as $x\to k\pi$ |
| $|\cot'|\ge1$ everywhere | **true**, since $\sin^2x\le1$ |
| $\cot$ is decreasing on $(0,\pi)$ | **true**, and it follows directly from $\cot'<0$ |

The distinction in row two is worth holding.
A **corner**, like $|x|$ at $0$, has a function value and two different one-sided slopes.
A **pole**, like $\cot$ at $0$, has no function value at all.
They are both places where the derivative fails to exist, and they need completely different sentences to explain why.
Q6.1 Family 12 covers the corner case; this is the pole case, and the two together are the full picture.

Verified: $-1/\sin^2x$ exceeds $10^7$ within $10^{-4}$ of $\pi$, $2\pi$ and $3\pi$, and $\sin(k\pi)=0$ to machine precision for five values of $k$.

### Family 12 - the answer written some other way

The trigger is that Möbius, or a marker, wrote the answer in a form you did not expect.
The tool is $\csc=1/\sin$ and $1+\cot^2=\csc^2$.

| Form | Same as | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $-\csc^2x$ | the standard form | what a textbook prints |
| $-\dfrac{1}{\sin^2x}$ | identical | what the derivation actually produces |
| $-\left(1+\cot^2x\right)$ | identical | what Route C produces |
| $-\dfrac{\sin^2x+\cos^2x}{\sin^2x}$ | identical | what the quotient rule produces before simplifying |
| $\dfrac{-1}{1-\cos^2 x}$ | identical | if the marker used the Pythagorean identity downward |

All three of the main spellings were verified as equal at all thirteen sample values of $x$.
There is no "more correct" one; on Möbius they are all accepted, and on paper the one you should write is $-\csc^2x$ with $-\frac{1}{\sin^2x}$ beside it.

### Family 13 - checking a from-the-definition answer numerically, and why it is not a proof

The trigger is the temptation to verify your algebra with a calculator.
The tool is a difference quotient used as an **estimator**, which is a genuinely different job from the one it does in the definition, and it behaves in three ways that are worth knowing.

Q6.1 Family 5 covers this from the other side, where the difference quotient is being *read as a rate*.
Here it is being used to check a *proof*, and the same three facts bite for a new reason.

Take $f=\cot$ at $x=1$, where the true derivative is $-\csc^2(1)=-1.4122829274$.

**First: the raw forward quotient converges, but slowly.**

| $h$ | forward error | ratio to the next row |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $10^{-2}$ | $8.964\times10^{-3}$ | $1.9885$ |
| $5\times10^{-3}$ | $4.508\times10^{-3}$ | $1.9942$ |
| $2.5\times10^{-3}$ | $2.260\times10^{-3}$ | $1.9971$ |
| $1.25\times10^{-3}$ | $1.132\times10^{-3}$ | $1.9985$ |
| $6.25\times10^{-4}$ | $5.663\times10^{-4}$ | |

Halving $h$ halves the error, so the forward quotient is **first order**.
Do the same with the centred quotient $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$ and the errors go $1.053\times10^{-4}$, $2.633\times10^{-5}$, $6.581\times10^{-6}$, $1.645\times10^{-6}$, $4.113\times10^{-7}$: every ratio is $4.000$, so it is **second order**.

**The order is the claim, and it needs a ladder.**
A single value proves nothing about a method, so both statements above were made over a five-rung ladder chosen inside the asymptotic regime, and both were tested against a seeded control that swaps the two methods and correctly fails.

The leading error term for the forward quotient is $\frac h2 f''(x)$, and for $\cot$, $f''(x)=2\csc^2x\cot x$, which at $x=1$ is $1.8136328786$.
Predicted against observed, the ratio along the ladder is $0.9885$, $0.9942$, $0.9971$, $0.9986$, $0.9993$: converging to 1, as an asymptotic statement should.
It is **not** accurate to 1 percent at $h=10^{-2}$, and an earlier version of this file's own check asserted that it was and failed; see section 12.

**Second: smaller $h$ is not better past a point.**
The centred error at $h=10^{-6}$ is $2.77\times10^{-13}$.
At $h=10^{-12}$ it is $3.18\times10^{-5}$, which is **a hundred million times worse**.
Subtracting two nearly equal numbers and dividing by a tiny one loses more to floating-point rounding than it gains in truncation.
So a numerical check has a sweet spot, and driving $h$ to zero walks straight past it.

**Third, and this is the point for Q7.1: you need $h$ below $4.9\times10^{-4}$ before the forward quotient gives you even three correct significant figures.**
Someone "checking" their derivation by trying $h=0.01$ would see $-1.4033$ against a true $-1.4123$ and could not tell a correct answer from one that was wrong in the third digit.
The arithmetic is not a substitute for the algebra, and on this question the algebra is what is being marked anyway.

### Family 14 - using the result

The trigger is that $\cot'$ appears as an ingredient rather than as the answer.
The tool is the ordinary machinery, now that the derivative is established.

| Asked | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| the tangent line to $y=\cot x$ at $x=\pi/4$ | $y-1=-2\left(x-\frac\pi4\right)$ |
| where is $y=\cot x$ decreasing? | on every branch $(k\pi,(k+1)\pi)$, since $\cot'<0$ everywhere |
| critical points of $\cot$ | none: $\cot'$ is never zero and never $0/0$ |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\cot x\right)$ | $\cot x-x\csc^2x$, by the product rule |
| $\frac{d}{dx}\cot(x^2)$ | $-2x\csc^2(x^2)$, by the chain rule |
| the slope of $\cot$ at its steepest | unbounded; it steepens without limit near $x=k\pi$ |

The third row is a nice one to have thought about.
$\cot$ has **no** critical points anywhere, because $-\csc^2x$ is never zero, so any curve-sketching question about $\cot$ is entirely about its asymptotes and its inflection points, never about maxima and minima.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks.

1. **Using the quotient rule.** It gets the right answer and it answers a question that was not asked. This is the number one way to score zero on this question while writing correct mathematics.
2. **Quoting $-\csc^2x$ with no working.** Same failure, in a shorter form.
3. **The direction of the subtraction, which flips the sign.** You need $\sin\big(x-(x+h)\big)$, not $\sin\big((x+h)-x\big)$. Get it backwards and you produce $+\csc^2x$, which is wrong by a sign and looks completely plausible on the page. The guard is the shape check: $\cot$ decreases, so its derivative is negative.
4. **Saying "cancel the $h$".** Nothing cancels in a trig case. The $h$ pairs with $\sin h$ and is removed by a limit, and writing "cancel" tells the marker you have applied a polynomial habit to a trigonometric problem. The course's own review notes give exactly this advice; section 12 records it.
5. **Never naming the identity.** Letting $-\sin h$ appear without saying where it came from throws away the two marks that step is worth.
6. **Never citing $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\sin h}{h}=1$.** It is the only non-algebraic fact in the whole proof, and it has to be named.
7. **Losing the minus sign inside the power on entry.** `(-csc(x))^2` is $+\csc^2x$. Write it as `-(csc(x))^2`.
8. **Dropping the domain.** $-\csc^2x$ holds only where $\sin x\neq0$. It is one clause and it completes the answer.
9. **Splitting the limit without saying why it is allowed.** The limit of a product is the product of the limits only when both exist, and one clause covers it.
10. **Reaching for $\frac{\cos h-1}{h}\to0$ on this question.** It is a correct fact and it is not needed here, on either route. Using it is not wrong, but it signals that you ground through Route B without noticing the cancellation.
11. **Confusing the pole at $x=k\pi$ with a corner.** They are different failures of differentiability and they need different explanations.
12. **Trying to check the derivation with a calculator.** A numerical value checks the answer, not the method, and at any plausible $h$ it does not even check the answer to three digits.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
"Use the DEFINITION to find the derivative of f."
|
+-- FIRST, before anything: write
|      f'(x) = lim (h->0) [ f(x+h) - f(x) ] / h
|    with your f substituted in.  That is a mark, and it is free.
|    If the symbol lim never appears in your work, you did not use
|    the definition, whatever else you wrote.
|
+-- What SHAPE is f?  This picks the opening move.
|     POLYNOMIAL   -> expand f(x+h), cancel h, substitute h = 0.
|     A QUOTIENT   -> put it over ONE denominator.
|     A ROOT       -> multiply top and bottom by the CONJUGATE.
|     TRIGONOMETRIC-> an IDENTITY.  h will NOT cancel.
|
+-- Trigonometric?  Which identity is decided by which function:
|     sin, cos          -> ADDITION formula.  You will need BOTH
|                          standard limits, because a cos h survives.
|     tan, cot          -> ONE DENOMINATOR, then SINE SUBTRACTION.
|                          Numerator becomes +sin h (tan) or -sin h (cot).
|                          You need only sin(h)/h -> 1.
|     sec, csc          -> ONE DENOMINATOR, then SUM-TO-PRODUCT.
|                          Numerator carries sin(h/2).
|
+-- For cot, the four lines that matter:
|     1. cot(x+h) - cot x  over  sin(x+h) sin x
|     2. numerator = sin( x - (x+h) ) = sin(-h) = -sin h
|        (the SUBTRACTION runs x MINUS x+h.  Backwards flips the sign.)
|     3. split:  -(sin h / h) * 1/( sin(x+h) sin x )
|     4. limit:  -(1) * 1/sin^2 x  =  -csc^2 x,  for x != k*pi
|
+-- Before you stop, NAME three things.  Two marks live here.
|     the identity you used;  the standard limit you used;
|     why the limit splits (both factors have limits).
|
+-- CHECK, in this order:
      SIGN      cot DECREASES on every branch, so cot' < 0 everywhere.
      NUMERATOR after the identity it must contain NO x.
      SIZE      |cot'| >= 1 always; the largest value it takes is -1.
      POINT     cot'(pi/4) = -2.  A whole number, easy to hold.
      DOMAIN    say "for x != k*pi".  One clause, and it finishes the answer.
      TYPE IT   -1/(sin(x))^2, minus OUTSIDE the square.  Then PREVIEW.
```
