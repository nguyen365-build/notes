## 0. The 30-second version

```
f(x) = x^4 + 2x - 5        f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2

  x_{n+1} = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)

  1. CHECK the bracket:  f(1) = -2 < 0 < 15 = f(2).     Root is inside.
  2. START at the midpoint x0 = 1.5  (or narrow first: f(1.2)<0<f(1.3)).
  3. ITERATE, carrying FULL precision, never rounding on the way.
  4. STOP when two successive iterates agree to the digits you must report.

  x0 = 1.5 -> 1.3024194 -> 1.2579188 -> 1.2559413      ANSWER 1.2559
```

## 1. What this question is really testing

Newton's method is not a new idea.
It is the Q12 tangent-line approximation, applied over and over, each time to a fresh anchor.

| The reflex | Where it comes from, where it returns |
| :--- | :--- |
| Differentiate a polynomial correctly | Category 8, and every wrong $f'$ here poisons every iterate |
| Solve a linearization for its own root | Category 12 - Q12.1's $\sqrt{9.2}$ estimate IS one Newton step |
| Read a sign change as an existence proof | The Intermediate Value Theorem, used here as a bracket |
| Judge what a numerical answer is worth | Q12.3's four-decimal finding, sharpened |
| Know when a method breaks | Only this question asks it - Q12 has no analogue |

**The one idea.** Q12 asked what a single linearization is worth.
Q13 asks what happens when you feed the answer back in.
The answer is that the error stops being $\tfrac{|f''|}{2}(\Delta x)^2$ once and starts being squared every step, which is why a first-order method that could not honestly deliver four decimal places becomes one that can.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

**(a) The formula, and where it comes from.**

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$$

Do not memorise it as a rule.
Derive it in four seconds: the tangent at $x_n$ is $y = f(x_n) + f'(x_n)(x - x_n)$, set $y = 0$, solve for $x$.
That derivation is also the whole of why the method works, and it is the sentence that earns the method mark.

**(b) This question's two ingredients.**

| Quantity | Value | Checked |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(x)$ | $x^4 + 2x - 5$ | given |
| $f'(x)$ | $4x^3 + 2$ | complex-step at seven points |
| $f(1)$ | $-2$ | exact |
| $f(2)$ | $15$ | exact |
| $f'(1)$ | $6$ | exact |
| $f'(2)$ | $34$ | exact |

**(c) The closed form of the iteration.**
Clearing the fraction once, before you start, saves arithmetic on every step:

$$x_{n+1} \;=\; \frac{3x_n^4 + 5}{4x_n^3 + 2}$$

This was verified as an exact rational identity at seven points.
It is worth deriving on the exam because it turns two evaluations plus a subtraction into one fraction.

**(d) The bracket is an existence proof, not decoration.**
$f(1) = -2$ and $f(2) = 15$ have opposite signs and $f$ is continuous, so the IVT puts a root strictly inside.
The stem hands you that bracket, which means the examiner has already done the hardest part of a real root-finding problem for you.

**(e) The shape of this particular $f$.**
$f''(x) = 12x^2 \ge 0$ everywhere, so $f$ is **convex on the whole real line**.
$f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2$ is at least 6 on $[1,2]$, so the function is steep and increasing across the bracket.
Both facts are what make this question safe, and knowing them is what lets you say so.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Write $f(x) = 0$.** If the equation is given as $g(x) = h(x)$, set $f = g - h$ first.
> 2. **Differentiate.** Get $f'$ and check it, because every later step inherits it.
> 3. **Bracket.** Evaluate $f$ at the two endpoints and confirm the signs differ.
> 4. **Choose $x_0$.** The midpoint always works; one extra sign check usually beats it.
> 5. **Iterate at full precision.** Never round an intermediate iterate.
> 6. **Stop** when two successive iterates agree to the digits you must report, plus one.
> 7. **Check** by substituting back: $f(x_n)$ should be tiny compared with $f$ at the endpoints.

Which situation are you in?

| The stem says | You are in | What changes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "in the interval $[a,b]$" | the standard case | the bracket is given; use its midpoint |
| nothing about an interval | Family 1 | sketch or sign-test to find your own bracket |
| "$g(x) = h(x)$" | Family 2 or 5 | subtract first, then proceed |
| "approximate $\sqrt[n]{a}$" | Family 3 | build $f(x) = x^n - a$ yourself |
| "carry out two iterations" | Family 6 | the answer is $x_2$, not the root |
| "explain why the method fails" | Family 7 | the answer is a sentence, not a number |

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Five points, no printed marking scheme anywhere in the four course files, so what follows is inference about where marks sit rather than a quoted rubric.

> Let $f(x) = x^4 + 2x - 5$, so $f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2$.
> $f(1) = -2 < 0$ and $f(2) = 15 > 0$, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem $f$ has a root in $(1,2)$.
> Newton's method sets $x_{n+1} = x_n - \dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)} = \dfrac{3x_n^4 + 5}{4x_n^3 + 2}$.
> Taking $x_0 = 1.5$:
> $x_1 = 1.302419$, $x_2 = 1.257919$, $x_3 = 1.255941$.
> Since $x_2$ and $x_3$ agree to four decimal places, the root is $x \approx 1.2559$.

Five sentences.
The first two are the setup, the third is the method, the fourth is the work, and the fifth is the justification for stopping.
Note the last sentence: it says *why* you stopped, which is the only part of a numerical answer that can be argued with.

## 6. Verify before you commit

**The substitution check.** $f(1.2559412852932328) = 3.7089 \times 10^{-5}$.
Compare that with $f(1) = -2$ and $f(2) = 15$ and the smallness is convincing.

**The magnitude check.** The root must lie in $[1,2]$ and $f$ is increasing, so any answer outside the bracket is wrong on sight.
Since $f(1.25) = 2.4414 - 5 + 2.5 < 0$ and $f(1.3) > 0$, the root is in the 1.25 to 1.3 range before you compute anything.

**The honest error estimate.** After you have computed $x_{n+1}$, the error in $x_n$ is almost exactly $|x_{n+1} - x_n|$.
Measured on Route A: that estimate is 99.81 percent accurate at $x_2$ and 99.9996 percent at $x_3$.

**What the step size does NOT tell you.** The same quantity $|x_{n+1} - x_n|$ over-states the error at the *new* iterate by a factor that grows without limit.

| after | $|x_n - x_{n-1}|$ over-states err$(x_n)$ by |
| :--- | ---: |
| $x_2$ | 22.5x |
| $x_3$ | 529x |
| $x_4$ | 280,593x |
| $x_5$ | 7.87e10 |

That is a safe bound and a useless one.
Use the step to grade the *previous* iterate, and use $|f(x_n)|/|f'(x_n)|$ - the size of the step you are about to take - to grade the current one.

**Numbers worth memorising.**

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| the root | 1.2559375481679234 |
| to four places | 1.2559 |
| the other real root | -1.7027063709099214 |
| $f'(r)$ | 9.92435868262291 |
| the asymptotic constant $f''(r)/2f'(r)$ | 0.9536409406443394 |

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as *structure*, not as a list of facts.
Every family below is the same three moves - write $f = 0$, differentiate, iterate - and what changes between them is only **where $f$ comes from** and **what can go wrong**.

### Family 0 - a polynomial root inside a stated bracket

**Trigger.** A polynomial equation plus an interval.
**Tool.** Straight Newton from the midpoint.

| equation | bracket | root (4dp) | steps from the midpoint |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $x^4 + 2x - 5 = 0$ | $[1,2]$ | **1.2559** | 3 |
| $x^3 - x - 1 = 0$ | $[1,2]$ | 1.3247 | 3 |
| $x^5 + x - 1 = 0$ | $[0,1]$ | 0.7549 | 4 |
| $x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$ | $[2,3]$ | 2.0946 | 3 |
| $x^4 - x - 10 = 0$ | $[1,2]$ | 1.8556 | 4 |
| $x^3 + x^2 - 3 = 0$ | $[1,2]$ | 1.1746 | 3 |

Six problems, six different polynomials, and **not one needs more than four steps** - four of the six need only three.
That is the family's real content: from a bracket midpoint this method is done in three or four steps essentially always, so if you are on step six you have made an arithmetic error, not a bad choice.

$x^3 - 2x - 5 = 0$ is worth recognising: it is the equation Newton himself used, and 2.0946 is a number examiners like.

### Family 1 - choosing $x_0$

**Trigger.** A wide bracket, or none at all.
**Tool.** One or two sign tests before any calculus.

| $x_0$ | steps to 1.2559 |
| ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 4 |
| 1.2 | 2 |
| 1.25 | 2 |
| 1.3 | 2 |
| 1.5 | 3 |
| 2 | **5** |

The spread between the best and worst legal start is **three steps** on a question that only needs three.
Only two distinct answers appear in the middle of that table, which tells you the payoff saturates: getting inside $[1.2, 1.3]$ is worth two steps, and getting closer than that is worth nothing.

**When no bracket is given**, find one the cheap way: evaluate $f$ at consecutive integers until the sign flips.
That is arithmetic, not calculus, and it takes seconds.

### Family 2 - a transcendental equation

**Trigger.** $\cos x = x$, $xe^x = 1$, $\ln x = 1/x$, $e^x = 3x$.
**Tool.** Subtract to make $f$, then proceed unchanged.

| equation | $f(x)$ | bracket | root (4dp) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\cos x = x$ | $\cos x - x$ | $[0,1]$ | 0.7391 |
| $xe^x = 1$ | $xe^x - 1$ | $[0,1]$ | 0.5671 |
| $\ln x = 1/x$ | $\ln x - 1/x$ | $[1,2]$ | 1.7632 |
| $e^x = 3x$ | $e^x - 3x$ | $[0,1]$ | 0.6191 |
| $\sin x = x/2$ | $\sin x - x/2$ | $[1,3]$ | 1.8955 |
| $x = \cos^2 x$ | $x - \cos^2 x$ | $[0,1]$ | 0.6417 |

Every one reaches four decimals within four steps, exactly like the polynomials.
The differentiation is the only new work.

**Two traps live in this family and nowhere else.**
First, $\sin x = x/2$ has $x = 0$ as a root as well, and Newton started at 0.5 collapses onto it: **rewriting as $f = 0$ can hand you a root the question did not want.**
Second, $e^x = 3x$ has a *second* intersection at 1.5121, so the bracket is not optional here even though it looks like decoration.

### Family 3 - a number defined as a root

**Trigger.** "Approximate $\sqrt{5}$", "estimate $\sqrt[3]{7}$".
**Tool.** You build $f$ yourself: $f(x) = x^n - a$.

Newton on $f(x) = x^2 - a$ simplifies beautifully:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n - \frac{x_n^2 - a}{2x_n} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_n + \frac{a}{x_n}\right)$$

which is the Babylonian method, two thousand years older than Newton.

| target | $x_0$ | the exact iterates |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $\sqrt2$ | 1 | $1,\; 3/2,\; 17/12,\; 577/408$ |
| $\sqrt5$ | 2 | $2,\; 9/4,\; 161/72,\; 51841/23184$ |
| $\sqrt{10}$ | 3 | $3,\; 19/6,\; 721/228,\; 1039681/328776$ |
| $\sqrt[3]{7}$ | 2 | $2,\; 23/12,\; 18215/9522$ |

Note how few distinct magnitudes appear: every third iterate is already correct to five or six figures, and the fourth is beyond what any exam asks.

**This family is where category 12 and category 13 meet, and the meeting is exact.**
Q12.1 estimated $\sqrt{9.2}$ from $a = 9$ with differentials and got $91/30$.
Newton on $x^2 - 9.2$ from $x_0 = 3$ gives $\dfrac{1}{2}\left(3 + \dfrac{9.2}{3}\right) = \dfrac{91}{30}$.

**The same rational number, by two routes that look nothing alike.**
That is not a coincidence and it is not approximate: solving the tangent line for its own root *is* one Newton step, so Q12 is Newton's method stopped after one iteration.
The second Babylonian step gives $16561/5460$, which is $\sqrt{9.2}$ to 5.5e-09, against Q12.1's error of 1.83e-04.
One more step buys four orders of magnitude, and that single comparison is the best argument for iterating that this course contains.

### Family 4 - a reciprocal with no division

**Trigger.** "Compute $1/a$ using only multiplication and subtraction."
**Tool.** $f(x) = 1/x - a$, which gives $x_{n+1} = x_n(2 - a x_n)$.

| target | $x_0$ | iterates |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| $1/7$ | 0.1 | 0.13, 0.1417, 0.14284777, 0.1428571422 |
| $1/3$ | 0.3 | 0.33, 0.3333, 0.33333333, 0.3333333333 |
| $1/13$ | 0.07 | 0.0763, 0.07691803, 0.0769230766, 0.0769230769 |

Look at the pattern in the middle column: each step roughly doubles the run of correct digits, visible to the naked eye.
This is how computers actually divide.

**This family has a convergence window and the others do not.** It converges only for $0 < x_0 < 2/a$.
From $x_0 = 0.5$ with $a = 7$ it diverges immediately, and $x_0 = 2/a$ exactly maps to 0 and sticks there.

### Family 5 - the intersection of two curves

**Trigger.** "Where does $y = e^x$ meet $y = 3x$?"
**Tool.** Identical to Family 2 - subtract, then find a root.

| curves | abscissa (4dp) |
| :--- | :--- |
| $x^3$ and $x + 1$ | 1.3247 |
| $e^x$ and $3x$ | 0.6191 |
| $\cos x$ and $x$ | 0.7391 |
| $x^2$ and $\cos x$ | 0.8241 |

The only new thing is remembering to report the $y$ value too if the question asks for the *point*.

### Family 6 - "carry out exactly $N$ iterations"

**Trigger.** "Find $x_2$", "perform two iterations of Newton's method".
**Tool.** The same iteration, stopped early and reported honestly.

| $x_0$ | $x_1$ | $x_2$ |
| ---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $4/3 = 1.3333333333$ | $391/310 = 1.2612903226$ |
| 1.5 | $323/248 = 1.3024193548$ | 1.2579188022 |
| 2 | $53/34 = 1.5588235294$ | 1.3243105219 |

**Those three values of $x_2$ disagree in the second decimal place.**
So "give $x_2$" has no answer at all without the stem's $x_0$, and copying a remembered iterate from a different starting point is a guaranteed zero.
Read the stem for $x_0$ before you compute anything.

### Family 7 - diagnose a failure

**Trigger.** "Explain why Newton's method fails for ...".
**Tool.** Name which of the four modes it is.

| mode | exhibit | what happens |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **flat $f'$** | $x^4+2x-5$ from $-0.79$ | $f' = 0.0278$, one step lands at 221.5 |
| **wrong basin** | $x^4+2x-5$ from $-0.8$ | converges to $-1.7027$, the other root |
| **a cycle** | $x^3-2x+2$ from 0 | $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$ for ever |
| **divergence** | $f(x) = x^{1/3}$ | $N(x) = -2x$, so $|x_n|$ doubles and the sign flips |

The cycle exhibit is exact, not approximate: $N(0) = 1$ and $N(1) = 0$ in exact rationals, and the real root at $-1.7693$ is never approached.
Sections 9 and 10 give the mechanism for each.

### Family 8 - a multiple root

**Trigger.** $f$ and $f'$ vanish together, e.g. $f(x) = (x-1)^2(x+3)$.
**Tool.** The same iteration, but the quadratic-convergence promise is void.

| multiplicity $m$ | measured error ratio $e_{n+1}/e_n$ | $1 - 1/m$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 0.50000000 | 0.5 |
| 3 | 0.66666667 | 0.6666667 |
| 4 | 0.75000000 | 0.75 |
| 5 | 0.80000000 | 0.8 |

Convergence drops from quadratic to **linear**, at rate exactly $1 - 1/m$.
Four decimal places then takes about fourteen steps instead of three.

**This is the sharpest caveat in the whole topic.**
"Correct digits double each step" is true only when $f'(r) \ne 0$.
Here $f'(r) = 9.92$, comfortably nonzero, which is *why* the headline question behaves.
Say that when you claim quadratic convergence, and the claim becomes a statement instead of a slogan.

### Family 9 - how many steps for a stated accuracy

**Trigger.** "How many iterations are needed for six decimal places?"
**Tool.** Count digits, do not count iterations.

Correct decimal digits along Route A: 0.61, 1.33, 2.70, 5.43, 10.88, 21.77.

Each entry is **more than double** its predecessor.
So the rule is: find how many correct digits $x_0$ has, then double until you pass your target.
From 0.61 digits, four decimal places needs three steps and six needs four.

The doubling is not magic, it is the constant $C = f''(r)/2f'(r) = 0.9536$: since $e_{n+1} \approx C e_n^2$ and $C$ is close to 1, the exponent simply doubles.
When $C$ is large the doubling starts later; when $f'(r) = 0$ it never starts at all, which is Family 8.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks.

**1. Rounding the intermediate iterates.**
This is the most expensive habit in the topic and it looks like carefulness.
Rounding every iterate to $k$ decimals caps the accuracy you can ever reach, no matter how many steps you take:

| rounded to | the chain stalls at | final error |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| 2dp | 1.26 | 4.06e-03 |
| 3dp | 1.256 | 6.25e-05 |
| 4dp | 1.2559 | 3.75e-05 |
| 5dp | 1.25594 | 2.45e-06 |
| 6dp | 1.255938 | 4.52e-07 |
| full precision, $x_4$ | 1.2559375489 | **7.06e-10** |

Quadratic convergence dies the moment you round, because the method can only square an error it is actually given.
On this question the 4dp-rounded chain does reach 1.2559 - but its residual error of 3.75e-05 is **three times** the 1.245e-05 margin to the nearest rounding boundary, so it survived by luck, not by design.

**2. Stopping at the wrong iterate, and the 1.2560 trap.**
The root is $1.25593754\ldots$, which sits only **1.245e-05** below the four-decimal rounding boundary at 1.25595.
So an iterate approaching from above must beat 1.245e-05, not the 5e-05 that "four decimal places" suggests.

Route B's $x_3 = 1.2559647$ has an error of 2.72e-05.
That is inside 5e-05 and it still rounds to **1.2560**, which is wrong.
**Do not infer a digit count from the size of an error.**
This is the same lesson Q12.3 found on $\sqrt{16.4}$, arriving here from the other direction.

**3. Reporting more digits than the iteration has earned.**
$x_2$ on Route A is 1.2579188021959602 as a float, and exactly two of those digits are correct.
Writing 1.25792 claims five.
The defensible rule: report only the digits that two successive iterates agree on.

**4. Getting $f'$ wrong.**
Every iterate inherits it, so a slip here is not a small error, it is a different method converging to a different place.
Check $f'$ once, at a point where the arithmetic is easy, before you start iterating.

**5. Believing the "iterate again and it does not move" check.**
It does not work, and this is the deepest trap in the topic.
Eight plausible corruptions of Newton's formula were tested at the true root:

| corruption | fixes the true root? |
| :--- | :--- |
| $x + f/f'$ (sign flipped) | **yes** |
| $x - f/f''$ | **yes** |
| $f' = 4x^3$ (lost the $+2$) | **yes** |
| $f' = 4x^4+2$ (power not dropped) | **yes** |
| $x - f\cdot f'$ | **yes** |
| $x - f(x)$ (forgot to divide) | **yes** |
| $x - f'/f$ | no |
| $f/f'$ (dropped the $x$) | no |

**Six of the eight leave the true root exactly where it is.**
The mechanism is simple once you see it: $f(r) = 0$, so any correction term that *multiplies* $f$ vanishes at the root regardless of what else is wrong with it.
The two survivors are precisely the two that do not multiply $f$.

So the root is a fixed point of almost any corrupted iteration, and "plug the answer back into my formula and see if it moves" cannot detect a wrong formula.
**Substitute into $f$, not into $N$.**
This is category 13's version of Q12's zero-increment blind spot and Q11's zero-rate blind spot, and it is worse than both, because the blind spot sits *at the answer* rather than at some degenerate input you could avoid.

**6. Choosing $x_0$ carelessly.**
Costs up to two steps here and can cost the whole question in Family 7.

**7. Answering the wrong question.**
"Two iterations" means report $x_2$. "Approximate the solution" means report the root.

**8. The factor-equals-one hiding point.**
Writing $f' = 4x^4 + 2$ instead of $4x^3 + 2$ is bit-identical to the truth at $x = 1$, because $x^4 = x^3$ there.
That is the queue's recurring rule again: a wrong answer hides wherever the factor it corrupts equals 1.
At $x_0 = 1.5$ and at $x_0 = 2$ **none** of the eight corruptions hides, so either of those is a safe place to sanity-check your formula.

## 10. Why this question cannot go wrong, and when the method can

The three failure modes are real, and none of them can happen here.
Being able to say *why* is what a "explain when Newton's method fails" question wants.

### Mode 1: a flat $f'(x_n)$

The iterate is thrown a distance $f(x_n)/f'(x_n)$, so a small denominator means a large jump.

| $x_0$ | $f'(x_0)$ | $x_1$ |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $-0.79$ | 0.0278 | 221.54 |
| $-0.7937005$ | 1.964e-07 | 3.15e+07 |
| $-0.7937005259840997$ | 4.44e-16 | 1.39e+16 |

The damage scales as $1/f'$: cut $f'$ by a factor and the jump grows by the same factor.

**Why it cannot happen here.** $f'(x) = 4x^3 + 2$ has exactly one zero, at $x_c = -\sqrt[3]{1/2} = -0.7937005259840998$.
On $[1,2]$, $f'$ never falls below 6.
The stem's interval is not just a hint about where the root is, it is a guarantee that the denominator stays healthy.

### Mode 2: converging to a different root

$f$ has a second real root at $-1.7027063709099214$.
Scanning starting points across $[-20, 20]$ finds **exactly one basin boundary**, and it is $x_c$ itself.

- every $x_0 > -0.7937005259840998$ converges to **1.2559**
- every $x_0 < -0.7937005259840998$ converges to **-1.7027**

That is unusually clean.
Most functions have basins that fractal near the boundaries; this one splits the line into two intervals, because $f$ is convex everywhere and has only two real roots.
$x_0 = -0.8$ looks harmless and lands on the wrong root, which is the version of this an examiner would set.

### Mode 3: oscillation between two points

The famous failure is $f(x) = x^3 - 2x + 2$ from $x_0 = 0$, which cycles $0, 1, 0, 1, \dots$ exactly and never approaches the real root at $-1.7693$.

**For $x^4 + 2x - 5$ this cannot happen, and that is provable rather than merely unobserved.**
Setting $N(N(x)) = x$ and clearing denominators gives a polynomial of degree 16.
Every root of $f$ is a fixed point of $N$, so $f$ divides it exactly, leaving a cofactor of degree 12.
**All twelve roots of that cofactor are complex**, in six conjugate pairs, so there is no real 2-cycle at all.

The same machinery run on $x^3 - 2x + 2$ returns a degree-6 cofactor with **four** real roots, which are two genuine 2-cycles: the famous $\{0, 1\}$ and a second one at $\{0.133346,\; 1.024967\}$.
A census that can find two cycles where cycles exist, and none here, is a measurement.
A census that always returns none would be indistinguishable from a broken one.

### Mode 4: outright divergence

Not available for this $f$ either.
The standard exhibit is $f(x) = x^{1/3}$, where $N(x) = x - 3x = -2x$, so the iterate doubles and flips sign every step: $0.1, -0.2, 0.4, -0.8, 1.6, -3.2, 6.4$.

### The positive statement

$f$ is convex ($f'' = 12x^2 \ge 0$) and increasing on $[1,2]$.
For a convex increasing function, starting anywhere with $f(x_0) > 0$ gives **monotone decreasing convergence**, guaranteed.
$f(2) = 15 > 0$, so $x_0 = 2$ is the provably safe start, and the iterates do descend monotonically to the root without ever crossing it.
$f(1) = -2 < 0$, so $x_0 = 1$ fails that condition and its first step overshoots to $4/3$, past the root - after which it is monotone.

Both converge.
The theorem is what lets you promise it in advance.

## 11. What the convergence order actually is

Do not assert that Newton's method is second order. Measure it.

Taking $\log e_{n+1} / \log e_n$ along Route B:

| step | measured order |
| :--- | ---: |
| $e_2$ from $e_1$ | 2.0440 |
| $e_3$ from $e_2$ | 2.0099 |
| $e_4$ from $e_3$ | 2.0045 |
| $e_5$ from $e_4$ | 2.0023 |
| $e_6$ from $e_5$ | 2.0011 |
| $e_7$ from $e_6$ | 2.0006 |
| $e_8$ from $e_7$ | 2.0003 |

**The order is 2, approached from above, halving its excess each step.**

Two cautions that the measurement itself supplies.

**The ladder must be bounded.**
Run at 220 digits, the ninth ratio reads **1.4925** and the tenth reads **1.0000**, because the errors have hit the arithmetic's precision floor and stopped being errors at all.
An unbounded ladder reports that the method is first order.
That is the same class of defect as an unbounded Richardson ladder elsewhere in this course: outside the asymptotic regime the number means nothing.

**The constant matters as much as the order.**
$e_{n+1}/e_n^2$ converges to $0.9536409406443394$, which is exactly $f''(r)/2f'(r)$ computed independently.
Because it is close to 1, correct digits simply double.
If $f''$ were large or $f'(r)$ small, the same order-2 convergence would take several steps to get going.

**A usable consequence.**
Once you are in the regime, $\text{err}(x_{n+1}) \approx C\,|x_{n+1} - x_n|^2$.
Measured accuracy of that prediction: 99.79 percent at $x_3$, 99.9996 percent at $x_4$, and 100.0000 percent at $x_5$.
At $x_1$ it is only 80 percent, so the estimate needs one good step before it can be trusted.

## 13. The one-page decision tree

```
  An equation to solve numerically
      |
      +-- Is it already f(x) = 0 ?
      |      no  -> subtract:  g = h  becomes  f = g - h
      |      (a root you did not want may appear; keep the bracket)
      |
      +-- Is the unknown a NUMBER (sqrt, cube root, reciprocal) ?
      |      yes -> build f yourself:   x^n - a    or   1/x - a
      |             sqrt shortcut:  x <- (x + a/x)/2
      |             1/a  shortcut:  x <- x(2 - ax)      window 0 < x0 < 2/a
      |
      +-- DIFFERENTIATE, and check f' before iterating
      |
      +-- Is a bracket given ?
      |      yes -> confirm the sign change; take the MIDPOINT
      |      no  -> evaluate f at integers until the sign flips
      |             then one extra sign test buys a whole step
      |
      +-- Does f'(r) = 0  (a repeated factor) ?
      |      yes -> convergence is LINEAR at rate 1 - 1/m; expect ~14 steps
      |      no  -> correct digits DOUBLE each step; expect 3 or 4
      |
      +-- ITERATE at full precision.  Never round on the way.
      |
      +-- STOP when two successive iterates agree to your target + 1 digit
      |
      +-- CHECK by substituting into f, NEVER by re-running the iteration
      |      (six of eight wrong formulas fix the root too)
      |
      +-- ROUND once, at the end               -> 1.2559
      +-- PREVIEW the entry before submitting
```
