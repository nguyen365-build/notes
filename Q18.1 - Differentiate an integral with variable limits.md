**The question (Final Exam 1, item 11, 4 points).**

## 0. The 30-second version

```
  int from 2x to x  of f(t) dt
        |
        |  1. SPLIT at any constant c:   int_{2x}^{c} + int_{c}^{x}
        |  2. FLIP the first one:        int_{c}^{x} - int_{c}^{2x}
        |  3. FTC1 + CHAIN on each:      f(x)*(x)'  -  f(2x)*(2x)'
        |  4. SUBSTITUTE into f:         sin(x^2)*1 - sin((2x)^2)*2
        v
  sin(x^2) - 2 sin(4x^2)
```

Read step 3 as one sentence: **top limit minus bottom limit, each one plugged into $f$ and each one multiplied by its own derivative.**
That is the whole theorem for this question.
The `c` in step 1 never appears in the answer, which is the tell that the split was legitimate.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is the hinge of MATH 265.
Everything before it (Q6 through Q15) is differential calculus, everything after it (Q16, Q17, Q19) is integration, and the Fundamental Theorem is the single statement that says those two subjects are the same subject read in opposite directions.
The examiner is not asking whether you can recall a formula.
The examiner is asking whether you know **which direction of the theorem the question is pointing in**, and whether the chain rule you learned in Q8 survives being applied to a limit of integration instead of to an argument.

| the reflex this question drills | where it came from | where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_a^x f = f(x)$, the derivative undoes the integral | new here | Q19's net-change and average-value questions |
| a factor of $g'(x)$ for every non-trivial limit | Q8.1 to Q8.4, the chain rule | every remaining question with a composite |
| a minus sign for a variable *lower* limit | Q16/Q17's orientation convention $\int_a^b = -\int_b^a$ | Q19's area questions, where a swapped limit flips a sign |
| the answer is a function of $x$, not of $t$ | Q1.2's domain-and-variable discipline | every "evaluate" question in the bank |
| continuity is a hypothesis, not decoration | Q5's conditions-to-sketch questions | Q17's bounding questions |

**Four points on a 124-point paper is about 3 percent, and it is the cheapest four points in the integration half of Final 1.**
The work is two substitutions and one multiplication.
There is no algebra to get wrong after the theorem is applied, which is unusual in this bank and is exactly why the marks are lost at the *setup* rather than at the finish.
Every grading statement in this document is an **inference**: no marking scheme for MATH 265 exists anywhere on this machine, re-confirmed this run.

### The integrand is a deliberate choice, and you should notice it

$\sin\left(t^2\right)$ has **no elementary antiderivative**.
It is the Fresnel sine integral, and no combination of powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms and trigonometric functions differentiates to it.
So the route "find an antiderivative, evaluate it at $x$ and at $2x$, then differentiate the result" is not merely slow here, it is **impossible**.
That is why the examiner picked this integrand rather than, say, $t^2$.
The question is engineered so that FTC Part 1 is the only road, and a student who reaches for Part 2 has nowhere to go.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

### 2a. The two halves of the theorem, and the fact that they are different statements

| | FTC Part 1 | FTC Part 2 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **statement** | if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F(x)=\displaystyle\int_a^x f(t)\,dt$, then $F'(x)=f(x)$ on $(a,b)$ | if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F'=f$, then $\displaystyle\int_a^b f(t)\,dt = F(b)-F(a)$ |
| **input** | a function $f$ | a function $f$ and an antiderivative $F$ |
| **output** | a *function* of $x$ | a *number* |
| **what it does** | builds an antiderivative out of an integral | computes an integral out of an antiderivative |
| **the verb in the question** | "differentiate", "$\frac{d}{dx}$", "find $F'$" | "evaluate", "compute", "find the value of" |
| **needs an antiderivative?** | no | yes |
| **this question** | **yes, this one** | no |

The single most useful sentence about the pair: **Part 1 says differentiation undoes integration, Part 2 says integration undoes differentiation.**
They are inverse statements, and the question tells you which one it wants by whether a $\frac{d}{dx}$ is sitting outside the integral sign.

### 2b. Why $t$ is a dummy and the answer cannot contain it

$\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)\,dt$ is, for each fixed $x$, a **number**.
The $t$ is consumed by the integration the way the counter in a sum is consumed by the sum: $\sum_{k=1}^{5} k^2 = 55$ has no $k$ in it.
Rename $t$ to $u$, to $s$, to anything, and the number does not change.
So a $t$ in your final answer is not a small notation slip, it is a claim that the answer depends on a variable that no longer exists.

> **If you can rename it without changing the value, it cannot appear in the value.**

### 2c. The orientation convention, stated once and used forever

$$\int_a^b f(t)\,dt = -\int_b^a f(t)\,dt \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int_a^a f(t)\,dt = 0$$

Everything about the minus sign on a variable lower limit comes from this one line and nothing else.
This question's integral runs from $2x$ **down** to $x$ for $x>0$, which is why $F$ comes out negative there: $F(0.3) = -0.062341433767$, while $F(-0.3) = +0.062341433767$.

### 2d. The chain rule, in the shape it takes here

You already know $\dfrac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$ from Q8.
Here it is composed with FTC1 instead:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\left(g(x)\right)\cdot g'(x)$$

Set $G(u) = \int_a^u f(t)\,dt$, so FTC1 gives $G'(u) = f(u)$.
The integral above is $G(g(x))$, and the ordinary chain rule gives $G'(g(x))\,g'(x) = f(g(x))\,g'(x)$.
**The $g'(x)$ is not an extra rule, it is the chain rule you already had.**

### 2e. What continuity is actually doing

The hypothesis "$f$ continuous" is what makes $F$ differentiable at all.
Here is the failure, measured rather than asserted.
Take $f(t)=0$ for $t<0$ and $f(t)=1$ for $t\ge 0$, and $F(x)=\int_0^x f$.
Then $F(x)=x$ for $x\ge0$ and $F(x)=0$ for $x<0$.

| $x$ | left difference quotient | right difference quotient | verdict |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| $-0.5$ | 0.000000 | 0.000000 | $F'=f(x)$ holds |
| $0$ | 0.000000 | 1.000000 | **derivative does not exist** |
| $0.5$ | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | $F'=f(x)$ holds |

$F$ is still continuous, and it is still differentiable everywhere except one point.
**Continuity of $f$ at a point is exactly what buys you $F'=f$ at that point**, and where it fails you get a corner in $F$ instead of an equation.

For this question the screen is clean.
As $x$ ranges over $[-3,3]$ the two limits $2x$ and $x$ sweep $t$ over $[-6,6]$; sampling $\sin\left(t^2\right)$ at 200001 points across that range found **0 non-finite values** and a largest step-to-step change of $7.055\times10^{-4}$ at a sample spacing of $6.00\times10^{-5}$, consistent with a continuous function and with no jump.
$\sin\left(t^2\right)$ is a composition of two entire functions, so it is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

> **Domain of validity: the answer $\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$ holds for every real $x$, with no exclusions.**

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Read the verb.** A $\frac{d}{dx}$ outside the integral means Part 1. A bare $\int_a^b$ with numbers at both ends means Part 2. If both limits are constants and there is a $\frac{d}{dx}$ outside, the answer is $0$, because the integral is a constant.
> 2. **Check continuity of $f$ across every value the limits take.** State the interval. If $f$ blows up between the limits, stop: the integral may not exist.
> 3. **Classify by the limits** using the table below.
> 4. **Split at a constant** if both limits are variable. Any constant inside the domain works, and it cancels.
> 5. **Apply FTC1 with the chain rule to each piece:** upper gives $+f(\text{upper})\cdot(\text{upper})'$, lower gives $-f(\text{lower})\cdot(\text{lower})'$.
> 6. **Substitute the whole limit into $f$**, brackets and all. $f$ at $2x$ means $\sin\left((2x)^2\right)$, not $\sin\left(2x^2\right)$.
> 7. **Simplify only inside the arguments**, then stop. There is no further algebra.
> 8. **Check:** is the answer in $x$ alone, does every variable limit contribute a factor, and does the parity or a spot value at a non-degenerate point agree?

### The classify table

| what the limits look like | the derivative | the factor students drop |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\displaystyle\int_a^b$, both constant | $0$ | - |
| $\displaystyle\int_a^x$ | $f(x)$ | none, and this is what makes it dangerous |
| $\displaystyle\int_a^{g(x)}$ | $f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ | $g'(x)$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_{g(x)}^{b}$ | $-f(g(x))\,g'(x)$ | the minus, and $g'(x)$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)}$ | $f(v(x))\,v'(x) - f(u(x))\,u'(x)$ | the minus, and either $g'$ |
| $x\displaystyle\int_a^{x}$ | product rule first, then FTC1 | the whole first term |

**Row 5 is this question.** Memorise row 5 and rows 2 to 4 are its special cases.

## 4. Q18.1 worked four ways

Throughout, $f(t) = \sin\left(t^2\right)$, $v(x) = x$ (the upper limit) and $u(x) = 2x$ (the lower limit).

### Route A - split at zero, the one to write down

$$\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \int_{2x}^{0}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt + \int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt - \int_{0}^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$$

The first equality is additivity over a subdivision.
The second is the orientation convention from 2c applied to the first piece only.
Now each piece has a constant lower limit and FTC1 with the chain rule applies to each:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \sin\left(x^2\right)\cdot 1, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \sin\left((2x)^2\right)\cdot 2$$

$$\boxed{\;\frac{d}{dx}\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = \sin\left(x^2\right) - 2\sin\left(4x^2\right)\;}$$

### Route B - split at an arbitrary constant, to show the split is free

Replace $0$ by any constant $c$:

$$\int_{2x}^{x} f = \int_{c}^{x} f - \int_{c}^{2x} f \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{d}{dx} = f(x)\cdot 1 - f(2x)\cdot 2$$

The $c$ has vanished, because differentiating kills the constant $\int_{c}^{0}f$ that the choice of $c$ contributes to each piece.
This is worth doing once in your head, because it is the answer to "was I allowed to split at zero when the interval sometimes lies entirely to the left of zero?".
Yes: for $x<0$ the interval $[2x,x]$ sits entirely on the negative side and $0$ is outside it, and the identity still holds, because additivity of the integral does not require $c$ to be between the limits once orientation is respected.

### Route C - name the accumulation function, then use the plain chain rule

Let $G(u) = \displaystyle\int_{0}^{u}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$, so FTC1 says $G'(u)=\sin\left(u^2\right)$.
Then

$$\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt = G(x) - G(2x)$$

and this is now an ordinary Q8-style differentiation:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[G(x) - G(2x)\right] = G'(x)\cdot 1 - G'(2x)\cdot 2 = \sin\left(x^2\right) - 2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$$

**This is the route that makes the $2$ impossible to forget**, because $G(2x)$ is visibly a composite and nobody differentiates a visible composite without the chain rule.
It is also the honest description of what Routes A and B are doing.

### Route D - the two-limit formula applied directly

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(v(x))\,v'(x) - f(u(x))\,u'(x)$$

$$= \sin\left(x^2\right)\cdot 1 - \sin\left((2x)^2\right)\cdot 2 = \sin\left(x^2\right) - 2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$$

One line, if you trust the formula.

### Route comparison

| route | lines | what it costs | what it protects you from | risk |
| :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, split at $0$ | 3 | one extra line of orientation | forgetting the minus, because you *wrote* the flip | the split can look unjustified if $0$ is outside the interval |
| B, split at $c$ | 3 | same as A plus a sentence | the "was I allowed to split there" doubt | none, but it shows no extra method work |
| C, name $G$ | 3 | one definition | **both** the minus and both chain factors | none |
| D, direct formula | 1 | nothing | nothing; it is pure recall | a mis-remembered formula is undetectable |

**Under exam pressure, write Route A and check with Route D.**
Route A is what a marker recognises as "used the Fundamental Theorem", it shows the orientation step that carries the minus sign, and it costs about twenty seconds.
Route D is the eight-second confirmation.
Route C is the one to *think* in, and the one to fall back on the moment you are unsure whether a factor belongs.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Four points, and this is inference rather than a published scheme, but the sentences below are the ones that name a method rather than assert a result:

> Since $\sin\left(t^2\right)$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, split the integral at $0$:
> $$\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt-\int_{0}^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt.$$
> By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1, together with the chain rule,
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt=\sin\left(x^2\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt=2\sin\left(4x^2\right).$$
> Therefore
> $$\frac{d}{dx}\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt=\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right).$$

Three things in that write-up are doing work and should not be cut.
**"Since $\sin\left(t^2\right)$ is continuous"** discharges the theorem's hypothesis in six words.
**"together with the chain rule"** names the reason the $2$ is there, so a marker does not have to guess whether you knew.
**The explicit $\sin\left(4x^2\right)$** shows you squared the whole limit, not just the $x$.

On an answer-only Möbius paper none of that is marked.
Write it anyway on any paper that shows work, and on Möbius spend the same twenty seconds on section 6's checks instead.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The eight-second checks, in the order they catch things

1. **Is there a $t$ anywhere in my answer?** If yes, the answer is wrong on its face.
2. **Count the terms against the variable limits.** Two variable limits means two terms. One means one.
3. **Count the chain factors.** Every variable limit contributes a factor of its own derivative. Upper limit $x$ contributes $1$; lower limit $2x$ contributes $2$.
4. **Is the lower limit's term negative?** It must be, before any sign inside $f$ is considered.
5. **Parity.** $\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$ is an **even** function of $x$, verified at 2000 random points with 0 violations. That is forced: $F$ is odd (the interval $[2x,x]$ reflects to $[x,2x]$ under $x\mapsto -x$ and the integrand is even), and the derivative of an odd function is even. An answer that is not even is wrong.
6. **Spot value at a well-chosen point.** $F'(1) = \sin(1)-2\sin(4) = 2.3550759754$, and $F'(0.5) = \sin(0.25)-2\sin(1) = -1.4355380104$.

### The check point matters more than the check

This is Q8.3's result in a new dress, and Q18.1 is its sharpest instance in the whole bank.

> **A dropped chain factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1.**
> (`notes\Q8.3 - Five derivatives, nested chain and quotient rules.md`, section 6.)

Here is what that means for the *check point* you pick.
All 18 wrong answers in section 9's census were evaluated against the truth at 6001 points on $[-3,3]$:

| check point | how many of the 18 wrong answers it fails to detect |
| :--- | ---: |
| $x = 0$ | **18 of 18** |
| $x = 1$ | 2 of 18 (plus the one that hides everywhere) |
| a generic point such as $x=0.7$ or $x=1.3$ | 1 of 18 |

$x=0$ detects **nothing**, because every term of every candidate answer contains a $\sin$ of something that vanishes at $0$.
$x=1$ additionally lets through the two errors that confuse $4x^2$ with $4x$ and $g'$ with $g$, because $4x^2=4x=4$ and $2x=2$ all coincide there.

> **Check at $x=0.7$ or $x=1.3$. Never at $x=0$, and never at $x=1$.**

### Numbers worth memorising

| quantity | value |
| :--- | ---: |
| $F'(0.5)=\sin(0.25)-2\sin(1)$ | $-1.4355380104$ |
| $F'(1)=\sin(1)-2\sin(4)$ | $2.3550759754$ |
| $F'(2)=\sin(4)-2\sin(16)$ | $-0.1809958620$ |
| $F(1)=\int_2^1\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$ | $-0.4945081876$ |
| $\max\left|F'\right|$ on $[0,2]$ | $2.9261038384$ |
| smallest positive critical number of $F$ | $x = 0.8385086293$ |
| the critical number you can name exactly | $x = \sqrt{\pi} = 1.7724538509$ |

$\sqrt{\pi}$ is a critical number because $x^2=\pi$ makes $\sin\left(x^2\right)=\sin\pi=0$ and $4x^2=4\pi$ makes $\sin\left(4x^2\right)=\sin 4\pi=0$, so both terms vanish at once.
It is the one exact answer available in this question's neighbourhood and it is a plausible follow-up part.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

Both MATH 265 exams are Möbius, answer-only and machine-marked, so the keystrokes are the answer.
The A1 block of the Exam Note Sheets artifact is the authoritative source for these rules; what follows applies them to this question.

**Type:** `sin(x^2)-2*sin(4*x^2)`

| do | do not | why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `sin(x^2)` | `sin x^2` | Möbius may parse `sin x^2` as $(\sin x)^2$ |
| `4*x^2` | `4x^2` | implicit multiplication is not guaranteed to be accepted |
| `2*sin(...)` | `2sin(...)` | same reason |
| `sin(4*x^2)` | `sin(2*x)^2` | that is $(\sin 2x)^2$, a different function |
| `sin(4*x^2)` | `sin(2*x^2)` | you must square the whole limit $2x$ |
| leave it unsimplified if unsure | invent a "nicer" identity | there is no product-to-sum simplification that helps here |

**The single most expensive typo is `sin(2*x^2)` for `sin(4*x^2)`.**
It looks right, it is one character away, and it is a wrong function everywhere except the two degenerate points.
Its worst-case gap from the truth is $3.520345$, so it is caught easily by a spot check at $x=0.7$, and not at all by one at $x=0$.

**Always press preview.**
The rendered form should read $\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$, with the $x^2$ *inside* the sine and the $2$ *outside* it.

### Family 2 - variable lower limit, so a minus sign appears

**Trigger:** the varying limit is at the bottom.
**Tool:** $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{g(x)}^{b} f = -f(g(x))\,g'(x)$.

| question | answer | max gap |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_x^5 \sin\left(t^2\right)dt$ | $-\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | $1.67\times10^{-12}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{x^3}^{1} e^{-t^2}dt$ | $-3x^2 e^{-x^6}$ | $3.17\times10^{-12}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{\cos x}^{2}\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt$ | $\sin x\sqrt{1+\cos^2 x}$ | $3.81\times10^{-12}$ |

**Row 3 shows how the signs are generated and why you should never "read off" a sign.**
The minus from the lower limit and the minus from $(\cos x)' = -\sin x$ multiply to a plus.
There is exactly one minus in the *rule* and however many the derivative of $g$ supplies; do not count them, multiply them.

### Family 3 - both limits variable, which is Q18.1

**Trigger:** neither limit is a number.
**Tool:** split at a constant, then Family 1 on the top and Family 2 on the bottom.

| question | answer | max gap |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| **$\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$** | $\sin\left(x^2\right)-2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$ | $5.20\times10^{-13}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{x}^{2x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$ | $2\sin\left(4x^2\right)-\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | $5.20\times10^{-13}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{x^2}^{x^3}\cos t\,dt$ | $3x^2\cos\left(x^3\right)-2x\cos\left(x^2\right)$ | $4.81\times10^{-12}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{\sin x}^{\cos x}\sqrt{1+t^4}\,dt$ | $-\sin x\sqrt{1+\cos^4x}-\cos x\sqrt{1+\sin^4 x}$ | $5.51\times10^{-12}$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{-x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$ | $2\sin\left(x^2\right)$ | $6.89\times10^{-12}$ |

**Row 2 is row 1 negated**, and that is the entire content of swapping the limits.
**Row 5 is the one people get wrong by symmetry intuition:** the lower limit $-x$ has derivative $-1$, so its term is $-\sin\left(x^2\right)\cdot(-1) = +\sin\left(x^2\right)$, and the two terms *add*.
The number of distinct magnitudes in this family is small: each row is $\left|f(\text{top})\cdot(\text{top})'\right|$ and $\left|f(\text{bottom})\cdot(\text{bottom})'\right|$, combined with a minus.

### Family 4 - an extra factor outside the integral, so the product rule comes first

**Trigger:** something multiplies the integral.
**Tool:** product rule, and only then FTC1 on the integral factor.

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[x^2\int_0^x \cos\left(t^2\right)dt\right] = 2x\int_0^x\cos\left(t^2\right)dt + x^2\cos\left(x^2\right)$$

Verified at $x = 0.5, 1.0, 1.5$ with a worst gap of $2.53\times10^{-12}$.
**The first term still contains an integral, and that is correct, not unfinished.**
Since $\cos\left(t^2\right)$ has no elementary antiderivative either, there is nothing more to write, and an answer that "simplifies" the leftover integral away is wrong.
The same shape with $x$ instead of $x^2$ gives $\int_0^x\sin\left(t^2\right)dt + x\sin\left(x^2\right)$, checked at four points with a worst gap of $2.23\times10^{-12}$.

### Family 5 - the FTC Part 2 questions, which look similar and are not

**Trigger:** no $\frac{d}{dx}$, both limits are numbers, and the verb is "evaluate" or "find the value".
**Tool:** find an antiderivative, then $F(b)-F(a)$.

| question | antiderivative | value |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^2 t^3\,dt$ | $t^4/4$ | $4$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_1^4 \dfrac{dt}{\sqrt t}$ | $2\sqrt t$ | $2$ |
| $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos t\,dt$ | $\sin t$ | $1$ |

Each was cross-checked against tanh-sinh quadrature, worst gap $6.66\times10^{-15}$.
**The tell that a question is Part 2 and not Part 1 is that an antiderivative is findable.**
An examiner who wants Part 1 will choose $\sin\left(t^2\right)$, $e^{-t^2}$, $\sqrt{1+t^4}$, $\frac{\sin t}{t}$ or $\ln(\ln t)$ precisely because you cannot antidifferentiate them.
That is a reliable signal in this bank: **a nasty integrand plus a $\frac{d}{dx}$ means you are not supposed to integrate anything.**

### Family 6 - using $F'$ to describe $F$

**Trigger:** "where is $F$ increasing", "find the critical numbers of $F$", "find the local maxima of $F$".
**Tool:** FTC1 first to get $F'$, then all of the Q14/Q15 machinery unchanged.

For this question's own $F(x)=\int_{2x}^{x}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$, solving $F'(x)=0$ on $[0,2]$ by bisection found exactly five critical numbers:

| critical number | how it arises |
| ---: | :--- |
| $0.0000000000$ | both sine terms vanish |
| $0.8385086293$ | $\sin\left(x^2\right)=2\sin\left(4x^2\right)$ |
| $1.3040266790$ | same |
| $1.5022940314$ | same |
| $1.7724538509$ | $x=\sqrt{\pi}$; both terms vanish independently |

The root census was itself controlled: the identical machinery run on $\sin\left(x^2\right)+2$, which is never zero, returned **0 roots**.
A census that always returns something is indistinguishable from a correct one until you show it can return nothing.

### Family 7 - continuity fails, so the theorem does not apply

**Trigger:** the integrand has a pole, a jump, or an undefined point between the limits.
**Tool:** state the domain, and split the problem where the trouble is.

$\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_{2x}^{x}\dfrac{dt}{t}$ is the instructive case.
For $x>0$ the interval avoids $0$ and $\int_{2x}^{x}\frac{dt}{t} = \ln x - \ln 2x = -\ln 2$, a **constant**, so the derivative is $0$.
FTC1 with the chain rule agrees: $\frac{1}{x}\cdot 1 - \frac{1}{2x}\cdot 2 = 0$.
For $x<0$ the same thing happens on the negative side.
At $x=0$ the interval degenerates and nothing is defined.
**The theorem is not wrong away from the discontinuity; it simply says nothing at it.**
Contrast the step-function case in section 2e, where $F'$ genuinely fails to exist at the one bad point.

### Family 8 - the trivial cases, which are exam questions too

| question | answer | why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_1^7 \sin\left(t^2\right)dt$ | $0$ | the integral is a number, and a number has zero derivative |
| $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_x^x f(t)\,dt$ | $0$ | the integral is identically $0$ |
| $\dfrac{d}{dt}\displaystyle\int_a^{t} f(s)\,ds$ | $f(t)$ | the letters changed, the theorem did not |

The third row is the check that you understood 2b rather than memorised a picture.

### The four named mark-losing errors, and which of them hide

The task this document was written to answer is which of the four classic errors a numeric check can find.

| named error | detectable? | evidence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| dropping $g'(x)$ on the **lower** limit | **yes**, at 6000 of 6001 points | $g'=2$, never $1$, so the term changes by a factor of two |
| dropping $g'(x)$ on the **upper** limit | **no, it hides at every real $x$** | $g'(x)=1$ identically, so the omitted factor is $1$ and the expression is unchanged |
| forgetting the minus on the variable lower limit | **yes**, at 6000 of 6001 points | worst gap $4.000000$ |
| substituting into $f$ incorrectly | **yes**, at 6000 or 5999 of 6001 points | $\sin\left(2x^2\right)$ gap $3.520345$; $\sin(4x)$ gap $3.995332$ |
| answering in $t$ | **yes, but not numerically** | the expression is numerically identical under renaming; only reading the answer catches it |

### Why the upper limit's factor hides, and what to do about it

Q8.3 established that **a dropped chain factor is invisible exactly where that factor equals 1**, and demonstrated it at a single bad point: part (a)'s inner factor is $2x$, which equals $1$ at $x=\frac12$, so a check there passes on a wrong answer.
Q18.1 is the extreme case of the same species.
Its upper limit is $g(x)=x$, so $g'(x)=1$ **for every $x$**, not at one unlucky point.
The measured gap between "with the factor" and "without the factor" is exactly $0.000000$ across all 6001 sample points.

This has an uncomfortable and useful consequence.

> **On Q18.1 specifically, a student who does not know the chain rule applies to a limit of integration writes down the correct answer anyway, for the upper term.**
> No check, numeric or algebraic, can distinguish them.
> The lower limit is the entire discriminating power of this question.

The examiner presumably knows this, which is a reasonable inference from the design: the question pairs a factor of $1$ with a factor of $2$, so the four points are decided almost entirely by whether the $2$ appears.
Here is the control that shows the same error is visible when the factor is not $1$, using $\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2}\sin\left(t^2\right)dt$, whose correct value is $2x\sin\left(x^4\right)$:

| $x$ | $g'(x)$ | correct | with $g'$ dropped | gap |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $0.25$ | $0.5$ | $0.001953120033$ | $0.003906240066$ | $1.953\times10^{-3}$ |
| **$0.50$** | **$1.0$** | $0.062459317842$ | $0.062459317842$ | **$0.000$, hides** |
| $0.75$ | $1.5$ | $0.466729832787$ | $0.311153221858$ | $1.556\times10^{-1}$ |
| $1.00$ | $2.0$ | $1.682941969616$ | $0.841470984808$ | $8.415\times10^{-1}$ |
| $1.50$ | $3.0$ | $-2.818003916027$ | $-0.939334638676$ | $1.879$ |

**The practical rule: never validate a chain factor by checking a number. Validate it by counting.**
One factor per variable limit, written down before you evaluate anything.

### The relationship to Q16.1's differentiate-back check

`notes\Q16.1 - Five integrals.md` closes with a decision tree whose check node reads:

> differentiate back -> catches every function error -> BLIND to $+C$, domain, limits

That discrimination is exactly on point here, because **this question is the differentiate-back check promoted to a theorem**.
FTC Part 1 is the statement that differentiating an accumulated integral returns the integrand.
So the same blind spots transfer: the check sees the *function*, and is blind to anything that does not change the function.
On Q18.1 the "anything that does not change the function" is a multiplication by $1$, which is why error 15 hides and why the four points really turn on the lower limit's orientation and factor.
