# Q5.2 - Interpret five conditions, then sketch

**The question (MT2 Q5, 10 points).**
Interpret in terms of the graph of the function $f$ the conditions listed below.
Sketch the graph of a single function $f(x)$ which satisfies all of the conditions listed below.

- a. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = 3$
- b. $f(0) = 0$
- c. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to -1} f(x) = 2$
- d. $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = \infty$
- e. the function is continuous but not differentiable at $x = 0$

**The answer.**
There is no unique answer, and that is the point.
Any function with these five features scores full marks, and the marks are spread over the five conditions.
This tutorial ships one, chosen because every landmark is a whole number or a small fraction.

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
-2x, & x \le 0\\[4pt]
\dfrac{x}{1-x}, & 0 < x < 1\\[4pt]
3 + \dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x > 1
\end{cases}$$

$$f(-1)=2
\quad
f(0)=0 \text{ with a corner, slopes } -2 \text{ and } +1
\quad
x=1 \text{ is a vertical asymptote, } +\infty \text{ both sides}
\quad
y=3 \text{ as } x\to\infty$$

**What to produce, and what to type:**

| what is being asked for | what you produce |
| :--- | :--- |
| the **interpretation** half | five sentences, one per condition, each naming a *feature* - section 5 gives them verbatim |
| the **sketch** half | the labelled graph in section 5, with a dashed $y=3$, a dashed $x=1$, a solid dot at $(0,0)$ and a visible kink there |
| "give a formula" | `-2*x` for `x<=0`, `x/(1-x)` for `0<x<1`, `3+1/(x-1)` for `x>1` |
| the horizontal asymptote | `y=3` |
| the vertical asymptote | `x=1` |
| $\lim_{x\to1^-}f$ and $\lim_{x\to1^+}f$ | `infinity` and `infinity` |
| $\lim_{x\to0}f$ | `0` - forced by (b) and (e) together |
| $f(-1)$ | `2` in the shipped answer, but the condition does **not** require it |
| where $f$ fails to be differentiable | `x=0` |
| the reason it fails there | `corner` |
| is $f$ continuous at $0$? | `yes` |
| is $f$ continuous at $1$? | `no` |

## 0. The 30-second version

Q5.1 was a translation exercise: four conditions at three separate places, drawn one at a time.
Q5.2 is translation **plus routing**, because its five conditions are not all independent, and because the graph now has to travel between them.

```
Interpret, then sketch, with:
   (a) lim_{x->inf} f = 3    (b) f(0)=0    (c) lim_{x->-1} f = 2
   (d) lim_{x->1} f = inf    (e) continuous but NOT differentiable at 0

STEP 1  SORT THE CONDITIONS BY x.     -1 . . . 0 . . . 1 . . . . . -> +inf
          x=-1  (c)      x=0  (b)+(e)      x=1  (d)      tail  (a)
        TWO of them share x=0.  Reconcile that pair BEFORE drawing anything.

STEP 2  x = 0   (b) says the point (0,0) is ON the graph.
                (e) says the graph is UNBROKEN there but has NO tangent line.
        Together:  lim_{x->0} f = f(0) = 0, and the curve arrives at the
        origin from both sides with DIFFERENT slopes.  Draw a V-vertex, not
        a smooth minimum, and not a break.

STEP 3  x = 1   "lim = inf" is an EQUALITY, not "does not exist".
        So BOTH branches run UP to +infinity.  Dashed vertical line x=1,
        curve rising on the left of it AND on the right of it.

STEP 4  x -> +inf   "= 3"  ->  dashed HORIZONTAL line y=3, right end settles
        onto it.  Combined with step 3, the right branch must come back DOWN
        from +infinity to 3.

STEP 5  x = -1   "lim = 2"  ->  the curve approaches height 2 there.  The
        VALUE f(-1) is your free choice: solid dot at (-1,2) is simplest.

STEP 6  JOIN UP.  -2x on the left, x/(1-x) in the middle, 3+1/(x-1) on the
        right.  Or any curve with the same five features.

CHECK   Read the slope each side of 0.   -2 and +1.  Different -> corner.
        Read the height each side of 0.   both 0.     Same     -> continuous.
        Read f at 11, 101, 1001.          3.1, 3.01, 3.001.    Gap shrinking.
        Both sides of x=1 go UP?          yes.
```

Six steps, no calculator, no algebra beyond substituting three numbers.
The two steps that carry the marks are step 2 and step 3, because both are places where the notation says something stronger than it looks.

## 1. What this question is really testing

Item 5 is worth 10 of the 94 points on Midterm 2, the same as the asymptote question and more than the derivative-rules question at 16 spread over four parts.
It is the joint-largest award per unit of algebra on the paper, and there is no algebra in it at all.

The reason it is priced at 10 rather than at 5 like its Midterm 1 cousin is that it asks for **two deliverables**, and the first sentence of the question is the one students skip.

| Deliverable | What it is | How it is marked |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "Interpret in terms of the graph" | five written statements, one per condition | one per condition, on whether you name the **feature** |
| "Sketch the graph of a single function" | one labelled picture satisfying all five | one per condition, on whether the feature is **visible** |

That structure is the difference from Q5.1.
Q5.1 asked only for the picture; here the same content has to be produced twice, once in words and once in ink, and each pass is worth roughly half.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| A limit and a function value are **independent** unless "continuous" is said | Q3.2, Q4.1's hole, Q5.1, Q5.3 |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=\infty$ is a claim about **both** sides | Q3.3b, Q4.1's vertical asymptotes, Q5.3 (c) |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L$ means a horizontal asymptote on that end only | Q3.3a, Q4.1, Final Exam curve sketching |
| Continuous does **not** imply differentiable | Q2.2's corner factory, the Mean Value Theorem, Q5.1 |
| Two conditions at the same $x$ must be reconciled first | this question, Q5.3, Family 1 below |
| Writing the feature's **name**, not a description of the picture | every "interpret" and "explain" item on both papers |

**Why the interpretation half is where the easy marks are.**
A sketch can be marked down for being unclear.
A sentence that says "$x=1$ is a vertical asymptote and both branches rise without bound as $x$ approaches $1$" cannot be misread.
Write the sentences first, then draw the picture from your own sentences, and the picture inherits their precision.

**Format warning.**
The sample paper is a written, constructed response asking for prose and a drawing.
The live delivery is Möbius, which is answer-only and machine-marked, and a machine can mark neither.
So the live version almost certainly asks for the same content in a machine-readable form: boxes for the asymptotes, boxes for the one-sided limits at $x=1$, a yes/no on continuity at $0$, a word naming the mechanism, or a multiple-choice item showing four candidate graphs.
Section 7 prepares for all of those, and the reasoning behind that expectation is stated there rather than presented as a fact about the platform.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

Q5.1's section 2 built the translation dictionary and the four-way non-differentiability list.
This section does not repeat them.
It covers the five things Q5.2 needs **that Q5.1 did not**.

### 2.1 The dictionary, read left to right and right to left

The interpretation half is the dictionary read backwards: you are handed the notation and asked for the picture.
The sketch half is the same dictionary read forwards.
Below, the middle column is what earns the mark and the right column is what does not.

| Condition | The sentence that earns the mark | The sentence that does not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| (a) $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=3$ | "$y=3$ is a horizontal asymptote on the right: as $x$ grows without bound the curve settles onto that line and the vertical gap to it tends to zero." | "the graph goes to 3" |
| (b) $f(0)=0$ | "the point $(0,0)$ lies on the graph; $f$ is defined at $0$ and its value there is $0$." | "the graph starts at zero" |
| (c) $\lim_{x\to-1}f=2$ | "as $x$ approaches $-1$ from either side the curve approaches height $2$. This says nothing about $f(-1)$ itself, which may be $2$, may be another number, or may be undefined." | "$f(-1)=2$" |
| (d) $\lim_{x\to1}f=\infty$ | "$x=1$ is a vertical asymptote. Because the statement is an **equality with $+\infty$** rather than 'does not exist', **both** one-sided limits are $+\infty$: the curve rises without bound on both sides of $x=1$. $f$ is undefined at $1$." | "the graph is undefined at 1", or drawing one branch down |
| (e) continuous but not differentiable at $0$ | "the curve is unbroken at $x=0$ - both one-sided limits equal the value $f(0)=0$ - but it has no tangent line there. The cheapest picture is a corner: two different finite one-sided slopes." | "there's a sharp point" |

Read the right column carefully.
Every entry there is a **true** description of the picture.
They lose marks anyway, because the question asked you to interpret the notation, and naming the feature is what proves you can.

### 2.2 Two conditions at the same $x$: cooperate, conflict, or ignore each other

This is the mechanic Q5.1 explicitly did not have.
Q5.1's own tutorial says its four conditions "live at three different places on the $x$-axis, so they cannot fight each other".
Q5.2's (b) and (e) both live at $x=0$.

| Pair at the same $a$ | Verdict | Consequence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(a)=c$ **and** continuous at $a$ | cooperate | forces $\lim_{x\to a}f = c$ |
| $f(a)=c$ **and** $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ with $L \neq c$ | cooperate | forces a **displaced dot**: hollow circle at $(a,L)$, solid dot at $(a,c)$ |
| $f(a)=c$ **and** $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE | cooperate | forces a break with an isolated dot - this is Q5.1 |
| $f(a)=c$ **and** $\lim_{x\to a}f=L\neq c$ **and** continuous | conflict | impossible |
| continuous **and** not differentiable | cooperate | three legal mechanisms survive, one is ruled out |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=\infty$ **and** continuous at $a$ | conflict | impossible: a continuous point is a finite point |
| $f(a)$ undefined **and** continuous at $a$ | conflict | impossible: continuity needs a value to be continuous *to* |

**Q5.2's pair is the first row and the fifth row at once.**
(b) supplies $f(0)=0$.
(e) supplies continuity at $0$, which forces $\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=0$, and also forbids the differentiability that a smooth join would give.

That derived limit is a fact the question never states and a marker will look for.
It was checked exhaustively rather than argued: over the 573-model domain built for Q5.1's Family 10, the conditions $f(0)=0$, continuous at $0$, not differentiable at $0$ admit exactly **one** model, and its two-sided limit is $0$.
Drop the word "continuous" and **nine** different two-sided behaviours survive.
One word does all that work.

### 2.3 "$=\infty$" is a stronger statement than "does not exist"

This is the single most misread line on the paper, and it is condition (d).

| What is written | What it means | Cheapest function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = +\infty$ | **both** sides run up | $\dfrac{1}{(x-a)^2}$ |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = -\infty$ | **both** sides run down | $-\dfrac{1}{(x-a)^2}$ |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE, with a vertical asymptote | the sides **disagree** | $\dfrac{1}{x-a}$ |
| $\lim_{x\to a^+}f = +\infty$ | only the **right** branch is constrained | anything, patched on the right |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE, no asymptote | a jump, or oscillation | a piecewise jump |

The rule underneath is one line: **an even power in the denominator keeps the sign, an odd power flips it.**
$\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$ is positive on both sides of $1$ because a square is positive.
$\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ is negative just left of $1$ and positive just right of it.

Condition (d) is written as an equality, so $\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ **fails it**.
That was verified rather than asserted: the function $-2x$, $\frac{x}{x-1}$, $3+\frac{1}{x-1}$ satisfies (a), (b), (c) and (e) and breaks (d) and only (d), because its left branch runs to $-\infty$.
It is route W in section 4, and it is the most attractive wrong answer on this question.

**One qualifier.**
Some textbooks treat "$\lim = \infty$" as loose shorthand for "the limit fails by blowing up".
Athabasca's own Q5.3 (c) writes the same thing, and the safest reading is the strict one, because a graph with both branches going up satisfies both readings while a graph with them disagreeing satisfies only the loose one.
Draw both branches up. It costs nothing and it cannot be wrong.

### 2.4 Continuous **and** not differentiable: three mechanisms, not four

Q5.1's condition said only "not differentiable" and admitted four mechanisms: corner, cusp, vertical tangent, and discontinuity.
Condition (e) adds the word "continuous", which deletes the fourth.

| Mechanism | One-sided slopes | Continuous? | Legal for (e)? | Cheapest example at $0$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Corner | two different **finite** numbers | yes | **yes** | $\lvert x\rvert$, slopes $-1$ and $+1$ |
| Cusp | $+\infty$ and $-\infty$, **opposite** signs | yes | yes | $\lvert x\rvert^{2/3}$ |
| Vertical tangent | $\pm\infty$, the **same** sign both sides | yes | yes | $\sqrt[3]{x}$ |
| Discontinuity | not applicable | no | **no** | a jump |

**Use the corner.**
It is one straight line meeting another, everyone can draw it, and a marker recognises it instantly.
The shipped answer's corner has slopes $-2$ and $+1$.

**A note on the course review file.**
`MATH265_Review.md` states this list four separate times and the four statements disagree with each other.
One of them, under "Non-differentiable points", gives exactly three items - corners, cusps, vertical tangents - and omits discontinuity.
That list is **wrong in general** and it is **exactly right for condition (e)**, which is a coincidence and not a reason to trust the file.
Another passage writes "Sharp corners (cusps)", which merges two different things.
The four-way list is the correct one; delete the discontinuity row only when the word "continuous" appears, as it does here.

### 2.5 A horizontal asymptote is not a fence, and only one end is constrained

Condition (a) constrains $x\to+\infty$ and says nothing about $x\to-\infty$.
The shipped answer's left branch runs to $+\infty$, which is fine, and route B's runs to $3$, which is also fine.

More importantly, the graph is allowed to **cross** $y=3$, and the shipped answer does so at $x=-\tfrac32$ and again at $x=\tfrac34$.
It has to.
The middle branch starts at height $0$ and runs to $+\infty$, so if it is drawn without lifting the pen the Intermediate Value Theorem forces it through every height above $0$, and $3$ is one of them.

So the picture is: the curve crosses the line $y=3$ twice on its way up, then blows up at $x=1$, then comes back down and settles onto the same line from above without ever touching it again.
That is not a contradiction. A horizontal asymptote constrains only the tail.
The vertical asymptote at $x=1$ is a genuine barrier, but only because $f$ is undefined there.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Write the $x$-value beside every condition.** Anything at $\pm\infty$ goes in a separate tail pile. Sort them.
> 2. **Find the collisions.** Any two conditions at the same $x$ get reconciled *before* you draw. Use the table in 2.2. If the pair is impossible, say which two clash and draw nothing.
> 3. **Translate each condition into a feature**, and write the sentence while you do it. The interpretation half of the answer is finished at this point.
> 4. **Draw the frame only.** Axes, a dashed line for each asymptote, a tick on the $x$-axis at every constrained value. No curves yet.
> 5. **Place the marks.** Solid dot where a value exists; hollow circle where a limit is approached but not attained.
> 6. **Route between them.** This is the step Q5.1 did not need. Ask what has to happen *between* two marks, not just at them.
> 7. **Check, condition by condition, in the order given.**
> 8. **Label everything.**

**Classify before you start.**

| The condition looks like | It is about | It constrains |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(a)=b$, or $f(a)$ undefined | the **value** | one point |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = L$ finite | the **neighbours** | two branches, not the point |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = \pm\infty$ | the **neighbours** | both branches, and the value is undefined |
| $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f = L$ | one **tail** | that end only |
| continuous at $a$ | value **and** neighbours together | ties them |
| differentiable / not at $a$ | the **slope** | the tangent line |
| $f'$ or $f''$ sign on an interval | the **shape** | direction or bend, never height |

**Step 6, routing, in detail.**
Once the marks are placed, each gap between consecutive marks is a small design problem with a forced answer.

| Gap in Q5.2 | What is forced |
| :--- | :--- |
| left of $-1$ | nothing at all |
| $-1$ to $0$ | travel from height $2$ down to height $0$ |
| $0$ to $1$ | leave the origin with a slope different from the left one, then rise to $+\infty$; if drawn unbroken it must cross **every** height above $0$, including $3$ |
| $1$ to $+\infty$ | come down from $+\infty$ and settle on $3$; if drawn unbroken it takes every value above $3$ |

**Q5.2 is one collision, one routing constraint, and three isolated features.**
That is exactly one more moving part than Q5.1, and it is worth exactly twice the marks.

## 4. Q5.2 worked eight ways

Every function below was audited against all five conditions by the same automated checker.
Each condition is tested the way its own definition is written, never by looking at one sample point.

### Route A - line, hyperbola, hyperbola (use this one)

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
-2x, & x \le 0\\[4pt]
\dfrac{x}{1-x}, & 0 < x < 1\\[4pt]
3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x > 1
\end{cases}$$

**At $x=-1$.** The left piece is a straight line through the origin, so $f(-1)=2$ and the curve is continuous there.
Condition (c) only asked for the limit; getting the value for free costs nothing.

**At $x=0$.** Both pieces arrive at $0$: the line gives $-2\cdot0=0$ and $\frac{x}{1-x}\to0$.
So $f$ is continuous, with $\lim_{x\to0}f=f(0)=0$.
The one-sided difference quotients are exactly $-2$ and exactly $\frac{1}{1-h}$, whose limit is $+1$.
Two different finite slopes, so no tangent line exists: a **corner**.

**At $x=1$.** From the left, $\frac{x}{1-x}$ has a positive numerator and a denominator shrinking to $0^+$, so it runs to $+\infty$.
From the right, $\frac{1}{x-1}$ has a denominator shrinking to $0^+$, so it also runs to $+\infty$.
Both sides up, so "$\lim_{x\to1}f=\infty$" is a true statement and not a shorthand.

**As $x\to\infty$.** $f(x)-3 = \frac{1}{x-1}$ exactly, which tends to $0$ from above.
So $y=3$ is a horizontal asymptote approached from above and never reached.

**Why these numbers.** The slope $-2$ on the left is forced once you decide to pass through both $(-1,2)$ and $(0,0)$ with a straight line.
The $\frac{x}{1-x}$ shape makes the middle branch pass through $(\tfrac12,1)$, $(\tfrac23,2)$, $(\tfrac34,3)$, $(\tfrac45,4)$ and $(\tfrac9{10},9)$, so five labelled points cost no arithmetic.
The right branch gives $f(2)=4$, $f(3)=\tfrac72$, $f(11)=\tfrac{31}{10}$, $f(101)=\tfrac{301}{100}$.

### Route B - one closed formula, no cases

$$f(x)=\frac{3x^2+5\lvert x\rvert}{(x-1)^2}$$

One expression covers all five conditions, which is worth knowing exists but is not worth deriving under exam pressure.

- $f(0)=0$ immediately.
- $f(-1) = \frac{3+5}{4} = 2$, and the function is continuous there, so (c) holds.
- Near $x=1$ the numerator tends to $8>0$ and the denominator to $0^+$ through a **square**, so both sides run to $+\infty$.
- As $x\to\pm\infty$ the $3x^2$ term dominates and $f\to3$ at **both** ends.
- The $\lvert x\rvert$ term is what breaks differentiability at $0$: the one-sided slopes are $-5$ and $+5$.

The construction is worth seeing once because it shows how the conditions determine the coefficients.
Write $f(x)=\frac{3x^2+b\lvert x\rvert}{(x-1)^2}$; the $3$ comes from (a), the numerator's lack of a constant term comes from (b), $\frac{3+b}{4}=2$ from (c) gives $b=5$, the square in the denominator gives (d), and $b\neq0$ gives (e).

### Route C - two pieces only

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}-2x, & x\le0\\[4pt] \dfrac{3x^2}{(x-1)^2}, & x>0\end{cases}$$

The right piece does double duty: it blows up at $x=1$ from both sides and settles on $y=3$ at infinity, so conditions (a) and (d) are handled by one expression.
Its corner slopes are $-2$ and $0$.
A slope of $0$ on the right means the curve leaves the origin **horizontally** and the kink is easy to draw but slightly easy to miss, which is why route A is preferred.

### Route D - a hole or a displaced dot at $x=-1$

Take route A and redefine $f(-1)=5$, or leave $f(-1)$ undefined.
The limit at $-1$ is still $2$, because a limit never looks at the point itself.
This route exists to make one thing concrete: condition (c) does **not** pin $f(-1)$.
Drawing a hollow circle at $(-1,2)$ with a solid dot at $(-1,5)$ is a full-mark answer, and so is drawing nothing but the hollow circle.

### Route E - a cusp at $0$ instead of a corner

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}2\lvert x\rvert^{2/3}, & x\le0\\[4pt] \dfrac{x^{2/3}}{1-x}, & 0<x<1\\[4pt] 3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x>1\end{cases}$$

Continuous at $0$ with $f(0)=0$, and $f(-1)=2$.
The difference quotient runs to $-\infty$ on the left and $+\infty$ on the right, so the two arms come into the origin vertically from opposite directions.
That is a **cusp**, and it satisfies (e) perfectly.
It is harder to draw convincingly than a corner and gains nothing.

### Route F - a vertical tangent at $0$

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}2\sqrt[3]{-x}, & x\le0\\[4pt] -2\sqrt[3]{x}+\dfrac{x}{1-x}, & 0<x<1\\[4pt] 3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x>1\end{cases}$$

Here the difference quotient runs to $-\infty$ on **both** sides, so the curve passes through the origin with a vertical tangent line, decreasing through it.
It is continuous and not differentiable, so (e) holds.
The distinction from a cusp is only the sign agreement, and it is worth being able to say which is which.

### Route G - the tail approaches $y=3$ from below

Take route A and replace the right piece with $3+\dfrac{2-x}{(x-1)^2}$.
Just right of $1$ the numerator is near $1$ and the denominator near $0^+$, so it still runs to $+\infty$.
As $x\to\infty$ the numerator is dominated by $-x$ and the denominator by $x^2$, so the gap tends to $0$ from **below** and the branch crosses $y=3$ at exactly $x=2$ before settling underneath it.
This exists to kill the belief that a graph must approach its asymptote from one particular side.

### Route W - the wrong reading of condition (d)

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}-2x, & x\le0\\[4pt] \dfrac{x}{x-1}, & 0<x<1\\[4pt] 3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}, & x>1\end{cases}$$

One sign changed in the middle branch, and it is the most natural wrong answer on the paper.
Conditions (a), (b), (c) and (e) all still hold.
But now the left branch runs to $-\infty$ while the right runs to $+\infty$, so the two-sided limit **does not exist** and "$\lim_{x\to1}f=\infty$" is false rather than approximate.
Verified: this function breaks condition (d) and only condition (d).

### The route comparison

| Route | Mechanism at $0$ | Time to draw | Risk | What it buys |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **A** line + two hyperbolas | corner, $-2$ and $+1$ | **fastest** | **lowest** | every landmark is a whole number or a small fraction |
| B one closed formula | corner, $-5$ and $+5$ | slow | medium | shows how the conditions fix the coefficients |
| C two pieces | corner, $-2$ and $0$ | fast | medium | the flat right slope reads as smooth if drawn carelessly |
| D hole at $-1$ | corner | fast | low | proves (c) does not pin $f(-1)$ |
| E cusp | cusp | slow | high | harder to draw so that a marker sees a cusp |
| F vertical tangent | vertical tangent | slow | high | the cusp-versus-tangent distinction |
| G tail from below | corner | medium | low | kills the "asymptotes are fences" belief |
| W wrong reading | corner | fast | **fails (d)** | the counterexample, never the answer |

**Under exam pressure: a straight line into the origin, a second curve leaving it at a visibly different angle, both branches rising up the dashed line $x=1$, and the right branch flattening onto the dashed line $y=3$.**

```
   1                   *** |  *  :
                         **|***  :
   0  --------------------*@*----:---------------------------------------
                           |     :
  -1                       |     :
                           |     :
         |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |     |
         -3    -2    -1    0     1     2     3     4     5     6     7
```

Three things a marker looks for in that picture.
The kink at the origin, where the steep descending line meets a gently rising curve.
The two branches climbing on **both** sides of the dashed line $x=1$.
The right branch flattening onto the dashed line $y=3$ from above, having already crossed it once on the far side of the asymptote.

Now the interpretation half.
These are the five sentences, and they are worth writing out in full because the question asked for them by name.

> **(a) $\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=3$.**
> The line $y=3$ is a horizontal asymptote of the graph at the right-hand end.
> As $x$ increases without bound the curve settles onto that line, and the vertical distance between them tends to zero.
> Nothing is claimed about the behaviour as $x\to-\infty$.
>
> **(b) $f(0)=0$.**
> The function is defined at $x=0$ and its value there is $0$, so the point $(0,0)$ lies on the graph.
> On its own this places a single dot and says nothing about the curve on either side.
>
> **(c) $\lim_{x\to-1}f(x)=2$.**
> As $x$ approaches $-1$ from the left and from the right, the height of the curve approaches $2$.
> This constrains the two branches near $x=-1$ but not the value $f(-1)$, which may equal $2$, may be some other number, or may be undefined.
>
> **(d) $\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=\infty$.**
> The line $x=1$ is a vertical asymptote.
> Because the statement is an equality with $+\infty$ rather than "does not exist", the curve rises without bound on **both** sides of $x=1$.
> The function is not defined at $x=1$.
>
> **(e) $f$ is continuous but not differentiable at $x=0$.**
> The graph is unbroken at $x=0$: both one-sided limits exist and equal the value, so $\lim_{x\to0}f(x)=f(0)=0$.
> But the graph has no tangent line there.
> In the sketch this is a corner, where the one-sided slopes are $-2$ and $+1$: two different finite numbers, so $f'(0)$ does not exist.

And then the function itself, with the justification a 10-point item expects.

> Take
> $f(x) = -2x$ for $x\le0$, $f(x) = \dfrac{x}{1-x}$ for $0<x<1$, and $f(x) = 3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ for $x>1$.
>
> $f(-1) = 2$, so $\lim_{x\to-1}f = 2$ by continuity.
> $f(0)=0$, and both branches tend to $0$ there, so $f$ is continuous at $0$; the one-sided slopes are $-2$ and $+1$, so $f'(0)$ does not exist and the graph has a corner.
> As $x\to1^-$, $\dfrac{x}{1-x}\to+\infty$; as $x\to1^+$, $3+\dfrac{1}{x-1}\to+\infty$; so $\lim_{x\to1}f = +\infty$ and $x=1$ is a vertical asymptote.
> As $x\to\infty$, $\dfrac{1}{x-1}\to0$, so $f\to3$ and $y=3$ is a horizontal asymptote approached from above.

Four things students routinely skip.

**Answering the first sentence at all.**
"Interpret in terms of the graph" is an instruction, not a preamble.
A perfect sketch with no interpretation is at best half the marks.

**Naming the feature rather than describing the drawing.**
"It shoots up near 1" and "$x=1$ is a vertical asymptote and both one-sided limits are $+\infty$" describe the same picture and are not worth the same.

**Saying that $f$ is continuous at $0$ out loud.**
The condition said it, so repeating it is free, and it proves you did not read "not differentiable" as "broken".

**Saying that (c) does not fix $f(-1)$.**
One clause, and it is the sentence that separates a student who knows what a limit is from one who has memorised a picture.

## 6. Verify before you commit

Go through the conditions **one at a time, in the order given**, and point at the feature.
Budget thirty seconds of a three-minute answer for this.

**Condition (a), the tail.**
Walk right and read three heights.

| $x$ | $f(x)$ | reading |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $2$ | $3+1 = 4$ | above the asymptote |
| $11$ | $3+\tfrac1{10} = \tfrac{31}{10}$ | closer |
| $101$ | $3+\tfrac1{100} = \tfrac{301}{100}$ | closer still |
| $1001$ | $3+\tfrac1{1000} = \tfrac{3001}{1000}$ | the gap shrinks by a factor of ten per decade |

The gap must **shrink**, not merely be small.
A branch that flattens out at $3.2$ and stays there has limit $3.2$, and on a hand sketch it is indistinguishable from a correct one.

**Condition (b), the dot.**
There must be exactly **one** solid dot on the vertical line $x=0$, and it must sit on the $x$-axis.
Count the marks in that column. If there are two, your graph is not a function.

**Condition (c), the limit at $-1$.**
Read the height just left of $-1$ and just right of $-1$.
Both must be near $2$ and they must be near **each other**.
Whatever you did with $f(-1)$ itself is your own business.

**Condition (d), both branches.**
Cover the right half of the picture with your hand and check the left branch runs **up**.
Then cover the left half and check the right branch runs **up**.
This is the check that catches route W, and it takes two seconds.

**Condition (e), the two halves of it.**
Continuity: read the height just left of $0$ and just right of $0$. They must be the **same**, and equal to $f(0)=0$.
Non-differentiability: read the **slope** just left of $0$ and just right of $0$. They must be **different**.
Same height, different slope. That pair of readings is condition (e) in its entirety.

**The check nobody does: is it a function at all?**
Sweep a vertical line across the whole picture.
It must meet the graph at most once everywhere.

### A trap in the checking itself

If you check differentiability by sampling either side of $0$ and averaging, you are computing the **centred** difference $\dfrac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}$, and for the shipped answer it returns a stable, entirely plausible $-0.5$.
That number is just the average of $-2$ and $+1$.
A centred difference is blind to corners, and here it does something worse than fail: it reports a specific slope, so it looks like a successful measurement.

Q5.1 documented this on $\lvert x+2\rvert$, where the symmetric quotient returns exactly $0$.
This is a second, differently shaped corner where the same quotient returns a non-zero number, which shows the failure is a property of the quotient and not of the absolute value.
Differentiability has to be tested **one side at a time**, which is what the definition says.

### The numbers worth memorising for this function

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| corner | $(0,0)$, slopes $-2$ then $+1$ |
| $f(-2),\,f(-\tfrac32),\,f(-1),\,f(-\tfrac12)$ | $4,\ 3,\ 2,\ 1$ |
| $f(\tfrac12),\,f(\tfrac23),\,f(\tfrac34),\,f(\tfrac45),\,f(\tfrac9{10})$ | $1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 9$ |
| $f(2),\,f(3),\,f(5),\,f(11)$ | $4,\ \tfrac72,\ \tfrac{13}4,\ \tfrac{31}{10}$ |
| where the graph crosses $y=3$ | $x=-\tfrac32$ and $x=\tfrac34$, never again |
| vertical asymptote | $x=1$, $+\infty$ on **both** sides |
| horizontal asymptote | $y=3$, right end only, approached from above |
| $f'$ on the left branch | $-2$ everywhere |
| $f'$ on the middle branch | $\dfrac{1}{(1-x)^2}$, so $+1$ at $0$, $+4$ at $\tfrac12$, $+16$ at $\tfrac34$ |
| $f'$ on the right branch | $-\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$, so $-1$ at $2$, $-\tfrac14$ at $3$, $-\tfrac1{16}$ at $5$ |
| shape | middle branch increasing and concave up; right branch decreasing and concave up |
| left end behaviour | $f\to+\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$, and that is unconstrained |

The middle branch's five points are a ladder: $\tfrac12\mapsto1$, $\tfrac23\mapsto2$, $\tfrac34\mapsto3$, $\tfrac45\mapsto4$.
The pattern is $\dfrac{n}{n+1}\mapsto n$, which is one fact rather than four.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

The sample paper wants prose and a drawing.
A machine can mark neither, so on Möbius the same ten points have to be collected some other way.
The forms that can be machine-marked are **feature boxes**, **yes/no items**, and **graph selection**, and preparing for all three costs nothing because the content is identical.

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| An infinite limit | `infinity` | `inf`, `oo`, `∞`, or a blank box |
| A negative infinite limit | `-infinity` | `neg infinity` |
| A limit that does not exist | `DNE` | `infinity`, when the sides disagree |
| The horizontal asymptote | `y=3` | `3`, or `x=3` |
| The vertical asymptote | `x=1` | `1`, or `y=1` |
| $\lim_{x\to0}f$ | `0` | `DNE` - conditions (b) and (e) force it to exist |
| $f(-1)$, if asked | whatever **your** graph does | `2`, unless you chose the continuous route |
| The point of non-differentiability | `x=0` | `0` if the box already shows `x =` |
| The mechanism | `corner` | `cusp`, which is a different thing |
| Continuity at $0$ | `yes` | `no`, which is the commonest slip |
| Continuity at $1$ | `no` | `yes` |
| Is $f$ differentiable at $0$? | `no` | `yes` |
| The middle branch | `x/(1-x)` | `x/1-x`, which parses as $\frac{x}{1}-x$ |
| The right branch | `3+1/(x-1)` | `3+1/x-1`, which parses as $3+\frac1x-1$ |
| Multiplication | `-2*x` | `-2x` |

**The single most expensive answer on this question is a graph that dives to $-\infty$ on the left of $x=1$.**
It satisfies four of the five conditions, it looks like a textbook hyperbola, and it silently contradicts the one condition written as an equality.
Before you submit anything, check that both branches next to $x=1$ point **up**.

**The second most expensive is a smooth curve through the origin.**
Conditions (b) and (e) together do force the curve to pass through $(0,0)$ unbroken, so the instinct to draw it in one stroke is half right.
What it must not be is **smooth**. The pen has to change direction there.

**The third is answering `no` to "is $f$ continuous at $x=0$".**
The condition said "continuous" in so many words.
Students answer `no` because they are reading "not differentiable" and pattern-matching to Q5.1.

**If the item is multiple choice, eliminate rather than select.**
Take the conditions in the order that kills candidates fastest, which is not the order they are printed.

1. Look at $x=1$ first. A graph with one branch down dies on (d). This usually removes half the options.
2. Look at $x=0$. A graph that is smooth there dies on (e); a graph that breaks there also dies on (e).
3. Look at the right tail. A graph that runs off or flattens at the wrong height dies on (a).
4. Only then check $(0,0)$ and the height at $-1$.

**If the item asks for the formula, bracket every denominator.**
`3+1/(x-1)` evaluates to $4$ at $x=2$; `3+1/x-1` evaluates to $2.5$.

## 8. The variation catalog

Q5.1's tutorial already carries Families 0 to 13, which cover the base translation dictionary: value conditions, finite limits, jumps, poles, limits at infinity, non-differentiability, $f'$ and $f''$ signs, one-sided conditions, impossibility, the reverse question, given-formula questions, and parameter fitting.
**This catalog does not repeat them.**
It covers the twelve things that can vary in a Q5.2-shaped question specifically: a question with an interpretation half, with conditions that collide, and with a route to plan between them.
Where a family overlaps Q5.1's, the cross-reference is given rather than the content.

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
In most of them the number of genuinely different pictures is two or three, and everything else is those pictures with different numbers substituted.

### Family 0 - the interpretation half on its own

**Trigger.** "Interpret in terms of the graph", "give the interpretation", "explain what this tells you about the graph", or "state what each condition means". Q5.3's entire first half is this.
**Tool.** The table in 2.1, read from the notation to the feature name.
**The trap.** Describing the picture in ordinary words. "It jumps" is worth less than "the one-sided limits are $3$ and $2$, so the two-sided limit does not exist".

| Notation | The feature name to write |
| :--- | :--- |
| $f(a)=b$ | "the point $(a,b)$ is on the graph" |
| $f(a)$ undefined | "$f$ is not defined at $a$; there is a hole or an asymptote there" |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ | "the curve approaches height $L$ from both sides; the value at $a$ is unconstrained" |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE | "the one-sided limits disagree; the graph is broken at $a$" |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=+\infty$ | "$x=a$ is a vertical asymptote and both branches rise without bound" |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L$ | "$y=L$ is a horizontal asymptote at the right-hand end" |
| $f$ continuous at $a$ | "the curve is unbroken at $a$; you can draw through it without lifting the pen" |
| $f$ not differentiable at $a$ | "the graph has no tangent line at $a$" |
| $f'>0$ on $I$ | "the curve rises across $I$" |
| $f''>0$ on $I$ | "the curve is concave up on $I$, bending like a cup" |

**A sentence template that always earns the mark.** *"\<feature name\> at \<location\>, which means \<what the curve does\>, and \<what it does not constrain\>."*
The third clause is the one nobody writes and it is usually where the mark is.

### Family 1 - two conditions at the same $x$: the reconciliation matrix

**Trigger.** Two of the listed conditions mention the same value of $x$.
**Tool.** The matrix below. Look the pair up before drawing.
**The trap.** Drawing them one at a time, so the second silently overwrites the first.

The matrix was settled by exhaustive search over the 573-model domain, not by argument.
An entry is **impossible** exactly when no model satisfies both.

| Paired with $\to$ | $f(a)=0$ | $f(a)$ undef | $\lim=0$ | $\lim=2$ | $\lim$ DNE | $\lim=+\infty$ | cont | diff | not diff |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| $f(a)=0$ | - | **X** | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok |
| $f(a)$ undef | **X** | - | ok | ok | ok | ok | **X** | **X** | ok |
| $\lim=0$ | ok | ok | - | **X** | **X** | **X** | ok | ok | ok |
| $\lim=2$ | ok | ok | **X** | - | **X** | **X** | ok | ok | ok |
| $\lim$ DNE | ok | ok | **X** | **X** | - | **X** | **X** | **X** | ok |
| $\lim=+\infty$ | ok | ok | **X** | **X** | **X** | - | **X** | **X** | ok |
| cont | ok | **X** | ok | ok | **X** | **X** | - | ok | ok |
| diff | ok | **X** | ok | ok | **X** | **X** | ok | - | **X** |
| not diff | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | **X** | - |

There are only sixteen impossible pairs and they come from four ideas.
Two different values at one point.
Two different limits at one point.
A limit and continuity disagreeing.
Differentiable and not differentiable at once.

**The two cells students get wrong are both in the "ok" column.**
$f(a)=0$ with $\lim_{x\to a}f=2$ is legal - it is the displaced dot, a hollow circle at $(a,2)$ and a solid dot at $(a,0)$.
"Continuous **and** not differentiable" is legal - it is condition (e), and it is the whole reason Q5.2 exists.

**Cooperating pairs force a third fact.** These are the ones worth memorising.

| Pair | The fact it forces |
| :--- | :--- |
| $f(a)=c$ and continuous at $a$ | $\lim_{x\to a}f = c$ |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ and continuous at $a$ | $f(a) = L$ |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ and $f(a)=c\neq L$ | not continuous, and therefore not differentiable |
| differentiable at $a$ | continuous at $a$, and therefore $\lim_{x\to a}f=f(a)$ |
| continuous and **not** differentiable | corner, cusp or vertical tangent; never a break |

### Family 2 - the infinite-limit wording ladder

**Trigger.** Any condition with $\infty$ in it near a finite $x$.
**Tool.** Read whether the statement is an **equality** or a **denial**. That single word decides the picture.
**The trap.** Treating "$=\infty$" as loose shorthand and drawing $\frac{1}{x-a}$.

| Written condition | Left branch | Right branch | A function that does it | Odd or even power |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = +\infty$ | up | up | $\dfrac{1}{(x-a)^2}$ | even |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f = -\infty$ | down | down | $-\dfrac{1}{(x-a)^2}$ | even |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE, asymptote at $a$ | down | up | $\dfrac{1}{x-a}$ | odd |
| $\lim_{x\to a^+}f = +\infty$ only | free | up | patch the right side | either |
| $\lim_{x\to a^-}f = -\infty$ only | down | free | patch the left side | either |
| $\lim_{x\to a}f$ DNE, no asymptote | finite | finite, different | a jump | none |

**Only two pictures exist here**: both branches the same way, or the two branches opposite.
The even power gives the first and the odd power gives the second, and every row above is one of those two with labels changed.

**A hole is not an asymptote.**
$\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ has a vanishing denominator at $x=1$ and its limit there is $2$, not infinity.
The vertical-asymptote rule is "denominator zero **after simplification**", and `MATH265_Review.md` states it correctly in one of the three places it appears and drops the qualifier in another.

### Family 3 - routing: what is forced between the marked points

**Trigger.** Any condition set with three or more constrained locations, where the marks are not all at the same height.
**Tool.** Between two consecutive marks, ask what the Intermediate Value Theorem forces on a branch you intend to draw without lifting the pen.
**The trap.** Treating the picture as five independent features and drawing a curve between them that quietly violates one.

| Situation on an interval where $f$ is continuous | What is forced |
| :--- | :--- |
| $f$ runs from height $c$ to $+\infty$ | $f$ takes **every** height above $c$, including any asymptote height |
| $f$ runs from $+\infty$ down to $L$ | $f$ takes every value above $L$, and none at or below it |
| $f$ runs from $c$ to $d$ with $c<k<d$ | $f$ crosses $y=k$ at least once |
| $f$ has a vertical asymptote at $p$ and a horizontal one at $L$ for $x>p$ | the branch must come **back down**; it cannot keep rising |
| $f'>0$ throughout and $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L$ | $f<L$ everywhere on that tail, approaching from below |
| $f'<0$ throughout and $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L$ | $f>L$ everywhere on that tail, approaching from above |

**In Q5.2 this bites twice.**
The middle branch runs from $0$ to $+\infty$, so it crosses $y=3$; you cannot avoid it, and a marker who notices is checking whether you understand what an asymptote is.
The right branch runs from $+\infty$ to $3$, so it must descend on net; a right branch that rises is an instant contradiction between (a) and (d).

### Family 4 - the mechanisms for "continuous but not differentiable"

**Trigger.** The words "continuous" and "not differentiable" in the same condition.
**Tool.** Corner, cusp or vertical tangent. See 2.4.
**The trap.** Using a jump because it is the fastest way to be non-differentiable. The word "continuous" makes it illegal.

Cross-reference: Q5.1's Family 5 and Family 6 cover the underlying four-way list.
What is new here is that the **choice is now constrained**, and that the exam can ask you to name which mechanism you used.

| If the question adds | Legal mechanisms |
| :--- | :--- |
| nothing | corner, cusp, vertical tangent, discontinuity |
| "continuous" | corner, cusp, vertical tangent |
| "continuous, with $f'$ finite on both sides" | corner only |
| "the one-sided derivatives are infinite" | cusp or vertical tangent |
| "there is a vertical tangent line" | vertical tangent only |
| "$f$ is differentiable everywhere else" | any of the three, isolated at that one point |

### Family 5 - a finite two-sided limit and its three pictures

**Trigger.** $\lim_{x\to a}f=L$ with $L$ finite and nothing else said about $a$. This is Q5.2 (c).
**Tool.** Both branches converge to height $L$; then choose the value independently.
**The trap.** Reading it as $f(a)=L$.

| Picture | $f(a)$ | Continuous at $a$? | Drawn as |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| solid dot on the curve | $L$ | yes | one solid dot at $(a,L)$ |
| displaced dot | some $c\neq L$ | no | hollow circle at $(a,L)$, solid dot at $(a,c)$ |
| hole | undefined | no | hollow circle at $(a,L)$, nothing else |

All three are full-mark answers to (c) alone.
Choose the first, because it is one pen stroke and because no other condition in Q5.2 touches $x=-1$.
Choose one of the other two only if the question adds "and $f$ is discontinuous at $-1$" or "and $f(-1)$ is undefined".

### Family 6 - which tails are constrained

**Trigger.** A condition with $x\to\infty$ or $x\to-\infty$.
**Tool.** A dashed horizontal line at that height, at that end only.
**The trap.** Forcing both tails onto the same line when only one was named. That is extra work and extra risk for zero marks.

| Condition set | Picture | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=3$ only | right end flattens, left end free | the shipped answer, whose left tail runs to $+\infty$ |
| $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}f=3$ | both ends flatten onto the same line | route B |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=3$, $\lim_{x\to-\infty}f=-1$ | two different horizontal asymptotes | $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}}$ scaled |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=\infty$ | no horizontal asymptote at that end | any polynomial |
| $f(x)-(2x+1)\to0$ | a **slant** asymptote, not a horizontal one | $2x+1+\dfrac1x$ |

### Family 7 - the parametric generator: any numeric reword, in one formula

**Trigger.** The same five conditions with different numbers: a different asymptote height, a different pole location, a different finite-limit point.
**Tool.** Substitute into the generator below. Nothing has to be re-derived.
**The trap.** Rebuilding from scratch each time and making an arithmetic slip in the line.

For the condition set *"$\lim_{x\to\infty}f=H$; $f(0)=v$; $\lim_{x\to c}f=m$ with $c<0$; $\lim_{x\to p}f=+\infty$ with $p>0$; continuous but not differentiable at $0$"*:

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
v + \dfrac{m-v}{c}\,x, & x\le0\\[8pt]
v + \dfrac{x}{p-x}, & 0<x<p\\[8pt]
H + \dfrac{1}{x-p}, & x>p
\end{cases}$$

The corner slopes are $\dfrac{m-v}{c}$ on the left and $\dfrac{1}{p}$ on the right.
Every instance below was audited against its own five conditions.

| $c$ | $m$ | $p$ | $H$ | $v$ | The function | Corner slopes |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | :--- |
| $-1$ | $2$ | $1$ | $3$ | $0$ | the shipped answer | $-2$, $+1$ |
| $-1$ | $2$ | $2$ | $3$ | $0$ | $-2x$ / $\frac{x}{2-x}$ / $3+\frac{1}{x-2}$ | $-2$, $+\tfrac12$ |
| $-2$ | $4$ | $1$ | $3$ | $0$ | $-2x$ / $\frac{x}{1-x}$ / $3+\frac{1}{x-1}$ | $-2$, $+1$ |
| $-1$ | $5$ | $1$ | $-2$ | $0$ | $-5x$ / $\frac{x}{1-x}$ / $-2+\frac{1}{x-1}$ | $-5$, $+1$ |
| $-3$ | $1$ | $4$ | $0$ | $0$ | $-\tfrac13x$ / $\frac{x}{4-x}$ / $\frac{1}{x-4}$ | $-\tfrac13$, $+\tfrac14$ |
| $-1$ | $-2$ | $1$ | $3$ | $0$ | $2x$ / $\frac{x}{1-x}$ / $3+\frac{1}{x-1}$ | $+2$, $+1$ |
| $-4$ | $2$ | $3$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $-\tfrac12x$ / $\frac{x}{3-x}$ / $-1+\frac{1}{x-3}$ | $-\tfrac12$, $+\tfrac13$ |
| $-1$ | $3$ | $2$ | $-2$ | $1$ | $1-2x$ / $1+\frac{x}{2-x}$ / $-2+\frac{1}{x-2}$ | $-2$, $+\tfrac12$ |

**One degenerate case, and it is the only way to get this wrong.**
If $\dfrac{m-v}{c} = \dfrac{1}{p}$ the two slopes agree, the join is straight, and condition (e) **fails silently**.
It was verified: $c=-1$, $m=-1$, $p=1$, $v=0$ gives slope $+1$ on both sides and the non-differentiability checker correctly refuses it.
If your numbers land there, change the middle branch to $\frac{kx}{p-x}$ for any $k\neq$ the degenerate value.

### Family 8 - adding a sixth condition

**Trigger.** The same list plus one more line, often phrased as "and in addition".
**Tool.** Check the new line against the reconciliation matrix at its own $x$. If it lands somewhere no existing condition lives, it is almost always satisfiable.
**The trap.** Assuming the set is still satisfiable and drawing something that quietly fails an earlier condition.

| Sixth condition | Verdict | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "$f$ is differentiable at $0$" | **impossible** | contradicts (e) directly |
| "$f$ is continuous at $1$" | **impossible** | (d) makes $f$ undefined at $1$ |
| "$f(1)=5$" | **impossible** | same reason: an asymptote means no value there |
| "$\lim_{x\to0}f=2$" | **impossible** | (b) and (e) already force that limit to be $0$ |
| "$f$ is bounded" | **impossible** | (d) drives $f$ above every bound |
| "$f$ is a polynomial" | **impossible** | polynomials are defined everywhere, so they have no poles |
| "$f(-1)$ is undefined" | satisfiable | route D; (c) never pinned the value |
| "$f(-1)=7$" | satisfiable | route D with a different height |
| "$f$ is differentiable at $-1$" | satisfiable | the shipped answer already is |
| "$f'<0$ on $(-\infty,0)$" | satisfiable | the shipped answer already is |
| "$\lim_{x\to-\infty}f=3$ as well" | satisfiable | route B, which has $y=3$ at both ends |
| "the graph never crosses $y=3$" | **impossible** | Family 3: the middle branch must cross it |

The last row is the interesting one, because it is impossible for a reason that lives in the routing rather than in any single condition.

### Family 9 - the reverse question: read the conditions off a given graph or formula

**Trigger.** "State the conditions this graph satisfies", or a formula plus "describe the graph".
**Tool.** Evaluate; do not translate. Find where the denominator vanishes, then check whether the numerator vanishes too.
**The trap.** Reading a hole as an asymptote, or assuming a pole is $+\infty$ on both sides without checking the sign.

Worked example: $f(x)=\dfrac{2x-6}{(x-2)^2}$.

| Reading | Value | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty}f$ | $0$ | degree $1$ over degree $2$ |
| behaviour at $x=2$ | $-\infty$ on **both** sides | the denominator is squared, and the numerator is $-2$ there |
| $f(0)$ | $-\tfrac32$ | substitute |
| $x$-intercept | $x=3$ | the numerator vanishes and the denominator does not |
| differentiability | everywhere except $x=2$ | it is a rational function |

The sign check in row two is the whole exercise.
A squared denominator alone does not give $+\infty$; the numerator's sign near the pole decides it.

### Family 10 - graph selection and elimination order

**Trigger.** "Which of the following graphs satisfies all the conditions?"
**Tool.** Eliminate on the condition that kills the most candidates first, which is almost never condition (a).
**The trap.** Checking the conditions in the printed order and running out of time.

| Order | Check | Typical kill rate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | both branches at the vertical asymptote go the **same** way | half the options |
| 2 | the kink at $x=0$ is a kink, not a smooth turn and not a break | most of the rest |
| 3 | the right tail flattens at the **stated** height | usually one more |
| 4 | the marked point and the finite limit are at the right heights | tie-breaker |

The six near-miss functions used to test this tutorial's own checker are exactly the six distractors an examiner would build: a tail at the wrong height, a value at the wrong height, a limit at the wrong height, no asymptote at all, a smooth join, and a broken join.
Each fails exactly one condition and passes the other four, which is what makes a good distractor.

### Family 11 - a formula is given; does it satisfy the conditions?

**Trigger.** "Verify that $f$ satisfies the conditions", or "which of these formulas works".
**Tool.** Audit condition by condition. Do not stop at the first one that passes.
**The trap.** Checking three conditions, finding them fine, and assuming the rest.

| Candidate | (a) | (b) | (c) | (d) | (e) | Verdict |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :--- |
| $\dfrac{3x^2+5\lvert x\rvert}{(x-1)^2}$ | ok | ok | ok | ok | ok | **works** - route B |
| $\dfrac{3x^2}{(x-1)^2}$ | ok | ok | **no** | ok | **no** | limit at $-1$ is $\tfrac34$; smooth at $0$ with $f'(0)=0$ |
| $3+\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$ | ok | **no** | **no** | ok | **no** | $f(0)=4$; smooth everywhere it is defined |
| $\dfrac{3x}{x+1}$ | ok | ok | **no** | **no** | **no** | its pole is at $-1$, not $1$ |

The second row is the instructive one.
It gets three of five conditions right, it looks like a serious candidate, and the two it fails are the two Q5.2 was built to test.

### Family 12 - the same conditions stated as $f'$ and $f''$ signs

**Trigger.** Conditions like $f'(x)<0$ on $[3,\infty)$ or $f''(x)>0$ on $[4,\infty)$. This is Q5.3.
**Tool.** Q5.1's Families 7 and 8, plus the routing table in Family 3 above, which is what connects a sign condition to an asymptote condition.
**The trap.** Confusing $f'<0$ ("going down") with $f<0$ ("below the axis").

| $f'$ | $f''$ | Shape | With $\lim_{x\to\infty}f=L$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $<0$ | $>0$ | falling, concave up | settles onto $y=L$ **from above** |
| $<0$ | $<0$ | falling, concave down | cannot settle; it accelerates downward |
| $>0$ | $<0$ | rising, concave down | settles onto $y=L$ **from below** |
| $>0$ | $>0$ | rising, concave up | cannot settle |

Row one is the shipped answer's right branch, and row one is also Q5.3's conditions (c), (d) and (f) taken together.
**A tail that settles onto a horizontal asymptote must be concave the opposite way from its direction of travel.** That single sentence answers most of Q5.3.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

1. **Drawing the left side of $x=1$ going down.** Condition (d) is an equality with $+\infty$, so both branches rise. A student who reaches for $\frac{1}{x-1}$ because it is the hyperbola they know loses the single most heavily signalled condition on the item. Even power in the denominator, or a piecewise patch; never $\frac{1}{x-a}$ on its own.
2. **Skipping the interpretation half.** The question opens with "Interpret in terms of the graph". A perfect sketch and no sentences is at best half of ten points.
3. **Drawing a smooth curve through the origin.** Conditions (b) and (e) do require the curve to pass through $(0,0)$ unbroken, which makes the smooth version feel right. It is not: the pen must change direction there.
4. **Breaking the graph at the origin instead.** The opposite error, and it comes from pattern-matching to Q5.1, where the conditions at $x=0$ really did force a break. The word "continuous" is the difference.
5. **Answering "no" to "is $f$ continuous at $x=0$".** The condition says continuous. Three of the four non-differentiability mechanisms are continuous anyway.
6. **Writing $f(-1)=2$ as though condition (c) said so.** It says the limit is $2$. Making $f(-1)=2$ is a legal choice, not a requirement, and a question can and does ask which it is.
7. **Believing the graph cannot cross $y=3$.** It must, on the interval $(0,1)$, if that branch is drawn unbroken. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers.
8. **Forcing the left tail onto $y=3$ as well.** Only $x\to+\infty$ was constrained. Extra work, extra risk, no marks.
9. **Letting the right branch rise.** Conditions (a) and (d) together force it to come back down. A right branch that climbs contradicts one of them, and which one it contradicts depends on where you stopped drawing.
10. **Using a cusp and calling it a corner, or the reverse.** A corner has two different **finite** one-sided slopes; a cusp has two **infinite** ones of opposite sign; a vertical tangent has two infinite ones of the same sign. `MATH265_Review.md` blurs corners and cusps into one phrase in one of its four attempts at this list.
11. **Checking differentiability with a centred difference.** $\frac{f(h)-f(-h)}{2h}$ returns a stable $-0.5$ at the shipped answer's corner, which is just the average of the two true slopes. Always test one side at a time.
12. **Putting the vertical asymptote at $x=-1$ and the finite limit at $x=1$.** The conditions are printed out of order along the $x$-axis: (c) is at $-1$ and (d) is at $1$. Sort them onto the axis before drawing.
13. **Leaving it unlabelled.** On a ten-point picture the marker is looking for five features. Dash both asymptotes, write their equations, mark the dot at the origin, and mark the height at $-1$.
14. **Failing the vertical line test.** Sweep a vertical line across the finished picture. A leftover solid dot turns a correct answer into a non-function.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
"Interpret the conditions, then sketch a single function satisfying them."
|
+-- STEP 0.  ANSWER THE FIRST SENTENCE.  For each condition write:
|            "<FEATURE NAME> at <where>, which means <what the curve does>,
|             and which does NOT constrain <what it leaves free>."
|            That is half the marks and it needs no drawing.
|
+-- STEP 1.  SORT THE CONDITIONS ONTO THE x-AXIS.
|            Tails (x -> +-inf) go in a separate pile.
|            The conditions are usually printed OUT OF ORDER.
|
+-- STEP 2.  TWO CONDITIONS AT THE SAME x?
|      no  -> they are independent.  Draw them in any order.
|      yes -> reconcile FIRST, using the matrix in Family 1.
|                f(a)=c  + continuous        -> FORCES lim = c
|                lim=L   + continuous        -> FORCES f(a) = L
|                f(a)=c  + lim=L, c != L     -> displaced dot, NOT continuous
|                f(a)=c  + lim DNE           -> break with an isolated dot
|                cont    + not differentiable-> corner / cusp / vertical tangent
|                lim=+inf+ continuous        -> IMPOSSIBLE
|                f(a) undef + continuous     -> IMPOSSIBLE
|                diff    + not diff          -> IMPOSSIBLE
|             If impossible: name the two conditions and draw NOTHING.
|
+-- STEP 3.  TRANSLATE.  (Q5.1 Families 0-8 hold the full dictionary.)
|                lim = +-infinity AT a  -> VERTICAL ASYMPTOTE at a
|                    EQUALITY  "= +inf"  -> BOTH branches UP.  Even power.
|                    DENIAL    "DNE"     -> branches DISAGREE.  Odd power.
|                lim at +-infinity      -> HORIZONTAL ASYMPTOTE, THAT END ONLY
|                continuous + not diff  -> CORNER is the cheapest of three
|
+-- STEP 4.  DRAW THE FRAME ONLY.  Axes.  Dashed line for each asymptote,
|            labelled with its equation.  A tick at every constrained x.
|
+-- STEP 5.  PLACE THE MARKS.  Solid dot where a value exists, hollow circle
|            where a limit is approached but not attained.
|            EXACTLY ONE solid dot on any vertical line.
|
+-- STEP 6.  ROUTE BETWEEN THE MARKS.  For each gap ask what is FORCED:
|                mark -> +inf on a continuous branch: it crosses EVERY height
|                        above the mark, asymptote heights included.
|                +inf -> horizontal asymptote L: the branch must come DOWN.
|                falling onto an asymptote  -> it is concave UP, from above.
|                rising onto an asymptote   -> it is concave DOWN, from below.
|
+-- STEP 7.  CHECK, condition by condition, in the order given.
|            lim at inf = L   -> read f at 3 growing x.  The GAP must SHRINK.
|            f(a) = b         -> exactly one solid dot, at the right height.
|            lim at a = L     -> read the height EACH SIDE.  Both near L.
|            lim at a = +inf  -> cover each half.  BOTH branches must go UP.
|            cont + not diff  -> heights each side SAME, slopes each side
|                                DIFFERENT.  Never a centred difference.
|            is it a function? -> vertical line test, everywhere.
|
+-- STEP 8.  LABEL.  Both asymptote equations, the height at every marked
             point, the word "corner" at the kink.  An unlabelled correct
             sketch and an unlabelled wrong one look the same to a marker.
```
