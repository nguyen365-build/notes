## 0. The 30-second version

```
   a is given in PIECES, so build v and x one piece at a time.

   PHASE 1  a = 4 on [0,2]   ->  v = 4t          ->  x = 2t^2
            hand off the END values:  v(2) = 8 m/s,  x(2) = 8 m
   PHASE 2  a = 0 on [2,T]   ->  v = 8 (constant) ->  x = 8 + 8(t-2)
            the 8 and the 8 are NOT decoration, they are the initial conditions

   FINISH   8 + 8(T-2) = 100  ->  T - 2 = 92/8 = 11.5  ->  T = 13.5 s
```

The whole technique is the third line.
The end state of one phase is the initial condition of the next, and carrying it across is the mark.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2a. The two integrations, and their two constants

$$v(t) = v(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} a(s)\,ds \qquad x(t) = x(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t} v(s)\,ds$$

Written this way the constants are not something you remember to add.
They are the lower end of the integral, and they are already in the formula.
This is the single most useful reframing on the page: **if you write the definite-integral form, you cannot forget the constant, because the constant is the $t_0$ term.**

### 2b. What is continuous and what is not

| Quantity | At a phase boundary | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $a$ | may **jump** | it is the given data, and the data changes |
| $v$ | must be **continuous** | it is an integral of a bounded function |
| $x$ | must be **continuous**, and smooth | it is an integral of a continuous function |
| $v'$ | may jump | $v' = a$, and $a$ jumped |

At $t = 2$ the acceleration falls from $4$ to $0$, a genuine jump of $4$.
Velocity does not jump: $v(2) = 8$ whether you compute it from the phase-1 formula $4t$ or the phase-2 formula, which is the constant $8$.
Position does not jump either: $x(2) = 8$ from $2t^2$ and $8$ from $8 + 8(t-2)$.

A body cannot teleport, and it cannot change speed instantaneously without infinite force.
Those two sentences are the physical content of "$x$ and $v$ are continuous".

### 2c. The picture

On a velocity-time graph the motion is a straight ramp from $(0,0)$ to $(2,8)$, then a horizontal line at height $8$.
Distance is area under that graph.
The ramp encloses a triangle of area $\tfrac12 \cdot 2 \cdot 8 = 8$, and the rest of the race is a rectangle of height $8$.
So $8 + 8(T-2) = 100$, which is the same equation the calculus gives.

### 2d. The half-ramp law

This one is worth memorising because it turns the whole question into one line.

> If a body starts from rest, accelerates uniformly for $T_1$ seconds to a top speed $v_{\text{top}}$, and then cruises to the line at distance $L$, then
> $$T = \frac{L}{v_{\text{top}}} + \frac{T_1}{2}.$$

The ramp costs exactly **half its own duration**, and the acceleration does not appear at all.
Here that is $\tfrac{100}{8} + \tfrac{2}{2} = 12.5 + 1 = 13.5$.
Verified at 140 grid points over seven accelerations, five ramp durations and four race lengths, with 0 violations.
It fails, correctly, when the line is crossed *inside* the ramp, and a control confirms that.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Draw the timeline.** Mark every boundary the stem names, and write the acceleration on each interval.
> 2. **Write the initial state.** "From the starting block" means $v(0) = 0$ and $x(0) = 0$. Say so in words.
> 3. **Do phase 1.** Integrate $a$ to get $v$, integrate $v$ to get $x$, using the initial state.
> 4. **Evaluate at the boundary.** Compute $v(t_1)$ and $x(t_1)$ and write both down on their own line. This line is the hand-off, and it is the line the marker is looking for.
> 5. **Do phase 2 with those two numbers as its initial state.** Not zero. The numbers from step 4.
> 6. **Repeat for every later phase.**
> 7. **Find the phase the finish line falls in**, then solve within that phase only.
> 8. **Check** against the sandwich in section 6 before writing the answer down.

### The classify table: which phase does the finish line fall in

| Compute | If | Then |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x(t_1)$ | $x(t_1) \ge L$ | the race ends inside phase 1, solve the quadratic |
| $x(t_1)$, $x(t_2)$ | $x(t_1) < L \le x(t_2)$ | the race ends in phase 2, solve there |
| the final phase's limit | $\lim x < L$ | **she never finishes** and that is the answer |

Do not skip the third row.
If a later phase decelerates, the runner can stop short, and the honest answer is a distance, not a time.

## 4. Q19.9 worked four ways

### Route A - phase by phase, indefinite integrals with named constants

Phase 1, on $0 \le t \le 2$, with $a = 4$.
$v = \int 4\,dt = 4t + C_1$, and $v(0) = 0$ gives $C_1 = 0$, so $v = 4t$.
$x = \int 4t\,dt = 2t^2 + C_2$, and $x(0) = 0$ gives $C_2 = 0$, so $x = 2t^2$.
At the boundary, $v(2) = 8$ m/s and $x(2) = 8$ m.

Phase 2, on $t \ge 2$, with $a = 0$.
$v = \int 0\,dt = C_3$, and $v(2) = 8$ gives $C_3 = 8$, so $v = 8$.
$x = \int 8\,dt = 8t + C_4$, and $x(2) = 8$ gives $16 + C_4 = 8$, so $C_4 = -8$ and $x = 8t - 8$.
Setting $x(T) = 100$ gives $8T = 108$, so $T = 13.5$ s.

Note how ugly $C_4 = -8$ is, and how easy it is to get its sign wrong.
Route B removes that risk entirely.

### Route B - phase by phase, definite integrals with a shifted clock

Phase 2, measured from the boundary with $u = t - 2$:
$$x(2+u) = x(2) + \int_0^{u} 8\,ds = 8 + 8u.$$
Set $8 + 8u = 100$, so $u = 11.5$, and $T = 2 + 11.5 = 13.5$ s.

There is no arbitrary constant to sign-check, because the initial condition is sitting in front of the integral where you cannot lose it.
**This is the route to use under exam pressure.**

### Route C - the velocity-time graph

Area of the triangle is $\tfrac12(2)(8) = 8$ m.
Remaining distance is $100 - 8 = 92$ m at a constant $8$ m/s, taking $92/8 = 11.5$ s.
Total $2 + 11.5 = 13.5$ s.

### Route D - the half-ramp law

$T = \tfrac{100}{8} + \tfrac{2}{2} = 13.5$ s, in one line.

### Route comparison

| Route | Steps | Where it goes wrong | Time under pressure |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, indefinite with constants | 8 | the sign of $C_4$; four constants to track | slowest |
| B, definite with a shifted clock | 4 | almost nothing; the constant cannot be dropped | fast |
| C, the graph | 3 | forgetting the triangle is half a rectangle | fast |
| D, the half-ramp law | 1 | applying it when the line falls inside the ramp | fastest |

**Use Route B, and check with Route C.**
Route D is a check, not a write-up: it earns no method marks because it shows no integration, and this is an integration question.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

> Take $t = 0$ at the gun, with $x$ measured along the track.
> She starts from the block, so $v(0) = 0$ and $x(0) = 0$.
>
> **Phase 1**, $0 \le t \le 2$, with $a = 4$:
> $v(t) = v(0) + \int_0^t 4\,ds = 4t$, and $x(t) = x(0) + \int_0^t 4s\,ds = 2t^2$.
> At the end of phase 1, $v(2) = 8$ m/s and $x(2) = 8$ m.
>
> **Phase 2**, $t \ge 2$, with $a = 0$.
> Her velocity is constant and equal to its value at the hand-off, $v = 8$ m/s.
> Writing $u = t - 2$ for the time since the boundary,
> $x = x(2) + \int_0^u 8\,ds = 8 + 8u$.
>
> She finishes when $x = 100$, so $8 + 8u = 100$, giving $u = 11.5$ s.
> Her time for the race is $T = 2 + 11.5 = \mathbf{13.5}$ seconds.
>
> Check: her top speed is 8 m/s, so she cannot beat $100/8 = 12.5$ s, and she does at least as well as standing still for 2 s and then cruising, which would take 14.5 s. The answer lies between them.

The two sentences that earn the method marks are "$v(2) = 8$ m/s and $x(2) = 8$ m" and "her velocity is constant and equal to its value at the hand-off".
Everything else is arithmetic.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The sandwich, which is the strongest check on the page

Her top speed is 8 m/s, reached only at the end of phase 1 and never exceeded.

- **Lower bound.** If she had run the whole race at 8 m/s she would take $100/8 = 12.5$ s. She cannot beat that.
- **Upper bound.** If she had stood still for the first 2 s and then cruised at 8 m/s she would take $2 + 12.5 = 14.5$ s. She does better than that, because she was moving during the ramp.

So $12.5 < T < 14.5$.

This is not merely a check.

> **The midpoint of the sandwich is the answer, exactly, and always.**
> $(12.5 + 14.5)/2 = 13.5$, and this identity was verified across a grid of accelerations, ramp durations and race lengths with 0 violations.

It is the half-ramp law wearing a different hat: the true time sits exactly half a ramp-duration above the cruise-only time.

Measured against the wrong-answer census, the sandwich rejects **25 of the 26** wrong answers that are numbers at all.

### The other checks, ranked by what they actually catch

| Check | Rejects | Verdict |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| top-speed sandwich, $12.5 < T < 14.5$ | **25 of 26** | the one to run |
| $T > 100/8 = 12.5$ alone | 18 of 26 | most of the sandwich's power is in this arm |
| average speed $100/T \le 8$ | 13 of 26 | the same idea, stated as a rate |
| $T < 14.5$ alone | 7 of 26 | weak on its own |
| $T > 2$, longer than phase 1 | 1 of 26 | nearly useless |
| positivity, $T > 0$ | **0 of 26** | completely inert |

Positivity is inert here, which is worth knowing because it is the check most students reach for first.
Every wrong method on this stem produces a positive time.

### The one survivor

The single wrong answer the sandwich cannot reject is $14.0$ s, from computing $x(2)$ as $\tfrac12 a t = 4$ instead of $\tfrac12 a t^2 = 8$.
It sits inside the physically possible band, so no plausibility check can catch it.
The only defence is the dimensional one in section 8: $\tfrac12 a t$ is a velocity, not a length.

### Numbers worth memorising

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| top speed $v(2)$ | 8 m/s |
| distance covered in the ramp $x(2)$ | 8 m |
| distance left | 92 m |
| time in phase 2 | 11.5 s |
| answer | 13.5 s |
| sandwich | 12.5 s to 14.5 s |

That the top speed and the ramp distance are both 8 is a coincidence of this stem, not a rule.
It comes from $\tfrac12 a T_1^2 = a T_1$ when $T_1 = 2$, which holds for any $a$ but only at that one duration.

## 10. Continuity, asserted rather than assumed

It is easy to write "$v$ and $x$ are continuous" and never check it, and a check that would also pass on a function with no jump at all is not a check.

Both arms were run here, together with their controls.

| Assertion | Result |
| :--- | :--- |
| $v(2^-) = v(2^+) = 8$, computed from both phase formulas | holds exactly |
| $x(2^-) = x(2^+) = 8$, computed from both phase formulas | holds exactly |
| $a(2^+) - a(2^-) = -4$, a genuine jump | holds exactly |
| CONTROL: the same continuity arms on a smooth decoy $a \equiv 4$ | they **pass**, so continuity alone proves nothing |
| CONTROL: the jump arm on that decoy | it **rejects**, as it must |
| BAIT: restart $v$ at 0 in phase 2 | rejected by the $v$-continuity arm |
| BAIT: restart $x$ at 0 in phase 2 | rejected by the $x$-continuity arm |

The middle control is the important one.
A smooth acceleration also has continuous $v$ and $x$, so the continuity check by itself cannot tell a piecewise problem from an ordinary one.
The jump arm is what makes the pair meaningful, and without it the whole test is vacuous.

Across the twelve-instance variation catalog there are 8 phase boundaries, all 8 continuous in $v$ and $x$, and all 8 carrying a genuine jump in $a$.

## 12. The trap list, ranked by how often it costs marks

1. **Restarting phase 2 from zero.** The defining error of the question. Restarting $x$ gives 14.5; restarting $v$ gives no answer at all.
2. **Answering 11.5.** The phase-2 duration is a number you computed and believed, one line before the answer. Five different methods land there.
3. **Dropping the $\tfrac12$ or the square in $x(2)$.** Gives 16 or 4 instead of 8, and the resulting 14.0 is the one wrong answer the sandwich cannot reject.
4. **Treating $a$ as constant over the whole race.** Gives 7.0711, badly wrong but superficially reasonable for a sprint.
5. **Applying the half-ramp law without checking the phase.** Correct on Family 1, wrong on Families 2, 4 and 5.
6. **Wrong limits on the phase-2 integral.** Integrating $\int_0^t$ instead of $\int_2^t$ shifts the clock and gives 11.5.
7. **Forgetting to check whether she finishes at all** when a phase decelerates.
8. **Writing "$a = 0$ so nothing happens".** Zero acceleration means constant velocity, not zero velocity. This is the conceptual root of trap 1.
9. **Units on the answer line.** The question asks for a time; 92 and 8 are distances.

Trap 8 deserves its own sentence, because it is the misconception underneath the whole question.
Zero acceleration is not zero motion.
It is *unchanged* motion, and what is unchanged is the value that was handed across the boundary.

## 16. Closing the category, and the bank

This is the ninth of nine applications-of-integration questions, and the forty-ninth of forty-nine in the bank.

### One procedure covering all nine

Every question in this category is the same three moves.

> 1. **Name the rate and the quantity.** Something is given as a rate of change, and something else is asked for. Write down which is which.
> 2. **Build the integrand.** Turn the physical description into an expression in one variable, with the correct limits. This is where nearly all the marks are.
> 3. **Integrate, then answer the question that was asked.** Not the one you can most easily compute.

The category's whole difficulty lives in move 2.
Move 3 is Calculus I arithmetic, and move 1 takes one sentence.

### Which question teaches what

| Question | Its own contribution |
| :--- | :--- |
| Q19.1, area between a line and a parabola | orientation: which curve is on top, and the sign that hides |
| Q19.2, area between a cosine and a line | finding the crossing points first, and splitting there |
| Q19.3, net change from a rate | the Net Change Theorem itself, $\int_a^b r = Q(b)-Q(a)$ |
| Q19.4, work raising a chain | building an integrand from a physical slice |
| Q19.5, work stretching a spring | finding the constant $k$ from the data before integrating |
| Q19.6, average temperature of a rod | the average-value formula and its dividing width |
| Q19.7, find $k$ from an average value | the same formula run backwards, as an equation to solve |
| Q19.8, displacement and distance | signed versus unsigned, and splitting at the zeros of $v$ |
| Q19.9, sprinter with piecewise acceleration | integrating in pieces, and carrying the hand-off across |

Read down that column and the category has a shape.
Q19.1 and Q19.2 are geometry.
Q19.3 states the theorem the other six use.
Q19.4 and Q19.5 build integrands from physics.
Q19.6 and Q19.7 are one formula in both directions.
Q19.8 and Q19.9 are motion, and they are deliberately paired: Q19.8 gives you $v$ and hides the constants, Q19.9 gives you $a$ and makes both constants matter.

### The single sentence for the whole category

> The integral of a rate of change is the net change in the quantity.
> Everything else is deciding what the rate is, over what interval, and in what pieces.

## 17. Numerical appendix

None of this is needed to answer the exam question.
It is here because the piecewise structure creates a corner, and corners are where numerical integration quietly stops working.

### The seam is not a rounding detail

An RK4 integrator that samples the acceleration *at* $t = 2$ reads the wrong branch and loses $h \cdot \Delta a / 6$ of velocity at the boundary.
With $h = 0.5$ that is $0.3333$ m/s.

> That velocity deficit then multiplies by the entire remaining 11.5 seconds and arrives at the tape as a **3.8333 m** position error.

This is the question's own lesson in numerical form.
An error made at the hand-off does not stay at the hand-off.
It is carried into every later phase, exactly as the correct value is.

The same defect on a Simpson rule leaves $\int_0^2 a\,dt$ short by 0.006667 instead of returning 8.

### Quadrature order across the boundary, measured

Nothing here was inherited from an earlier question in the queue; all of it was measured on this stem.

The corner in $v$ sits at $t = 2$ on $[0, 13.5]$, which is a fraction $4/27$ of the interval.
Its node index is therefore $4n/27$, and the offset $s$ of the corner from its enclosing Simpson pair's midpoint takes 27 distinct values.

The local Simpson error on the pair containing a corner in a piecewise-linear integrand, verified against the rule at 180 points spanning both signs of $s$, is
$$E(s) = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)h^2}{6}\,(3s \mp 1)(s \mp 1),$$
taking the upper sign for $s \ge 0$ and the lower for $s < 0.$
Its roots are at $\lvert s\rvert = 1/3$ and $\lvert s\rvert = 1$.

| Regime | Condition | Order |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| corner on a pair boundary, $\lvert s\rvert = 1$ | $n \equiv 0 \pmod{54}$ | **exact** |
| corner at the error's other root, $\lvert s\rvert = 1/3$ | $n \equiv 18$ or $36 \pmod{54}$ | **exact** |
| generic | everything else | **2** |
| split at the corner | any $n$ | **exact** |
| the same $v$ on $[0, 4\pi]$, corner irrationally placed | any $n$ | **2**, never exact |

So the exact residues are $0$, $18$ and $36$ modulo 54, which is **exactly one even $n$ in nine**, derived and then measured at 0.1107 against $3/27$.
The generic order was confirmed by two independent instruments: an $\text{err} \times n^2$ band over 400 values of $n$ that stays inside $[16, 104]$, and a least-squares slope of $-1.9978$.

Two cautions that were measured rather than assumed.

- **A consecutive-ratio order lies here.** Taking $\log_2$ of successive errors gives $-1.1415$, $0.3907$, $-0.6242$, $-0.6829$, none of which is near 2, because the error coefficient depends on an alignment that changes with $n$. Use a scaled-error band over many $n$, not a ratio between two.
- **Simpson buys nothing over the trapezoid at a corner.** Both read order 2. A control confirms Simpson is exact on a cubic and reads order 4 on a smooth sine, so the rule is sound; it is the corner that sets the order.

### The jump costs one more order than the corner

Integrating $a$ itself, which jumps rather than kinks, Simpson reads order **1**.

| Integrand | Behaviour at the seam | Simpson's order |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $a$ | jumps | 1 |
| $v$ | corner, continuous | 2 |
| smooth | nothing | 4 |

The practical rule needs no theory at all: **split the integral at the boundary and every rule becomes exact.**
At $n = 400$ the unsplit Simpson rule on $v$ carries an error of $1.25 \times 10^{-4}$ metres, and splitting at $t = 2$ takes it to zero for the same number of function evaluations.
