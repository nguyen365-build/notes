# Q5.1 - Sketch a function from four conditions

**The question (MT1 Q10, 5 points).**
Sketch the graph of a single function $f(x)$ that satisfies all of the conditions listed below.

- the limit $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ does not exist.
- $f(0) = 0.$
- $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = -1.$
- the function $f$ is not differentiable at $x = -2.$

**The answer.**
There is no unique answer, and that is the point.
Any function with these four features scores full marks.
This tutorial ships one, chosen because every feature is visible and every number is round.

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
\lvert x+2 \rvert + 1, & x < 0\\[4pt]
0, & x = 0\\[4pt]
-1 + \dfrac{3}{x+1}, & x > 0
\end{cases}$$

$$\text{corner }(-2,1)
\quad
\lim_{x\to0^-}f = 3
\quad
f(0)=0
\quad
\lim_{x\to0^+}f = 2
\quad
\text{crosses at }(2,0)
\quad
y=-1 \text{ as } x\to\infty$$

**What to draw, and what to type:**

| what is being asked for | what you produce |
| :--- | :--- |
| the sketch itself | the labelled graph in section 5, with **two open circles** at $(0,3)$ and $(0,2)$ and **one solid dot** at $(0,0)$ |
| "give a formula" | `abs(x+2)+1` for `x<0`, `0` at `x=0`, `-1+3/(x+1)` for `x>0` |
| the horizontal asymptote | `y=-1` |
| the left and right limits at $0$ | `3` and `2` |
| the two-sided limit at $0$ | `DNE` |
| where $f$ fails to be differentiable | `x=-2` |
| the reason it fails there | `corner` |
| is $f$ continuous at $-2$? | `yes` |
| is $f$ continuous at $0$? | `no` |

> **The one sentence that decides this question.**
> The four conditions live at **three different places** on the $x$-axis, so they cannot fight each other.
> Draw them one at a time, at $x=-2$, at $x=0$ and far to the right, and then join up whatever is left over however you like.

## 0. The 30-second version

This is the cheapest 5 points on the paper, and it is not an algebra question at all.
It is a **translation** question: four sentences in limit notation, four features on a picture.

```
Sketch f with:  lim_{x->0} f DNE ;  f(0)=0 ;  lim_{x->inf} f = -1 ;  f not diff at -2.

STEP 1  PUT THE THREE LOCATIONS ON THE AXIS FIRST.   -2 . . . 0 . . . . . . . -> +inf
        They do not interact.  Nothing you do at -2 can break anything at 0.

STEP 2  x = -2   "not differentiable"  ->  cheapest legal picture is a CORNER.
        Draw a V.   Vertex (-2, 1).   Arms of slope -1 and +1.

STEP 3  x = 0    two conditions at once, and they fight ON PURPOSE:
           "lim DNE"  -> the graph must BREAK here          -> two OPEN circles
           "f(0) = 0" -> the graph must HAVE a value here   -> one SOLID dot at (0,0)
        Left branch arrives at height 3.  Right branch starts at height 2.  Dot at 0.
        Three marks in one vertical line.  That single line is 3 of the 5 points.

STEP 4  x -> +inf  "limit = -1"  ->  HORIZONTAL ASYMPTOTE y = -1 on the RIGHT.
        Draw it as a dashed line and have the right branch settle onto it.
        -1 + 3/(x+1) does this and crosses the x-axis at (2,0) on the way.

STEP 5  LABEL.  Axes, the three heights at x=0, the corner, the dashed y=-1.
        An unlabelled correct picture and an unlabelled wrong picture look identical.

CHECK   Cover the graph left of -1: does it break at 0 and flatten onto -1?   yes
        Is there exactly ONE dot on the vertical line x=0?                    yes
        Does any vertical line meet the graph twice?                          no
```

Five steps, no calculator, no algebra.
If you drew the vertical line at $x=0$ with two open circles and one solid dot, you have most of the marks already.

## 1. What this question is really testing

Item 10 is worth 5 of the 64 points on Midterm 1, the same as the exact-cosine question and the differentials question.
It is the last item on the paper, it takes ninety seconds, and it is the one people leave blank because it looks like it needs an idea.

It does not need an idea.
It needs you to know what four pieces of notation **mean**, and the examiner is checking exactly that.

| Reflex | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- |
| "$\lim$ does not exist" is a statement about the two **sides** disagreeing | Q3.1, Q3.2, Q3.3, every DNE limit in the course |
| A limit and a function value are **different things** | Q3.2, Q4.1's hole at $(4,\tfrac58)$, the definition of continuity |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty} f = L$ means a horizontal asymptote | Q3.3a, Q4.1, Final Exam curve sketching |
| Non-differentiable means corner, cusp, vertical tangent **or** break | Q2.2 (the corner factory $-3\lvert x-2\rvert$), critical points, the Mean Value Theorem |
| Open circle versus solid dot | every piecewise graph you will ever draw |
| Reading a condition and drawing the feature | Q5.2, Q5.3, Final Exam Q4 |
| Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous, and never the reverse | Q7 (the derivative from its definition), Family 10 below |

**Why five points and not two.**
Compare the neighbours: the tangent-line question is 4 points, the related-rates cone is 5, the differentials question is 5.
This item is priced the same as a full related-rates problem, for a picture.
That tells you the marks are spread across the **four conditions separately**, roughly one each, with the fifth for producing a coherent single function rather than four disconnected fragments.

That pricing changes how you draw.
Do not spend your time making one feature beautiful.
Spend it making sure all four are unambiguously present and **labelled**, because a marker awarding one point per condition needs to find each one.

**Format warning.**
The sample paper is a written, constructed response and asks for a sketch.
The live delivery is Möbius, which is answer-only and machine-marked, and a machine cannot mark a freehand curve.
So the live version almost certainly asks for the same content in a form a machine can read: boxes for the one-sided limits, a box for the asymptote, a box naming the point of non-differentiability, or a multiple-choice item showing four candidate graphs to pick between.
Section 7 prepares for both, and the reasoning behind that expectation is stated there rather than presented as a fact about the platform.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

Everything in this question is recall of definitions.
There is nothing here to derive under pressure, so if you do not have it cold you cannot get it at all.

### 2.1 The translation dictionary

This single table is the question.
Learn it in both directions, because Q5.3 asks you to go right-to-left ("interpret in terms of the graph") and Q5.1 asks you to go left-to-right.

| Condition | What it says | What you draw |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(a) = b$ | the point $(a,b)$ is **on** the graph | a **solid dot** at $(a,b)$ |
| $f(a)$ undefined | there is no point above $x=a$ | an **open circle**, or nothing at all |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f = L$ | both sides approach height $L$ | the curve **converges** to height $L$ from both sides |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-} f = L$ | only the left side | the left branch arrives at $(a,L)$ |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f$ DNE | the sides **disagree**, or one blows up, or it oscillates | a **break**: jump, pole, or oscillation |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f = \infty$ | **both** sides run to $+\infty$ | a vertical asymptote, **same** direction both sides |
| $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty} f = L$ | the far right settles at height $L$ | a horizontal asymptote $y=L$ **on the right only** |
| $f$ continuous at $a$ | $\lim_{x\to a} f = f(a)$, and both exist | draw through the point without lifting the pen |
| $f$ not differentiable at $a$ | no single tangent slope exists | a corner, a cusp, a vertical tangent, or a break |
| $f'(x) > 0$ on $I$ | the graph rises left to right on $I$ | an increasing arc |
| $f''(x) > 0$ on $I$ | the slope itself is rising | a cup, concave up |

Three of the eleven rows are Q5.1's four conditions, and the fourth condition is the "not differentiable" row.
Nothing else in the table is needed for this question, but every other row is needed for Q5.2 or Q5.3.

### 2.2 The limit and the value are independent

This is the idea the first two conditions are built to test, and it is the one students most often do not believe.

$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ is about what happens **near** $a$.
$f(a)$ is about what happens **at** $a$.
The definition of a limit deliberately excludes the point itself, so the two can be anything relative to each other.

All four combinations are legal, and you should be able to draw each one in three seconds.

| $\lim_{x\to a} f$ | $f(a)$ | The picture | Name |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| exists, $=L$ | exists, $=L$ | one smooth point | **continuous** |
| exists, $=L$ | undefined | an open circle at $(a,L)$ | a **hole** |
| exists, $=L$ | exists, $\neq L$ | an open circle at $(a,L)$ and a solid dot elsewhere | a **removable discontinuity**, a displaced dot |
| does **not** exist | exists | a break, with a solid dot somewhere on the line $x=a$ | a **jump** or an infinite discontinuity, with a value |

**Q5.1 asks for row 4.**
"$\lim_{x\to0} f$ does not exist" plus "$f(0)=0$" together say: break the graph at $0$, and then put a dot at the origin anyway.

The single most common wrong answer to this question is a graph that passes smoothly through the origin.
That drawing satisfies $f(0)=0$ perfectly and destroys the first condition, because a curve you can draw through $(0,0)$ without lifting the pen has $\lim_{x\to0}f = 0$, which exists.

### 2.3 The three ways a two-sided limit can fail

"Does not exist" is not one thing.
Knowing all three is what lets you choose the easiest one to draw, and it is also what Q3.2 keeps asking about.

| Mechanism | What the sides do | Cheapest example | Drawable? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Jump** | both sides finite, and different | $f(x)=\begin{cases}-1 & x<0\\ 1 & x>0\end{cases}$ | **yes, trivially** |
| **Blow-up** | at least one side runs to $\pm\infty$ | $\dfrac1x$ at $0$; $\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ at $0$ | yes, but taller |
| **Oscillation** | the sides settle on nothing | $\sin\!\left(\tfrac1x\right)$ at $0$ | not really |

Use the jump.
It is two line segments and it takes four seconds.

**The blow-up route is legal here, and the course notes say it is not.**
`MATH265\MATH265.md` claims that "since $f(0)=0$ is defined, a vertical asymptote is ruled out".
That is false.
$f(x) = -\dfrac{\lvert x+2\rvert}{x}$ for $x \neq 0$, together with $f(0)=0$, has a vertical asymptote at $x=0$, still has $f(0)=0$ defined, and satisfies all four conditions.
It is Route G in section 4 and it was verified numerically.
Defining a value at a point does not prevent the function from blowing up around it; the value is one point, and the limit does not look at that point.

### 2.4 The four ways to fail differentiability

$f$ is differentiable at $a$ when $\displaystyle\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ exists as a single finite number.
There are exactly four ways for that to fail, and the exam wants you to know you may pick any of them.

| Mechanism | One-sided slopes | Continuous there? | Cheapest formula |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Corner** | two different **finite** values | **yes** | $\lvert x-a \rvert$ |
| **Cusp** | $+\infty$ on one side, $-\infty$ on the other | **yes** | $\lvert x-a \rvert^{2/3}$ |
| **Vertical tangent** | $+\infty$ on **both** sides (or $-\infty$ on both) | **yes** | $\sqrt[3]{x-a}$ |
| **Discontinuity** | the quotient has nothing to converge to | **no** | any jump |

**Use the corner.** $\lvert x+2 \rvert$ takes two seconds and everyone can draw a V.

Two notes that separate a confident answer from a lucky one.

**A corner is not a cusp, and the course review file conflates them.**
`MATH265_Review.md` writes "Not Differentiable at: Sharp corners (cusps), vertical tangents, or points of discontinuity", which reads as though a corner and a cusp are the same thing.
A later passage in the same file gets it right: a cusp is a point where $f$ is continuous but the one-sided derivative limits are $+\infty$ and $-\infty$.
A corner has two different **finite** slopes; a cusp has two **infinite** ones.
Both are correct answers to this question, so nothing is lost on Q5.1, but the words are not interchangeable and Q5.3-style questions do ask you to name the feature.

**Three of the four mechanisms are continuous.**
"Not differentiable" does not mean "broken".
Only the fourth row breaks the graph, and Q5.1 does not require a break at $-2$, so you are free to choose.
Q5.2 condition (e), "continuous but not differentiable at $x=0$", is the version that removes the fourth row and forces one of the first three.

### 2.5 A horizontal asymptote at one end says nothing about the other

The third condition is $\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = -1$, with $+\infty$ only.
Nothing at all is said about $x \to -\infty$.

That is a freedom, not an omission.
The left half of your graph may run up to $+\infty$, down to $-\infty$, settle on some completely different level, or oscillate.
The shipped answer's left branch runs up to $+\infty$, because $\lvert x+2\rvert + 1$ does, and that is fine.

Three facts about horizontal asymptotes that this question quietly relies on.

**A graph may cross its horizontal asymptote.**
It is an end-behaviour statement, not a fence.
$-1 + \dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ crosses $y=-1$ at every multiple of $\pi$ and still has $y=-1$ as its horizontal asymptote.

**A graph may sit exactly on its horizontal asymptote.**
The constant function $f(x) = -1$ has $\lim_{x\to\infty}f = -1$ by the definition, since the error is zero and zero is close to zero.
That makes the flat-tail answer in Route B fully legal, and it is the cheapest thing to draw on the whole paper.

**Only the right end is constrained.**
If you find yourself drawing $y=-1$ as a dashed line across the whole picture and forcing the left branch onto it too, you are doing unrequested work and adding a chance to contradict something.

## 3. The universal procedure

This works for Q5.1, Q5.2, Q5.3, and every reworded version in section 8.

> 1. **List the conditions and write the $x$-value of each one beside it.** Anything at $\pm\infty$ goes in a separate "tail" pile. This step alone prevents the commonest error, which is satisfying one condition in a way that destroys another.
> 2. **Sort the $x$-values and check whether any two conditions sit at the SAME place.** Conditions at different places are independent and can be drawn in any order. Conditions at the same place must be reconciled before you draw, and that is where the difficulty always is.
> 3. **Translate each condition into a feature** using the table in 2.1. Write the feature, not the notation: "corner at $-2$", not "$f$ not differentiable at $-2$".
> 4. **Check for a contradiction before drawing.** If two conditions at the same point demand incompatible things, the honest answer is "no such function exists" plus the reason. This is Family 10, and examiners do set it.
> 5. **Draw the frame first: axes, the asymptote as a dashed line, and a mark on the axis at every constrained $x$-value.** Do not draw any curve yet.
> 6. **Place the point conditions.** Solid dots for values that exist, open circles for limits that are approached but not attained. Do this before the curves, because the dots are what the curves have to hit.
> 7. **Join up with the simplest curve that hits everything.** Straight lines and V shapes are allowed and are usually best. There is no requirement that the function be given by one formula, be smooth, or be pretty.
> 8. **Label everything, then re-read the conditions one at a time against your picture.** Section 6 is that re-read.

Step 2 is where the marks are.
In Q5.1 the constrained places are $-2$, $0$ and the right tail, and only $x=0$ carries two conditions.

**Classify before you start.**
One look at a condition tells you which family you are in and therefore what to draw.

| The condition you are given | What it forces | Family |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(a)=b$ | a solid dot | 0 |
| $f(a)$ undefined | an open circle | 0 |
| $\lim_{x\to a} f = L$, finite | convergence to height $L$ | 1 |
| $\lim_{x\to a} f$ DNE, sides finite | a **jump** | **2** |
| $\lim_{x\to a} f = \pm\infty$ | a vertical asymptote | 3 |
| $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty} f = L$ | a horizontal asymptote | **4** |
| not differentiable at $a$ | corner, cusp, vertical tangent or break | **5** |
| continuous **but** not differentiable at $a$ | corner, cusp or vertical tangent only | 6 |
| $f' > 0$ or $f' < 0$ on an interval | monotonic arc | 7 |
| $f'' > 0$ or $f'' < 0$ on an interval | concavity | 8 |
| a one-sided limit given alone | one branch only | 9 |
| two conditions that cannot both hold | say why, do **not** draw | 10 |
| a graph is given, describe it | read the table backwards | 11 |
| a formula is given, sketch $f$ and $f'$ | evaluate, do not translate | 12 |
| find $a,b$ making $f$ continuous or differentiable | match values, then match slopes | 13 |

**Q5.1 is Families 2, 0, 4 and 5, one per condition, and no two of them collide.**

## 4. Q5.1 worked seven ways

Every one of these seven functions satisfies all four conditions.
All seven were run through the same automated audit and all seven passed, which is the honest way to say "there is no unique answer".

### Route A - jump, corner, hyperbola (use this one)

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
\lvert x+2 \rvert + 1, & x < 0\\
0, & x = 0\\
-1 + \dfrac{3}{x+1}, & x > 0
\end{cases}$$

**At $x=-2$.** $\lvert x+2 \rvert$ has a corner. The left slope is $-1$, the right slope is $+1$, they are different and both finite, so no tangent line exists and $f$ is not differentiable. It is still continuous there, with $f(-2)=1$.

**At $x=0$.** The left branch arrives at $\lvert 0+2 \rvert + 1 = 3$. The right branch starts at $-1 + \tfrac31 = 2$. Since $3 \neq 2$ the two-sided limit does not exist. And $f(0)=0$ was defined separately, so the origin is on the graph as an isolated dot below both branches.

**As $x \to \infty$.** $\dfrac{3}{x+1} \to 0$, so $f \to -1$. The asymptote is approached from above, and never reached, because $\dfrac{3}{x+1}$ is never zero.

**Why the numbers are these numbers.** The $+1$ lifts the V so the left limit is a whole number, $3$. The $3$ in the numerator makes the right limit $2$ and puts the $x$-intercept at the whole number $x=2$. Neither choice matters mathematically; both make the picture easy to label.

### Route B - the cheapest legal answer

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
\lvert x+2 \rvert - 1, & x < 0\\
0, & x = 0\\
-1, & x > 0
\end{cases}$$

The right branch is the horizontal line $y=-1$ itself.
$\lim_{x\to\infty} f = -1$ holds because the error is exactly zero forever, and zero is a perfectly good limit.
Left limit $1$, right limit $-1$, $f(0)=0$, corner at $(-2,-1)$.

This is a V, a dot and a ray.
It takes about ten seconds and it is worth full marks.
The only reason it is not the shipped answer is that a right branch that **approaches** an asymptote demonstrates that you know what an asymptote is, and a right branch that **is** the asymptote leaves a marker guessing.

### Route C - exponential tail instead of a hyperbola

Replace the right branch with $-1 + 3e^{-x}$.
Same left limit $3$, same right limit $2$, same asymptote $y=-1$, and it crosses the $x$-axis at $x=\ln 3 \approx 1.0986$ instead of at $2$.

Strictly worse for an exam, because the crossing point is no longer a round number and you cannot label it without a calculator.
Worth knowing because $e^{-x}$ is the other standard way to reach a horizontal asymptote and it appears constantly in later units.

### Route D - a cusp at $-2$ instead of a corner

Replace the left branch with $\lvert x+2 \rvert^{2/3} + 1$.
Still continuous at $-2$, still not differentiable, but now the one-sided slopes run to $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ rather than to $-1$ and $+1$.

Legal, and strictly harder to draw well.
Its only real use is as the answer to a question that says "not differentiable at $-2$, and the one-sided derivatives are infinite", which is a cusp being named without the word.

### Route D' - a vertical tangent at $-2$

Replace the left branch with $\sqrt[3]{x+2} + 1$.
Both one-sided slopes run to $+\infty$, so the curve passes through $(-2,1)$ going straight up.

The distinction from Route D is worth thirty seconds: a cusp turns around, a vertical tangent carries on through.
Both are continuous and neither is differentiable.

### Route E - break the graph at $-2$

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
3, & x < -2\\
1, & -2 \le x < 0\\
0, & x = 0\\
-1 + \dfrac{3}{x+1}, & x > 0
\end{cases}$$

A function that is discontinuous at $-2$ is automatically not differentiable there, because differentiability implies continuity.
So this satisfies condition four with two horizontal segments and no V at all.

It is legal, it is fast, and it is the answer a marker is least likely to expect.
It is also the answer that goes wrong if the question ever adds "and $f$ is continuous at $-2$", which Q5.2 effectively does at its own point.
Prefer the corner: it costs nothing extra and it survives that extra condition.

### Route F - oscillation at $0$ instead of a jump

Add $\sin\!\left(\tfrac1x\right)$ near zero.
The two-sided limit fails because the values do not settle: along $x = \tfrac{1}{\pi(2k+\frac12)}$ the function heads for one height and along $x = \tfrac{1}{\pi(2k+\frac32)}$ it heads for another, and the two differ by about $2$.

Correct, verified, and completely unreasonable to draw by hand.
Included because "the limit fails" and "the sides disagree" are not synonyms, and a question that says "give an example where the limit fails but neither one-sided limit exists either" is asking for exactly this.

### Route G - one formula, and a pole at zero

$$f(x) = -\frac{\lvert x+2 \rvert}{x} \quad (x \neq 0), \qquad f(0) = 0$$

**At $x=0$.** The numerator tends to $2$ and the denominator to $0$, so the function blows up: $+\infty$ from the left and $-\infty$ from the right. The limit does not exist. And $f(0)=0$ was declared, so the origin really is on the graph, sitting alone between two infinite branches.

**At $x=-2$.** Near $-2$ the factor $\tfrac{-1}{x}$ is smooth and nonzero, so $\lvert x+2\rvert$ keeps its corner. The one-sided slopes are $-\tfrac12$ and $+\tfrac12$.

**At the ends.** For large positive $x$, $\lvert x+2\rvert = x+2$, so $f \to -1$. For large negative $x$, $\lvert x+2\rvert = -(x+2)$, so $f \to +1$. Two different horizontal asymptotes, which is allowed because nothing was said about the left end.

This is the route that disproves the course note quoted in 2.3.
It is also the answer to give if a question ever says "do it with a single formula".

### The route comparison

| | A jump + corner + hyperbola | B flat tail | C exponential tail | D cusp | E break at $-2$ | F oscillation | G one formula |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Satisfies all four conditions | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Seconds to draw | 40 | **10** | 45 | 70 | 25 | never | 90 |
| Shows you understand "asymptote" | **yes** | no, it sits on it | yes | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| Shows you understand "corner" | **yes** | yes | yes | no, a cusp | no, a break | yes | yes |
| Survives "and $f$ is continuous at $-2$" | **yes** | yes | yes | yes | **no** | yes | yes |
| Round numbers to label | **yes** | yes | no, $\ln 3$ | no | yes | no | yes |
| Risk | none | a marker may want to see approach | unlabelable crossing | hard to draw | fails the extra condition | undrawable | hardest picture |
| Verdict | **use this one** | use if short of time | avoid | know it exists | know it exists | know it exists | use if a formula is demanded |

**Under exam pressure: V at $-2$, break at $0$ with two open circles and a solid dot, dashed $y=-1$ on the right.**
Then spend the last twenty seconds labelling, because on a five-point picture the labels are what the marker reads.

Read the vertical line at $x=0$: three marks, top to bottom, open at $3$, open at $2$, **solid at $0$**.
That column is the answer to two of the four conditions and it is the part a marker looks at first.

If the paper asks you to justify the picture, these are the sentences.
Five points wants roughly this much and no more.

> Take
> $f(x) = \lvert x+2\rvert + 1$ for $x<0$, $f(0)=0$, and $f(x) = -1 + \dfrac{3}{x+1}$ for $x>0$.
>
> **The limit at $0$ does not exist.**
> $\lim_{x\to0^-} f(x) = \lvert 2 \rvert + 1 = 3$ and $\lim_{x\to0^+} f(x) = -1 + 3 = 2$.
> The one-sided limits are both finite and unequal, so the two-sided limit does not exist.
> This is a jump discontinuity.
>
> **$f(0)=0$.**
> The value at the origin is assigned directly, so $(0,0)$ is on the graph even though neither one-sided limit is $0$.
> It is drawn as a solid dot, with open circles at $(0,3)$ and $(0,2)$.
>
> **$\lim_{x\to\infty} f(x) = -1$.**
> $\dfrac{3}{x+1} \to 0$ as $x\to\infty$, so $f(x) \to -1$ and $y=-1$ is a horizontal asymptote on the right.
> The graph approaches it from above and never reaches it, since $\dfrac{3}{x+1} \neq 0$ for all $x$.
>
> **$f$ is not differentiable at $-2$.**
> $\lvert x+2 \rvert$ has a corner at $x=-2$: the difference quotient tends to $-1$ from the left and to $+1$ from the right.
> The two one-sided derivatives differ, so $f'(-2)$ does not exist.
> Note that $f$ **is** continuous at $-2$, with $f(-2)=1$; continuity does not imply differentiability.

Four things a marker looks for that students routinely skip.

**Drawing the open circles.**
Without them the picture shows a function taking three values at $x=0$, which is not a function.
The open circles are not decoration; they are what makes the drawing legal.

**Saying the two one-sided limits and their values.**
"The limit does not exist" is the claim, not the reason.
$3 \neq 2$ is the reason, and it is one line.

**Naming the mechanism at $-2$.**
Write the word "corner".
An unlabelled kink can be read as a sloppy curve.

**Saying that $f$ is continuous at $-2$.**
It costs one clause and it proves you know the two ideas are different.
Many students believe "not differentiable" implies "broken" and this sentence shows you do not.

## 6. Verify before you commit

This is a checking question more than a drawing question, so budget twenty of your ninety seconds for the check.
Go through the conditions **one at a time, in the order given**, and point at the feature.

**Condition 1, the limit at $0$.**
Put your finger on the graph just left of $0$ and read the height.
Put it just right of $0$ and read the height.
If the two heights are the same number, you have failed, and you have failed invisibly, because a smooth graph through the origin looks fine.
For the shipped answer the heights are $3$ and $2$.

**Condition 2, $f(0)=0$.**
There must be exactly **one solid dot** on the vertical line $x=0$, and it must sit on the $x$-axis.
Count the dots. If there are two, your graph is not a function. If there are none, the value is undefined and you have answered a different question.

**Condition 3, the tail.**
Walk right and read three heights.

| $x$ | $f(x)$ | reading |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $5$ | $-1+\tfrac12 = -\tfrac12$ | heading down towards $-1$ |
| $11$ | $-1+\tfrac14 = -\tfrac34$ | closer |
| $29$ | $-1+\tfrac1{10} = -\tfrac9{10}$ | closer still, and always above $-1$ |
| $299$ | $-1+\tfrac1{100} = -\tfrac{99}{100}$ | the gap is shrinking by a factor of $10$ per decade |

The gap must **shrink**, not merely be small.
A branch that flattens out at $-0.9$ and stays there has limit $-0.9$, not $-1$, and looks almost identical on a hand-drawn sketch.

**Condition 4, the corner.**
Read the slope just left of $-2$ and just right of $-2$.
For the shipped answer they are $-1$ and $+1$: two different finite numbers, so no tangent exists.
If your slopes agree, you drew a smooth minimum and the condition fails.

**The check nobody does: is it a function at all?**
Sweep a vertical line across the whole picture.
It must meet the graph at most once everywhere, and exactly once at $x=0$ (the solid dot; the open circles are not points of the graph).
Piecewise sketches fail this more often than you would think, usually by leaving a stray filled dot behind.

### A trap in the checking itself

If you ever check differentiability by computing a **centred** difference $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}$, it will tell you that $\lvert x+2\rvert$ **is** differentiable at $-2$ with derivative $0$.
It is exactly $0$ there, for every $h$, because the two arms rise by the same amount on both sides and the symmetric quotient cancels them.
A centred difference is blind to corners.
Differentiability has to be tested **one side at a time**, which is what the definition says and what the automated check behind this document was rewritten to do.

### The numbers worth memorising for this function

| Quantity | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| corner | $(-2,\,1)$, slopes $-1$ then $+1$ |
| $\lim_{x\to0^-} f$ | $3$ (open circle) |
| $\lim_{x\to0^+} f$ | $2$ (open circle) |
| $f(0)$ | $0$ (solid dot) |
| $x$-intercept of the right branch | $(2,\,0)$ |
| horizontal asymptote | $y=-1$, right end only, approached from above |
| $f(-4),\,f(-3),\,f(-1)$ | $3,\ 2,\ 2$ |
| $f(1),\,f(5),\,f(11)$ | $\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac34$ |
| $f'(1),\,f'(2),\,f'(5)$ | $-\tfrac34,\ -\tfrac13,\ -\tfrac1{12}$ |
| right branch shape | decreasing and concave up throughout |
| left end behaviour | $f \to +\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$, and that is unconstrained |

The left branch is symmetric about $x=-2$, so $f(-3)=f(-1)=2$ and $f(-4)=f(0^-)=3$.
That symmetry is the fastest way to label four points on the V without computing anything.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

The sample paper wants a drawing.
A machine cannot mark a drawing, so on Möbius the same five points have to be collected some other way.
The two forms that can be machine-marked are **feature boxes** and **graph selection**, and preparing for both costs nothing because the content is identical.

| Rule | Do | Not |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A limit that does not exist | `DNE` | a blank box |
| A one-sided limit | `3` and `2` as separate boxes | `3,2` in one box |
| An infinite limit, if a variation has one | `infinity`, `-infinity` | `inf`, `oo`, the symbol |
| A horizontal asymptote | `y=-1` | `-1`, or `x=-1` |
| The point of non-differentiability | `x=-2` | `-2` if the box shows `x =` already |
| The mechanism | `corner` | `cusp`, which is a different thing |
| A yes/no on continuity at $-2$ | `yes` | `no`, which is the commonest slip |
| A yes/no on continuity at $0$ | `no` | `yes` |
| A piecewise formula | `abs(x+2)+1` | `|x+2|+1`, since the bars usually do not parse |
| Multiplication | `3*x` | `3x` |
| The rational tail | `-1+3/(x+1)` | `-1+3/x+1`, which parses as $-1+\frac3x+1$ |

**The single most expensive answer on this question is a graph drawn smoothly through the origin.**
It satisfies condition two, it looks tidy, and it silently destroys condition one.
Before you submit anything, read the left height and the right height at $x=0$ and confirm they are different numbers.

**The second most expensive is answering `no` to "is $f$ continuous at $x=-2$".**
The question said "not differentiable", and a corner is continuous.
Three of the four mechanisms in 2.4 are continuous, so "not differentiable" carries no information about continuity at all.

**If the item is multiple choice, eliminate rather than select.**
Take the four conditions in order and knock out candidates.
A graph that is smooth at $-2$ dies on condition four.
A graph with no dot at the origin dies on condition two.
A graph whose right end drifts up or runs off dies on condition three.
A graph that is continuous at $0$ dies on condition one.
Usually two conditions are enough to leave one survivor.

**If the item asks for the formula, bracket the whole denominator.**
`-1+3/(x+1)` is right and `-1+3/x+1` evaluates to $1.6$ at $x=5$ instead of $-0.5$.

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
The families are organised by **the tool the condition forces**, not by how the condition looks on the page.
Every function below was run through the automated condition-checker.

Read the whole catalog as one idea seen fourteen times.
Only three things ever vary.
**Where** the condition sits, **which** of the eleven dictionary rows it is, and whether two conditions land on the **same** $x$ and have to be reconciled.

### Family 0 - a value condition: one dot

**Trigger.** $f(a)=b$, or "$f(a)$ is undefined".
**Tool.** A single mark on the vertical line $x=a$. Nothing else.
**The trap.** Reading $f(0)=0$ as "the curve passes through the origin **continuously**". It says the point is on the graph, and says nothing about the two branches either side.

| Condition | The mark | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f(0)=0$ | solid dot at the origin | **Q5.1** |
| $f(0)=0$ | solid dot at the origin | Q5.2 (b) |
| $f(0)=-3$ | solid dot at $(0,-3)$ | Q5.3 (a) |
| $f(a)$ undefined | open circle, or nothing | Q4.1's hole at $\left(4,\tfrac58\right)$ |
| $f(-2)=1$ | solid dot on the corner | the shipped answer's corner |

Read this as structure.
A value condition is always exactly one dot and never constrains anything else.
It is the only kind of condition that can never be contradicted by a limit condition, because the limit does not look at the point.

### Family 1 - a finite two-sided limit: three legal pictures

**Trigger.** $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ with $L$ a number.
**Tool.** Make both branches converge to height $L$. Then decide separately what happens **at** $a$.
**The trap.** Assuming the limit condition also tells you $f(a)$. It does not, and which of the three pictures you draw is your free choice unless a second condition pins it.

| Function | $\lim_{x\to a}$ | $f(a)$ | Picture |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $x+3$ at $a=0$ | $3$ | $3$ | continuous, one dot |
| $\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}$ at $a=1$ | $2$ | undefined | a **hole** at $(1,2)$ |
| $\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{x-1}$, with $f(1)=5$ | $2$ | $5$ | open circle at $(1,2)$, solid dot at $(1,5)$ |
| Q5.2 (c): $\lim_{x\to-1} f = 2$ | $2$ | **not stated** | any of the three above |

Row 4 is the exam's own condition and it is deliberately incomplete.
Q5.2 (c) gives a limit at $x=-1$ and never mentions $f(-1)$, so all three pictures earn the mark.
Draw the continuous one; it is the fastest and it cannot contradict anything else on that paper.

### Family 2 - the limit fails with both sides finite: a jump. This is Q5.1

**Trigger.** "$\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ does not exist", with no mention of infinity.
**Tool.** Two branches arriving at two different finite heights.
**The trap.** Drawing a continuous curve because $f(a)$ was also given. The value and the limit are independent, per 2.2.

| Function | left limit | right limit | $f(a)$ | note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Q5.1's shipped answer at $0$** | $3$ | $2$ | $0$ | three marks in one column |
| $f = -1$ for $x<0$, $+1$ for $x>0$, $f(0)=0$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | $0$ | the sign function, the cheapest jump there is |
| $f = 0$ for $x<1$, $2$ for $x \ge 1$ | $0$ | $2$ | $2$ | right-continuous: the dot sits on the right branch |
| $f = x$ for $x<1$, $f(1)=3$, $2+\tfrac3x$ for $x>1$ | $1$ | $5$ | $3$ | the dot sits between the two branches |
| Route E at $-2$ | $3$ | $1$ | $1$ | a jump used to kill **differentiability**, not to kill a limit |

Read this as structure rather than as five facts.
Every row is the same picture: two heights and a dot.
What varies is only **where the dot goes** relative to the two heights, and all three positions are legal.
Row 5 is the interesting one, because it uses a jump to satisfy the *fourth* condition rather than the first.

### Family 3 - the limit fails by blowing up: a vertical asymptote

**Trigger.** $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = \infty$, or a limit that fails because a denominator vanishes.
**Tool.** A vertical dashed line at $x=a$ with the branches running off along it.
**The trap.** Writing $\lim_{x\to a} f = +\infty$ when the two sides go **opposite** ways. Then the limit does not exist at all, and "$=+\infty$" is a wrong statement rather than a shorthand.

| Function | left side | right side | is "$\lim = +\infty$" correct? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^{2}}$ | $+\infty$ | $+\infty$ | **yes** |
| $\dfrac{1}{x-1}$ | $-\infty$ | $+\infty$ | **no**, the limit does not exist |
| $-\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^{2}}$ | $-\infty$ | $-\infty$ | no, but "$=-\infty$" is correct |
| Route G, $-\dfrac{\lvert x+2\rvert}{x}$ at $0$ | $+\infty$ | $-\infty$ | no, the limit does not exist |

**Q5.2 (d) says $\lim_{x\to1} f(x) = \infty$, so it needs row 1, not row 2.**
That is the single most useful line in this family.
Drawing $\tfrac{1}{x-1}$ for Q5.2 (d) gives a graph that plunges to $-\infty$ on the left, which contradicts the condition as written.
Both sides must go **up**.

Note also that a pole at $a$ is compatible with $f(a)$ being defined, as Route G shows.
The graph then has two infinite branches and one lonely dot.

### Family 4 - limits at infinity: horizontal asymptotes

**Trigger.** $\lim_{x\to+\infty} f = L$ or $\lim_{x\to-\infty} f = L$.
**Tool.** A dashed horizontal line at height $L$, and the relevant tail settling onto it.
**The trap.** Treating the asymptote as a fence, or assuming a condition about one end says anything about the other.

| Function | $x\to+\infty$ | $x\to-\infty$ | note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $-1+\dfrac{3}{x+1}$ **(Q5.1)** | $y=-1$ | (unconstrained here) | approached from above, never reached |
| $-1$ | $y=-1$ | $y=-1$ | the graph **is** its own asymptote |
| $-1+\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$ | $y=-1$ | $y=-1$ | **crosses** $y=-1$ at every $x=k\pi$, infinitely often |
| $\dfrac{3x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}}$ | $y=3$ | $y=-3$ | **two different** horizontal asymptotes |
| $\arctan x$ | $y=\tfrac\pi2$ | $y=-\tfrac\pi2$ | the standard two-asymptote example |
| $x-1$ | none | none | the tail runs off; no horizontal asymptote |
| $3+\dfrac1x$ | $y=3$ | $y=3$ | decreasing **and** concave up, so it comes down from above |

Read this as structure.
Row 2 and row 3 are the two things students believe are forbidden and both are legal: the graph may sit on the line, and it may cross the line as often as it likes.
Row 4 is why "the horizontal asymptote", singular, is safe language only for rational functions.
Row 1 is Q5.1, and it is worth knowing that the approach is from **above**, because that is what "decreasing and concave up" looks like and it is the same shape as row 7.

### Family 5 - non-differentiable at a point: four mechanisms. This is Q5.1's fourth condition

**Trigger.** "$f$ is not differentiable at $a$", with nothing else said.
**Tool.** Pick whichever of the four is cheapest to draw. There is no wrong choice.
**The trap.** Believing the graph has to break. Three of the four are continuous.

| Mechanism | Cheapest formula at $a=-2$ | one-sided slopes | continuous at $-2$? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **corner** | $\lvert x+2 \rvert$ | $-1$ and $+1$ | **yes** |
| **cusp** | $\lvert x+2 \rvert^{2/3}$ | $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ | **yes** |
| **vertical tangent** | $\sqrt[3]{x+2}$ | $+\infty$ and $+\infty$ | **yes** |
| **discontinuity** | $3$ for $x<-2$, $1$ for $x \ge -2$ | nothing to converge to | **no** |

The four rows are generated by two independent yes/no questions.
Are the one-sided slopes finite or infinite, and if infinite, do they agree in sign?
Plus the separate question of whether the function is continuous at all.

Note that rows 1 to 3 all pass through $(-2, f(-2))$ without lifting the pen, which is what makes them legal answers to Q5.2 (e) as well.
Row 4 is the only one Q5.2 (e) forbids.

### Family 6 - continuous **but** not differentiable: Q5.2 (e)

**Trigger.** The word "continuous" appears alongside "not differentiable".
**Tool.** Rows 1 to 3 of Family 5. The discontinuity route is now illegal.
**The trap.** Using a jump anyway, because it is the fastest way to be non-differentiable.

| Function | continuous at $0$? | differentiable at $0$? | $f(0)$ | mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lvert x \rvert$ | yes | no | $0$ | corner |
| $-3\lvert x \rvert$ | yes | no | $0$ | corner, and this is Q2.2's function shifted |
| $\lvert x \rvert^{2/3}$ | yes | no | $0$ | cusp |
| $\sqrt[3]{x}$ | yes | no | $0$ | vertical tangent |
| $0$ for $x<0$, $1$ for $x \ge 0$ | **no** | no | $1$ | **illegal for Q5.2 (e)** |

Rows 1 to 4 all also satisfy $f(0)=0$, which is Q5.2 (b), so any of them answers two of Q5.2's five conditions at once.
That is not a coincidence: Q5.2 (b) and (e) both sit at $x=0$ and were written to be satisfied together by a corner at the origin.

**$\lvert x \rvert$ is the answer to Q5.2 (e) and you should be able to write it without thinking.**
Q2.2 on the same paper is a whole question about $-3\lvert x-2\rvert$, which is the same corner moved and stretched.
Owning one corner formula answers part of three separate questions.

### Family 7 - sign conditions on $f'$: monotonicity

**Trigger.** $f'(x) < 0$ on an interval, or "$f$ is increasing on $[a,b]$".
**Tool.** Draw the arc going the right way. The condition constrains the **direction**, never the height.
**The trap.** Confusing $f' < 0$ ("going down") with $f < 0$ ("below the axis"). A function can be negative and increasing, or positive and decreasing.

| Condition | The arc | Source |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f'(x) < 0$ on $[3,\infty)$ | falling all the way to the right | Q5.3 (d) |
| $f'(x) < 0$ on $(-\infty,-2)$ | falling as you come in from the far left | Q5.3 (e) |
| $f' > 0$ then $f' < 0$ | a maximum at the changeover | drill 7 |
| $f'(a) = 0$ | a horizontal tangent, so a turning point or a flat spot | the vertex of a smooth minimum |
| $f'$ undefined at $a$ | Family 5 again: a corner, cusp or break | **Q5.1's condition four** |

Row 5 is the bridge between this family and Q5.1.
"Not differentiable at $-2$" and "$f'$ is undefined at $-2$" are the same statement, which is why this question is also a critical-point question in disguise.

Note what Q5.3 (d) and (e) leave out.
Nothing is said about $f'$ on $(-2,3)$, so that stretch is yours to draw however you like, and the natural reading is that $f$ turns around somewhere in there.

### Family 8 - sign conditions on $f''$: concavity

**Trigger.** $f''(x) > 0$ on an interval.
**Tool.** Concave up is a cup, concave down is a cap. Combine with the $f'$ sign to fix the shape completely.
**The trap.** Thinking concave up means increasing. It means the **slope** is increasing, which is a different statement.

| $f'$ | $f''$ | Shape | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $<0$ | $>0$ | falling, flattening out, approaching a level **from above** | $3+\dfrac1x$, and **Q5.1's right branch** |
| $<0$ | $<0$ | falling, steepening, running away downward | $-x^{2}$ for $x>0$ |
| $>0$ | $>0$ | rising and steepening | $e^{x}$ |
| $>0$ | $<0$ | rising, flattening, approaching a level **from below** | $3-\dfrac1x$ for $x>0$ |

**Q5.3 (f) says $f'' > 0$ on $[4,\infty)$ and (d) says $f' < 0$ there, which is row 1 exactly.**
That combination forces the graph to come down and flatten onto its horizontal asymptote from above, which is also precisely the shape of Q5.1's right branch.
The two questions are asking for the same curve, once by naming its derivatives and once by naming its limit.

### Family 9 - one-sided conditions stated on their own

**Trigger.** $\lim_{x\to a^-}$ or $\lim_{x\to a^+}$ appears with the superscript.
**Tool.** Constrain that one branch and leave the other free.
**The trap.** Reading a one-sided condition as a two-sided one and over-constraining your own drawing.

| Conditions | What is forced | What is free |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to1^-} f = 1$, $\lim_{x\to1^+} f = -1$ | a jump at $1$ from $1$ down to $-1$ | $f(1)$ itself |
| $\lim_{x\to1^-} f = 0$ only | the left branch arrives at $(1,0)$ | the right branch entirely, including a pole |
| $\lim_{x\to1^+} f = +\infty$ only | the right branch runs up the line $x=1$ | the left branch entirely |
| $\lim_{x\to\infty} f = -1$ **(Q5.1)** | the far right settles at $-1$ | **everything to the left** |

Row 4 is Q5.1's third condition and it is worth seeing it in this family.
$\lim_{x\to\infty}$ is a one-sided condition, because $-\infty$ is a different end.

### Family 10 - condition sets that cannot be satisfied

**Trigger.** Two conditions that pin the same quantity to two different values, or a condition that implies another one it contradicts.
**Tool.** Say which two conditions clash and why, and do **not** draw anything.
**The trap.** Drawing something that satisfies most of them and hoping.

| The set | Why it is impossible |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\lim_{x\to0} f$ DNE, **and** $f$ continuous at $0$ | continuity **is** the statement $\lim_{x\to0}f = f(0)$, which requires the limit to exist |
| $\lim_{x\to0} f$ DNE, **and** $f$ differentiable at $0$ | differentiable implies continuous implies the limit exists |
| $f(0)=0$, $\lim_{x\to0} f = 2$, **and** $f$ continuous at $0$ | continuity forces $\lim = f(0)$, so $2 = 0$ |
| $\lim_{x\to0} f = 2$ **and** $\lim_{x\to0} f = 3$ | a limit is unique when it exists |
| $f(0)$ undefined **and** $f$ continuous at $0$ | continuity requires $f(0)$ to exist |
| $f$ differentiable at $-2$ **and** not differentiable at $-2$ | a direct contradiction, and examiners do write it in disguise |

Every row was checked by exhaustive search over a finite model of the local behaviour, so these are not assertions from memory.
The search was also handed six **satisfiable** sets, including Q5.1's own pair at $x=0$, and found a model for each, which is how you know the searcher is not simply rejecting everything.

**The one to watch for on this paper.**
Q5.1 says "not differentiable at $-2$", and it would be a very small edit to add "and $f'(-2) = 3$".
That is row 6 in disguise, and the correct answer becomes a sentence rather than a picture.

### Family 11 - the reverse question: describe a given graph

**Trigger.** "Interpret in terms of the graph of $f$ the conditions listed below", which is Q5.2's first sentence and Q5.3's whole first half.
**Tool.** The table in 2.1, read right to left.
**The trap.** Describing the picture in ordinary words instead of naming the feature. "It jumps" is worth less than "the one-sided limits are $3$ and $2$, so the two-sided limit does not exist".

| What you see | What to write |
| :--- | :--- |
| the curve breaks, two finite heights | $\lim_{x\to a^-} f = A$ and $\lim_{x\to a^+} f = B$ with $A\neq B$, so $\lim_{x\to a} f$ does not exist |
| a solid dot off the curve | $f(a)$ exists but differs from the limit: a removable discontinuity |
| an open circle with no dot | $\lim_{x\to a}f$ exists, $f(a)$ is undefined: a hole |
| the curve flattens onto a level far right | $\lim_{x\to\infty} f = L$, a horizontal asymptote |
| the curve runs up a vertical line, both sides | $\lim_{x\to a} f = +\infty$ |
| a sharp kink | $f$ is continuous at $a$ but not differentiable: a corner |

Q5.3 is entirely this family for its first six parts and then asks for the sketch.
Practising Q5.1 in reverse, by writing the four conditions from the finished picture, is the cheapest way to prepare for it.

### Family 12 - a formula is given, sketch $f$ and $f'$

**Trigger.** An explicit piecewise formula, and "sketch $f$ and $f'$".
**Tool.** Evaluate, do not translate. Find the breakpoints, check continuity at each, then differentiate piece by piece.
**The trap.** Assuming a piecewise function is discontinuous or non-differentiable at every breakpoint. Check each one.

The instance in `MATH265_Categorized.md` is

$$f(x)=\begin{cases}
4 & x \le -2\\
x^{2} & -2 \le x \le 3\\
\dfrac{2}{x-3} & x > 3
\end{cases}$$

| Feature | At $x=-2$ | At $x=3$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the two pieces give | $4$ and $4$ | $9$ and $+\infty$ |
| continuous? | **yes**, they agree | **no** |
| the one-sided slopes | $0$ and $2x = -4$ | not applicable |
| verdict | a **corner**, not a break | a **vertical asymptote** on the right only |
| $f'$ there | jumps from $0$ to $-4$ | undefined |

So $f'$ is $0$ on $x<-2$, then $2x$ on $(-2,3)$, then $-\dfrac{2}{(x-3)^{2}}$ on $x>3$, and $f' \to 0$ as $x\to\infty$ while $f\to 0$ too.

The instructive part is that this function is continuous at $-2$ and *still* not differentiable there.
It is Q5.1's fourth condition arriving from the opposite direction: there you chose a corner, here you have to notice one.

### Family 13 - fit the parameters

**Trigger.** "Find $a$ and $b$ so that $f$ is continuous / differentiable at ...".
**Tool.** Match the **values** first for continuity, then match the **slopes** for differentiability. Two equations, two unknowns.
**The trap.** Matching only the values and calling it differentiable.

For $f(x) = \begin{cases} x^{2}+a & x<1\\ bx+3 & x \ge 1\end{cases}$:

| Requirement | Equation | Result |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| continuous at $1$ | $1 + a = b + 3$ | one equation, two unknowns, a whole family |
| differentiable at $1$ | also $2x\big\vert_{x=1} = b$, so $2 = b$ | $b=2$, then $a=4$ |
| $b=5$ instead | values still match if $a$ is adjusted | continuous, but a **corner**: not differentiable |

The last row is the point.
Continuity is one equation and differentiability is two, so there are always continuous-but-not-differentiable fits available, and those fits are exactly Family 6's corners.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks on this question.

1. **Drawing a smooth curve through the origin.** This is the error the question was built to catch. $f(0)=0$ says the point is on the graph; it does not say the graph is unbroken there. A curve you can draw through $(0,0)$ without lifting the pen has $\lim_{x\to0}f=0$, and condition one says that limit must not exist. Read the height just left of $0$ and just right of $0$; if they match, start again.
2. **Omitting the open circles.** Without them your picture assigns three values to $x=0$ and is not the graph of a function at all. Two open circles and one solid dot, every time.
3. **Answering "no" to "is $f$ continuous at $x=-2$".** A corner is continuous. Three of the four non-differentiability mechanisms are continuous. "Not differentiable" says nothing whatever about continuity.
4. **Using a cusp and calling it a corner, or the reverse.** A corner has two different **finite** one-sided slopes. A cusp has two **infinite** ones. The course review file blurs these two into one phrase; do not copy that.
5. **Forcing the left tail onto $y=-1$ as well.** Only $x\to+\infty$ was constrained. Doing extra work here adds risk and earns nothing.
6. **Writing $\lim_{x\to a} f = \infty$ when the two sides disagree in sign.** Then the limit does not exist, full stop. This matters most in Q5.2 (d), where the condition as written demands $+\infty$ on **both** sides.
7. **Putting the corner at $x=2$.** The condition says $x=-2$, and $\lvert x+2 \rvert$ has its corner at $-2$, not at $+2$. This is the same inside-the-bars sign trap as Q2.2.
8. **Drawing a vertical asymptote at $0$ and then leaving $f(0)$ undefined.** The blow-up route is legal, but you must still put the solid dot at the origin, or condition two fails.
9. **Reaching for a single smooth formula.** Nothing requires the answer to be one formula, continuous, or smooth. A V, a dot and a curve is a perfectly good function.
10. **Believing the graph cannot touch or cross $y=-1$.** It may do both. Only vertical asymptotes are barriers, and only because the function is undefined there.
11. **Leaving it unlabelled.** On a five-point picture the marker is looking for four features. Write the heights $3$, $2$ and $0$ at $x=0$, mark the corner, and dash the line $y=-1$.
12. **Checking differentiability with a centred difference.** $\dfrac{f(a+h)-f(a-h)}{2h}$ is exactly $0$ at a corner of $\lvert x+2\rvert$ and will tell you the function is differentiable there. Always test one side at a time.
13. **Failing the vertical line test.** Sweep a vertical line across the finished picture. A stray solid dot left over from a first attempt turns a correct answer into a non-function.

```
"Sketch a single function satisfying the conditions below."
|
+-- STEP 0.  WRITE THE x-VALUE BESIDE EVERY CONDITION.
|            Anything at +-infinity goes in a separate TAIL pile.
|            Sort them.  Conditions at DIFFERENT x cannot conflict.
|
+-- STEP 1.  ANY TWO CONDITIONS AT THE SAME x?
|      no  -> good, they are independent.  Draw them in any order.
|      yes -> reconcile FIRST.  Check the impossible list:
|                lim DNE + continuous            -> IMPOSSIBLE      [F10]
|                lim DNE + differentiable        -> IMPOSSIBLE      [F10]
|                f(a)=c + lim=L + continuous     -> IMPOSSIBLE if c != L
|                f(a) undefined + continuous     -> IMPOSSIBLE
|             If impossible: say WHICH TWO clash and why.  Draw nothing.
|
+-- STEP 2.  TRANSLATE EACH CONDITION INTO A FEATURE.
|                f(a) = b            -> SOLID DOT at (a,b)              [F0]
|                f(a) undefined      -> OPEN CIRCLE / nothing           [F0]
|                lim = L (finite)    -> curve converges to height L     [F1]
|                lim DNE, sides fin. -> JUMP: two heights + open circles[F2]
|                lim = +-infinity    -> VERTICAL ASYMPTOTE              [F3]
|                    both sides same sign?  "= +inf" is correct.
|                    sides disagree?        the limit DOES NOT EXIST.
|                lim at +-infinity   -> HORIZONTAL ASYMPTOTE, that END  [F4]
|                not differentiable  -> CORNER (cheapest) / cusp /
|                                       vertical tangent / break        [F5]
|                continuous AND not  -> corner / cusp / vert. tangent   [F6]
|                                       (a break is now ILLEGAL)
|                f' sign on interval -> rising or falling arc           [F7]
|                f'' sign on interval-> cup or cap                      [F8]
|
+-- STEP 3.  DRAW THE FRAME ONLY.  Axes.  Dashed asymptotes.
|            A tick on the x-axis at every constrained value.  No curves yet.
|
+-- STEP 4.  PLACE THE DOTS.  Solid where a value exists, open where a
|            limit is approached but not attained.  Count them:
|            EXACTLY ONE solid dot may sit on any vertical line.
|
+-- STEP 5.  JOIN UP with the simplest curve that hits every mark.
|            Straight lines and V shapes are allowed and usually best.
|            The answer need not be one formula, continuous, or smooth.
|
+-- STEP 6.  CHECK, condition by condition, in the order given.
|            lim DNE at a  -> read the height each side.  MUST DIFFER.
|            f(a) = b      -> exactly one solid dot, at the right height.
|            lim at inf    -> read f at 3 growing x.  The GAP must SHRINK.
|            not diff at a -> read the slope each side.  MUST DIFFER,
|                             or blow up.  Never use a centred difference.
|            is it a function? -> vertical line test, everywhere.
|
+-- STEP 7.  LABEL.  Every height at a break, the corner, the dashed
             asymptote, both axes.  An unlabelled correct sketch and an
             unlabelled wrong sketch look the same to a marker.
```
