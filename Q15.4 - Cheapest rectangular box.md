# Q15.4 - Cheapest rectangular box

**The question (Final Exam 2, item 5, 10 points).**
"What are the dimensions of the cheapest rectangular box that can be constructed if the material for the box is \$1.20 per square cm and the cost for the lid is \$1.50 per square cm. The length of the base is twice as long as it is wide and the volume must be $120\text{ cm}^3$? What is the minimum cost?"

**The answer.**
The box is $2\sqrt[3]{5}$ cm wide, $4\sqrt[3]{5}$ cm long and $3\sqrt[3]{5}$ cm tall.
That is $\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 3.4200$ by $2\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 6.8399$ by $\tfrac{3}{2}\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 5.1299$ centimetres.
The minimum cost is $\dfrac{324}{\sqrt[3]{5}} = 16.2 \cdot 40^{2/3} = 64.8\sqrt[3]{25} \approx 189.4763$ dollars, so 189.48 dollars to the cent.

### 1.2 The one idea

**The naive prior is wrong, and the question is built to punish it.**
Every student arrives believing that a box problem ends in a cube, because the two most famous optimization facts in a first calculus course are that the rectangle of least perimeter is a square and that the box of least surface area is a cube.
This box is $2:4:3$.
It is not a cube, it cannot be a cube, and the cube that a student reaches for is **cheaper than the true answer** while being illegal, so a "does my number look sensible" check actively rewards the error.

The cube is a special case with two conditions attached, and this stem breaks both of them.

| condition for a cube | this stem |
| :--- | :--- |
| the base must be free to be square | the stem pins $l = 2w$ |
| every face must cost the same per unit area | the lid costs 25 percent more than everything else |

### 1.5 The one sentence that generalises to any per-face-cost box

Write the base as $w$ by $l$ with $l = kw$, let the base cost $c_b$, the lid $c_t$ and the sides $c_s$ per unit area, and fix the volume at $V$.
Then the cost is $C(w) = (c_b+c_t)k\,w^2 + \dfrac{2c_s(1+k)V}{k}\cdot\dfrac{1}{w}$, and

$$w^3 = \frac{c_s(1+k)V}{k^2(c_b+c_t)}, \qquad \frac{h}{w} = \frac{k(c_b+c_t)}{c_s(1+k)}.$$

Read the second formula carefully, because it is the shape result and the first is only the size.
**The proportions of the optimal box depend on the cost ratios and on $k$, and not at all on the volume.**
Doubling the volume moves every dimension by the same factor $2^{1/3}$ and leaves the shape untouched, which was verified here at $V = 1$, $60$, $120$, $1000$ and $99999$.

For this stem $k=2$, $c_b = c_s = 1.20$ and $c_t = 1.50$, so $h/w = 2(2.7)/(1.2 \cdot 3) = 5.4/3.6 = 3/2$ exactly, and with $l/w = 2$ the box is $2:4:3$.

## 2. The six things you must have cold

### 2.1 The geometry, which is not printed on the exam

| quantity | formula | unit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| volume of a $w$ by $l$ by $h$ box | $V = wlh$ | cm$^3$ |
| area of the base | $wl$ | cm$^2$ |
| area of the lid | $wl$ | cm$^2$ |
| area of the four sides | $2wh + 2lh = 2(w+l)h$ | cm$^2$ |
| total surface | $2wl + 2(w+l)h$ | cm$^2$ |

With $l = 2w$ these become base $= $ lid $= 2w^2$, sides $= 6wh$, total $= 4w^2 + 6wh$.
**Count the faces out loud before you differentiate.**
Six faces, two horizontal and four vertical, and the four vertical ones come in two unequal pairs.

### 2.2 Reading the stem's rates, which is where the marks are lost

The stem gives two rates and no diagram, so the reading has to come from the words.

| phrase in the stem | what it covers | rate |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "the material for the box" | the base **and** the four sides | 1.20 per cm$^2$ |
| "the cost for the lid" | the top only | 1.50 per cm$^2$ |

The lid is named separately, so "the box" is everything else.
That is five faces at 1.20 and one face at 1.50.

**This is the single most expensive misreading in the question, and no numeric check catches it.**
Every alternative reading gives a perfectly well-formed problem with a perfectly plausible answer.
The course notes themselves get it wrong; see section 12.

### 2.3 The dimensional gate, run before any calculus

A rate is dollars per square centimetre.
Multiply it by an area and you get dollars; multiply it by anything else and you do not.

| expression | dimension | verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $c \cdot wl$ | (\$ cm$^{-2}$)(cm$^2$) $=$ \$ | a cost |
| $c \cdot wlh$ | (\$ cm$^{-2}$)(cm$^3$) $=$ \$ cm | not a cost |
| $V / (wl)$ | cm$^3$ / cm$^2$ $=$ cm | a height |
| $V / w$ | cm$^3$ / cm $=$ cm$^2$ | not a height |
| $5.4w^2$ | \$ | a cost |
| $432/w$ | (\$ cm) / cm $=$ \$ | a cost |

Note the last row.
The constant $432$ is **not** a plain number of dollars; it carries a length, because it arrived as a rate times an area divided by a width.
That is the check that tells you $432/w$ belongs in a money sum while $432w$ would not.

Run this on your own expression before differentiating.
It costs ten seconds and it is the only gate that sees a rate applied to the wrong thing.

### 2.4 Why the domain is open, in one sentence each

| end | why it is not attainable |
| :--- | :--- |
| $w = 0$ | a box of zero width has no height that gives it volume 120 |
| $w \to \infty$ | nothing in the stem caps the width, and every wide flat box still holds 120 |

So the feasible set is $(0,\infty)$, open at both ends.
There is no left endpoint and no right endpoint to put in a candidate table, the Extreme Value Theorem does not apply, and **Q15.1's comparison method is simply unavailable**.
Measured: $C(10^{-9}) > 4 \times 10^{11}$ and $C(10^{9}) > 5 \times 10^{18}$, with the growth monotone over eight decades in each direction.

### 2.5 The three ways to justify a minimum with no endpoints

| argument | what you write | when to reach for it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **convexity** | $C'' = 10.8 + 864/w^3 > 0$ for every $w > 0$, so the local minimum is global | first choice, one line, settles everything |
| **sign change** | $C' < 0$ before $\sqrt[3]{40}$ and $C' > 0$ after, so the cost falls then rises | always available, and survives $C'' = 0$ |
| **the two ends** | $C \to \infty$ at both ends, so a minimum exists and cannot escape to an end | the honest one, and the only one that explains why a minimum exists at all |

The second-derivative test is comfortable here, unlike on both parts of Q15.1.
$C''$ at the answer is exactly $10.8 + 864/40 = 10.8 + 21.6 = 32.4$.

**Convexity on the whole domain is strictly stronger than $C''(c) > 0$ at the point**, and the phrase "for all $w > 0$" is what upgrades local to global.
On an open domain that phrase is the proof, not decoration.

### 2.6 The two-term ratio, which is Q15.2's check and does not survive

At the minimum of $aw + b/w$ the two terms are **equal**.
At the minimum of $aw^2 + b/w$ they are not.

For $f = aw^p + b w^{-q}$ the stationary condition is $pa\,w^p = qb\,w^{-q}$, so

$$\frac{b\,w^{-q}}{a\,w^{p}} = \frac{p}{q}.$$

Here $p=2$ and $q=1$, so **the reciprocal term is exactly twice the power term**.
Measured at the answer: the horizontal faces cost $63.1588$ and the vertical faces cost $126.3176$, and the ratio is $2$ to fourteen decimal places.

That is a real self-check and it is cheap, but it is a different number from the one Q15.2 taught.
Verified across five exponent pairs: $(2,1) \to 2$, $(1,1) \to 1$, $(3,1) \to 3$, $(1,2) \to 1/2$, $(2,3) \to 2/3$.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Name the variables** and draw the box. Three unknowns, $w$, $l$, $h$.
> 2. **Write every constraint** the stem gives. $l = 2w$, and $wlh = 120$.
> 3. **Write the objective** face by face, with a rate on each face. $C = 1.20(wl + 2wh + 2lh) + 1.50(wl)$.
> 4. **Check the units are homogeneous.** Every term of $C$ must be dollars.
> 5. **Eliminate**, one constraint at a time. $l = 2w$ first, then $h = 60/w^2$.
> 6. **State the domain.** $w > 0$, open at both ends, and say so.
> 7. **Differentiate and solve.** $C' = 10.8w - 432/w^2 = 0$, so $w^3 = 40$.
> 8. **Discard infeasible roots**, saying why. Here there is only one real root, and it is positive.
> 9. **JUSTIFY**, because there are no endpoints. $C'' > 0$ on all of $(0,\infty)$.
> 10. **Recover the other two dimensions.** $l = 2w$, $h = 60/w^2 = \tfrac32 w$.
> 11. **Check the volume constraint on your own three numbers.**
> 12. **Answer the question asked.** Three dimensions first, then the cost, and label the units.

Twelve steps, and steps 2, 3, 5, 7, 9 and 12 are the ones a marker can see went missing.

## 4. Q15.4 worked five ways

### 4.1 Route A, eliminate then use the second derivative

Name $w$ for the base width, $l$ for the base length and $h$ for the height, all in centimetres.

The stem gives two constraints.
$l = 2w$, and $wlh = 120$, which with $l = 2w$ becomes $2w^2h = 120$, so $h = \dfrac{60}{w^2}$.

Now build the cost face by face.
The base and the lid each have area $wl = 2w^2$.
The four sides have total area $2wh + 2lh = 2wh + 4wh = 6wh$.
The base and the four sides are "the material for the box" at 1.20, and the lid is 1.50.

$$C = 1.20\left(2w^2 + 6wh\right) + 1.50\left(2w^2\right) = 2.4w^2 + 7.2wh + 3.0w^2 = 5.4w^2 + 7.2wh.$$

Substitute $h = 60/w^2$ into the second term only.
$7.2w \cdot \dfrac{60}{w^2} = \dfrac{432}{w}$, so

$$C(w) = 5.4w^2 + \frac{432}{w}, \qquad w > 0.$$

Differentiate.

$$C'(w) = 10.8w - \frac{432}{w^2} = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; 10.8w^3 = 432 \;\Longrightarrow\; w^3 = 40.$$

So $w = \sqrt[3]{40} = 2\sqrt[3]{5} \approx 3.4199518934$.

Justify.
$C''(w) = 10.8 + \dfrac{864}{w^3}$, which is positive for **every** $w > 0$, so $C$ is convex on the whole feasible domain and this local minimum is the global one.
At the answer $C'' = 10.8 + 864/40 = 32.4$.

Recover the rest.
$l = 2w = 4\sqrt[3]{5} \approx 6.8399037867$.
$h = 60/w^2 = 60w/w^3 = 60w/40 = \tfrac32 w = 3\sqrt[3]{5} \approx 5.1299278400$.

That last step is worth pausing on.
Because $w^3 = 40$ exactly, $\dfrac{60}{w^2} = \dfrac{60w}{w^3} = \dfrac{60w}{40} = \dfrac{3w}{2}$, with no decimals anywhere.
The height is a rational multiple of the width, which is why the box comes out as a clean $2:4:3$.

Check the volume.
$2\sqrt[3]{5} \cdot 4\sqrt[3]{5} \cdot 3\sqrt[3]{5} = 24\left(\sqrt[3]{5}\right)^3 = 24 \cdot 5 = 120$, exactly, with no rounding.

Report the cost.

$$C = 5.4\left(40^{2/3}\right) + \frac{432}{40^{1/3}} = 16.2 \cdot 40^{2/3} = \frac{324}{\sqrt[3]{5}} \approx 189.4763494362.$$

### 4.2 Route B, the sign chart instead of the second derivative

$C'(w) = 10.8w - \dfrac{432}{w^2} = \dfrac{10.8w^3 - 432}{w^2} = \dfrac{10.8\left(w^3 - 40\right)}{w^2}$.

The denominator is positive on the whole domain, so the sign of $C'$ is the sign of $w^3 - 40$.

| interval | sign of $w^3 - 40$ | sign of $C'$ | $C$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $0 < w < \sqrt[3]{40}$ | negative | negative | falling |
| $w = \sqrt[3]{40}$ | zero | zero | the minimum |
| $w > \sqrt[3]{40}$ | positive | positive | rising |

Two sample values, computed rather than asserted: $C'(3) = 32.4 - 48 = -15.6$ and $C'(4) = 43.2 - 27 = 16.2$.

This route is the one to use if you have any doubt about the second derivative, and it is the only one that survives a flat $C''$.
It also makes the factored form visible, which is the fastest way to see there is exactly **one** critical point.

### 4.3 Route C, three-term AM-GM, with no calculus at all

Q15.2's two-line AM-GM proof does not transfer, and it is worth knowing exactly why.

Two-term AM-GM on $5.4w^2 + \dfrac{432}{w}$ gives $2\sqrt{5.4 \cdot 432} = 96.5981$, which is **below** the true minimum of $189.4763$.
It is a true inequality and a useless one, because the equality case $5.4w^2 = 432/w$ is not where the minimum sits.
Equality would need $w^3 = 80$, which is not the answer.

The three-term version works, because it splits the reciprocal term to match the $1:2$ ratio of section 2.6.

$$5.4w^2 + \frac{216}{w} + \frac{216}{w} \;\ge\; 3\sqrt[3]{5.4 \cdot 216 \cdot 216} = 3\sqrt[3]{251942.4} = 189.4763494362,$$

with equality exactly when all three terms are equal, that is when $5.4w^2 = 216/w$, that is when $w^3 = 40$.
At the answer each of the three terms is $63.1587831454$.

**The rule.** Split $aw^p + bw^{-q}$ into $p$ copies of $\dfrac{a}{p}w^p$ and $q$ copies of $\dfrac{b}{q}w^{-q}$, then apply AM-GM to all $p+q$ of them.
Here $p=2$ and $q=1$, so it is one copy of the square term and two copies of half the reciprocal term.

### 4.4 Route D, the ends and convexity, which is the honest existence argument

$C(w) \to +\infty$ as $w \to 0^{+}$, because the $432/w$ term blows up.
$C(w) \to +\infty$ as $w \to \infty$, because the $5.4w^2$ term blows up.
$C$ is continuous on $(0,\infty)$.

Therefore a minimum exists somewhere in the interior, and since $C' = 0$ at exactly one point, that point is it.

This is the argument that answers "why does a minimum exist at all", which neither the second-derivative test nor the sign chart addresses.
On a closed interval the Extreme Value Theorem answers it for free.
Here you have to say it.

Note the same argument also proves there is **no maximum**, which is a deliverable if the examiner asks for one.

### 4.5 Route E, the ratio shortcut, for checking rather than for writing

Once you know that the reciprocal term is $p/q$ times the power term at any minimum of $aw^p + bw^{-q}$, you can find the answer without differentiating.

Set $\dfrac{432/w}{5.4w^2} = 2$.
Then $432 = 10.8w^3$, so $w^3 = 40$.

That is the same equation, arrived at in one line.
It is not a proof, because it assumes a minimum exists and assumes the ratio result, but as a **check** on the exam it is eight seconds and it is the only cheap check that catches a nearly-right width.

### 4.6 Route comparison

| route | lines | proves global? | needs $C''$? | risk |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A, eliminate and $C''$ | 6 | yes, if you say "for all $w>0$" | yes | writing $C''(c)>0$ and stopping, which proves only local |
| B, sign chart | 6 | yes | no | forgetting the denominator is positive |
| C, three-term AM-GM | 3 | yes, with equality | no | using the **two**-term version, which is not a valid bound |
| D, the two ends | 4 | yes, and explains existence | no | it needs the uniqueness of the critical point |
| E, the ratio shortcut | 1 | no | no | it is a check, not an argument |

**Use route A under exam pressure, and add route D's one sentence.**
Route A is the one the course notes gesture at, it is the shortest complete argument, and the single extra sentence "and $C''>0$ for every $w>0$, so this local minimum is global" converts it from a local claim into a global one for free.
Keep route E in your head for the eight-second check, and keep route C for the case where you cannot remember which way the second-derivative test points.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

### 5.1 The short form, as it should appear on the paper

> Let the base be $w$ wide and $l$ long and the box $h$ tall, in cm, with $w > 0$.
> The stem gives $l = 2w$ and $wlh = 120$, so $2w^2h = 120$ and $h = 60/w^2$.
> The base and lid each have area $2w^2$; the four sides have total area $2(w+l)h = 6wh$.
> Cost $= 1.20(2w^2 + 6wh) + 1.50(2w^2) = 5.4w^2 + 7.2wh = 5.4w^2 + 432/w$ dollars, for $w>0$.
> The domain is open at both ends, so there are no endpoints to test.
> $C'(w) = 10.8w - 432/w^2 = 0$ gives $w^3 = 40$, so $w = \sqrt[3]{40}$, the only real root and positive.
> $C''(w) = 10.8 + 864/w^3 > 0$ for **all** $w > 0$, so $C$ is convex and this is the **global** minimum.
> Then $l = 2\sqrt[3]{40}$ and $h = 60/w^2 = \tfrac32\sqrt[3]{40}$, and $w \cdot l \cdot h = 120$ as required.
> **Dimensions** $\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 3.42$ cm by $2\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 6.84$ cm by $\tfrac32\sqrt[3]{40} \approx 5.13$ cm.
> **Minimum cost** $16.2 \cdot 40^{2/3} \approx 189.48$ dollars.

### 5.2 The sentences that are doing work

| sentence | what it earns |
| :--- | :--- |
| "the base and lid each have area $2w^2$; the sides $6wh$" | it shows you counted six faces and grouped them by rate |
| "for $w>0$" | it is the domain, which the stem does not print |
| "there are no endpoints to test" | it is the reason you are about to justify instead of compare |
| "for **all** $w>0$" | it upgrades a local minimum to a global one |
| "$w\cdot l\cdot h = 120$ as required" | it is the constraint check on your own answer |
| "cm" and "dollars" | the question asks for two different kinds of quantity |

### 5.3 What to write if you are asked for the most expensive box instead

There is none.
$C(w) \to \infty$ as $w \to 0^{+}$ and as $w \to \infty$, so the cost is unbounded above on the feasible domain and no maximum is attained.
**Saying that clearly is the whole answer**, exactly as on Q15.2.

If a cap is added, say $w \le 3$, then the domain becomes $(0,3]$, one endpoint reappears, and the answer moves to it: the minimum is then at $w = 3$ with cost $192.60$.

**Do not try to check the dimensions using the cost.**
This is Q15.2's warning and it is *worse* here.
A width of $3.42$ instead of $3.4199518934$ is $0.0014$ percent wrong, and its cost is wrong by three parts in a hundred million, so the two costs agree to nine significant figures.
A width of $3.5$, which is $2.3$ percent wrong, still gives a cost only $0.054$ percent high, which reads as a rounding difference.

And the volume check does not catch it either.
$3.42 \times 6.84 \times \dfrac{60}{3.42^2} = 120$ exactly, because $h$ was *defined* by the constraint.
**The constraint check can only catch a dimension you recovered wrongly, never a critical point you solved wrongly.**

Only $C'$ catches a nearly-right width, and the ratio check of row 2 is the cheap version of it.
$C'(3.5) = 2.5347$, not zero, and the ratio at $3.5$ is $1.8659$, not $2$.
At $3.42$ the ratio is $1.9999$, which is inside a hand-arithmetic slip, so even the ratio check has a resolution limit.

### 6.2 Magnitude sanity

$3^3 = 27$ and $4^3 = 64$, so $w^3 = 40$ puts $w$ between 3 and 4 and nearer to 3.
The height is one and a half times that, so between 4.5 and 6.
The total surface is $152.05$ cm$^2$, and at rates between 1.20 and 1.50 the cost must lie between $182.46$ and $228.07$ dollars.
Any answer outside that bracket is wrong, and $189.48$ sits comfortably inside it.

That bracket is a genuinely useful gate, because it is computed from the **surface area** rather than from the cost, so it is independent of the rate reading.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

| do | do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| give **four** answers: three dimensions and one cost | give the width and stop |
| write `40^(1/3)` with the exponent bracketed | write `40^1/3`, which reads as $40/3 = 13.33$ |
| write `2*40^(1/3)`, with the explicit `*` | write `2*40^1/3` or `240^(1/3)` |
| write the cost as `16.2*40^(2/3)` or `324/5^(1/3)` | round to `189` and lose the cents |
| label the dimensions in cm and the cost in dollars | leave four bare numbers |
| keep the cube root exact | write `3.42` and lose the volume constraint |
| say which number is the width, length and height | let the marker guess the order |

**The single most expensive typo** is the unbracketed fractional exponent, exactly as on Q15.1 and Q15.2.
`40^(1/3)` is $3.4200$ and `40^1/3` is $13.3333$, a factor of $3.9$, and Möbius marks the second one wrong without comment.

**The second most expensive** is answering the cost where a dimension is wanted.
The cost is $55.4$ times the width, so the error is enormous and obvious once you look, but it is easy to make when four boxes want filling.
Answering the width where the cost is wanted is $98.2$ percent low.

**Unlike Q15.2, this confusion is detectable from the units alone.**
Q15.2's two deliverables were both lengths in centimetres, so neither looked wrong on its own.
Here three answers are lengths and one is money, so a stray $189$ among the centimetres is visible.
Use that.

**Always preview.**
The exam is answer-only and machine-marked, so a right method with a bad keystroke scores zero.

## 8. The variation catalog

Ten families, grouped by **the tool the variation forces**.
Read each table as structure rather than as a list of facts.

Every row in every table below is one instance of the same two formulas from section 1.5, so the whole catalog contains exactly **two** magnitudes, $\left(\dfrac{c_s(1+k)V}{k^2(c_b+c_t)}\right)^{1/3}$ and $\dfrac{k(c_b+c_t)}{c_s(1+k)}$, with different letters substituted in.
Everything else is arithmetic.

Every value in every table was computed by the harness from a face-by-face sum, cross-checked against a formula-blind sweep with a per-instance derived tolerance, and then locked against a digit string harvested from that run.
No decimal below was typed.

### Family 0 - per-face rates, base length $k$ times the width, fixed volume

### Family 1 - no lid, so $c_t = 0$

**Trigger.** "an open box", "a box with no top", "an open-topped container".
**Tool.** The same formulas with $c_t = 0$.

| instance | $w$ | $l$ | $h$ | cost | $h/w$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the exam box with no lid | $4.4814047466$ | $8.9628094931$ | $2.9876031644$ | $144.5975172177$ | $2/3$ |

**The shape worth memorising is row 2: an open-top box with a square base and one rate is exactly twice as wide as it is tall.**
That is the three-dimensional analogue of Q15.2's square, and it is the single most-asked variation of this question in every textbook.

Rows 3 and 4 show the whole family in one comparison.
Make the base twice the side rate and the open box becomes a cube; make it four times and the height doubles the width.
The base rate pushes the box upward, because the way to spend less on an expensive base is to make the base smaller.

### Family 2 - a square base, so $k = 1$

**Trigger.** "the base is square", or the stem gives no relation between $w$ and $l$ at all and you are free to choose.
**Tool.** $k = 1$, so $h/w = 2(c_b+c_t)/(2c_s) = (c_b+c_t)/(2c_s)$.

| instance | $w$ | $l$ | $h$ | cost | cube? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| the exam rates on a square base, $V=120$ | $4.7425244060$ | $4.7425244060$ | $5.3353399567$ | $182.1814557052$ | no, $h/w = 9/8$ |
| one rate, square base, $V=120$ | $4.9324241487$ | $4.9324241487$ | $4.9324241487$ | $145.9728478938$ | **yes** |

**This family is the discriminator for the whole topic.**
Row 2 is the only instance in this entire document where the answer is a cube, and it needs **both** $k=1$ **and** all three rates equal.
Row 1 has $k=1$ and is not a cube.
The exam has equal rates on five of six faces and $k=2$, and is not a cube either.

"The answer is a cube" is a property of a symmetric objective over a free base, never a fact about optimization.

### Family 3 - a reinforced base

**Trigger.** "the base is made of a material three times as expensive", "the bottom must be reinforced".
**Tool.** $c_b \ne c_s$, so the ratio formula skews upward.

| instance | $w$ | $l$ | $h$ | cost | $h/w$ |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| base 3.00, lid 1.00, sides 1.00, $k=1$, $V=100$ | $3.6840314986$ | $3.6840314986$ | $7.3680629973$ | $162.8650569957$ | $2$ |
| base 5.00, lid 1.00, sides 2.00, $k=2$, $V=60$ | $2.4662120743$ | $4.9324241487$ | $4.9324241487$ | $218.9592718406$ | $2$ |

Both rows have $h/w = 2$, reached by two different routes, which is the point.
The second row is a designed instance: its width solves $w^3 = 15$ exactly, and its length equals its height.

**The skew direction, stated once and measured.**
Raising $c_t$ from 0 to 0.60 to 1.20 to 1.50 to 2.40 to 6.00 while everything else is held fixed makes $h/w$ increase monotonically through $2/3$, $1$, $4/3$, $3/2$, $12/5$ and $6$, and makes the width decrease monotonically.
Raising the **side** rate instead reverses both.

**The rule.** Money spent per unit area on the horizontal faces pushes the box **up and in**; money spent on the vertical faces pushes it **down and out**.
An expensive face gets made smaller.

### Family 4 - the dual, a fixed budget and the greatest volume

**Trigger.** "you have 300 dollars of material, what is the largest box".
**Tool.** Constraint and objective exchange places, and the extreme becomes a maximum.

| instance | $w$ | $l$ | $h$ | volume |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| exam rates, budget equal to the exam's own minimum cost | $3.4199518934$ | $6.8399037867$ | $5.1299278400$ | $120.0000000000$ |
| exam rates, budget 100 | $2.4845199750$ | $4.9690399500$ | $3.7267799625$ | $46.0096291667$ |
| one rate, $k=2$, budget 300 | $5.0000000000$ | $10.0000000000$ | $6.6666666667$ | $333.3333333333$ |

**Row 1 is a duality theorem in one line.**
Feed the dual the exam's own minimum cost and it returns the exam's own box.
Minimising cost at fixed volume and maximising volume at fixed cost are the same problem.

**But the domain is different, and that is the teaching point.**
The primal's domain is $(0,\infty)$, open at both ends.
The dual's is $(0, w_{\max}]$, where $w_{\max} = \sqrt{C_0/((c_b+c_t)k)}$ is the width at which the base alone eats the whole budget and the height is zero.
The volume is zero at both ends of that, so the extreme is interior either way, and the maximiser is exactly $w_{\max}/\sqrt{3}$.

Row 3 is designed: the width is exactly 5 and the length exactly 10.

### Family 5 - a cylinder instead of a box

**Trigger.** "a cylindrical can", "a tin", "a closed cylindrical container".
**Tool.** Identical algebra with $r$ for $w$: $\pi r^2 h = V$ gives $h = V/(\pi r^2)$, and the cost is $A r^2 + B/r$ again.

| instance | $r$ | diameter | $h$ | cost or area |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| closed can, one rate, $V=220$ | $3.2715051188$ | $6.5430102375$ | $6.5430102375$ | $201.7420043801$ |
| open-top can, one rate, $V=1000$ | $6.8278406326$ | $13.6556812651$ | $6.8278406326$ | $439.3775662685$ |
| closed can, ends 3.00 side 1.00, $V=220$ | $2.2683349582$ | $4.5366699164$ | $13.6100097492$ | $290.9623191302$ |

**The two shapes worth memorising, and they are one formula.**
For a can with end rate $c_e$ (on $f$ ends) and side rate $c_s$, the optimum has $\dfrac{h}{r} = \dfrac{f\,c_e}{c_s}$.
A closed one-rate can has $f=2$ and $c_e=c_s$, so $h = 2r$: **its height equals its diameter.**
An open one-rate can has $f=1$, so $h = r$: **its height equals its radius.**
Row 3 has $f=2$ and $c_e = 3c_s$, so $h = 6r$, a tall thin tube.

### Family 6 - minimise the area rather than the cost

**Trigger.** "the least material", "the smallest surface area", with no prices at all.
**Tool.** The same solver with all rates equal to 1, because a uniform rate is only a scale factor on the objective and cannot move the minimiser.

| box | $w$ | $l$ | $h$ | surface | cost at the exam's rates |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| least **cost** (the exam's answer) | $3.4199518934$ | $6.8399037867$ | $5.1299278400$ | $152.0489223871$ | $189.4763494362$ |
| least **area**, same $k$ and $V$ | $3.5568933045$ | $7.1137866090$ | $4.7425244060$ | $151.8178797543$ | $189.7723496929$ |

**These are two different boxes, and that is the headline of this family.**
The cheapest box uses $0.152$ percent more material than the box of least material, and the box of least material costs $0.296$ dollars more than the cheapest box.

Both penalties are tiny, which is exactly why the confusion survives.
Neither number looks wrong.
The only thing that separates the two problems is which objective you differentiated.

### Family 7 - a cap closes the domain

**Trigger.** "the width cannot exceed 3 cm", "the box must fit on a 4 cm shelf", "the height must be at least 5 cm".
**Tool.** Q15.1's closed-interval comparison comes back, and the answer may sit at the cap.

| restriction | domain | where the minimum is | cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $w \le 3$ | $(0,3]$ | the **endpoint** $w = 3$ | $192.60$ |
| $w \ge 4$ | $[4,\infty)$ | the **endpoint** $w = 4$ | $194.40$ |
| $3 \le w \le 4$ | $[3,4]$ | the interior critical point $\sqrt[3]{40}$ | $189.4763494362$ |

**A cap restores the Extreme Value Theorem**, and with it a maximum that the open problem does not have.
On $[3,4]$ the maximum is at $w=4$.
On $(0,3]$ and $[4,\infty)$ there is still no maximum, because one end is still open.

The cap only bites if it **excludes** the critical point.
Row 3's cap contains $3.42$ and changes nothing.

### Family 8 - the general power-sum objective

**Trigger.** Any objective of the form $aw^p + bw^{-q}$, which covers every question in families 0 to 7.
**Tool.** $w^{p+q} = \dfrac{qb}{pa}$, and the reciprocal term is $\dfrac{p}{q}$ times the power term.

| objective | where it comes from | $p:q$ | term ratio at the minimum |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $5.4w^2 + 432/w$ | this question | $2:1$ | $2$ |
| $2w + 440/w$ | Q15.2 | $1:1$ | $1$ |
| $w^3 + 1/w$ | a cost with a cubic term | $3:1$ | $3$ |
| $w + 1/w^2$ | a volume-squared penalty | $1:2$ | $1/2$ |
| $7w^2 + 13/w^3$ | an invented control | $2:3$ | $2/3$ |

**The point of this family is negative.**
Q15.2's "the two terms are equal at the minimum" is the $p=q$ row and nothing more.
Applying it here gives $w = \sqrt{432/5.4} = 8.944$, which is off by a factor of $2.6$.

### Family 9 - report the point or report the value

**Trigger.** Every question in the topic.
**Tool.** Read the stem twice.

| the stem says | it wants |
| :--- | :--- |
| "what are the dimensions" | three lengths in cm |
| "what is the minimum cost" | one amount in dollars |
| "find the cheapest box" | the dimensions, because a box is a shape |
| "how much does the cheapest box cost" | the cost only |

The exam's stem asks for both, in that order, and gives ten points for it.
Three of your four answers are lengths and one is money, so **a units label is a free error check** here in a way it was not on Q15.2, where both deliverables were centimetres.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks.

1. **Assuming the answer is a cube.**
   It is $2:4:3$.
   The cube of volume 120 has side $4.9324241487$ and costs $182.4660598672$, which is $7.0103$ dollars **cheaper** than the true answer while violating $l = 2w$.
   So the reflex "my answer should be a cube" produces a number that is lower, and a "is my cost sensible" check therefore rewards it.
   The two conditions for a cube are $k = 1$ and equal rates on every face, and this stem breaks both.

2. **Misreading which faces carry which rate.**
   "The material for the box" is the base plus the four sides; "the cost for the lid" is the top alone.
   The three most common alternative readings give $w = 4.4814$ at cost $144.5975$ (no lid at all), $w = 4.1602$ at cost $155.7630$ (the base left unpaid, which is what the course notes say), and $w = 3.5569$ at cost $182.1815$ (a uniform 1.20).
   All three are well-formed answers to well-formed problems.
   **No numeric check catches this**; only the dimensional gate and a careful re-read do.

3. **Verifying the width with the cost.**
   The objective is flat to second order at the minimum, so a width $0.0014$ percent wrong gives a cost wrong in the ninth significant figure, and a width $2.3$ percent wrong gives a cost only $0.054$ percent high.
   The volume check does not help either, because the height was *defined* by the constraint, so any width you choose satisfies it exactly.
   **Never validate the minimiser using the minimum.**

4. **Reading "twice as long as it is wide" backwards.**
   This one is special, because it is the **only** mutilation in the sixteen-case census that hides completely.
   Setting $w = 2l$ instead of $l = 2w$ gives the same physical box with the labels $w$ and $l$ exchanged, so the three dimensions and the cost are all identical and only the word "width" is attached to the wrong number.
   The reason is that the cost is a **symmetric function of the two base edges**: swapping them leaves the base area and the base perimeter unchanged, and those are the only two things the cost sees.
   Q15.2's involution therefore survives into three dimensions, and the constraint $l = 2w$ is what breaks it.

5. **Using the two-term AM-GM bound.**
   $2\sqrt{5.4 \cdot 432} = 96.5981$ is a true lower bound and a useless one, sitting half the true minimum.
   The three-term split is the one that is exact.

6. **Applying Q15.2's "the two terms are equal" check.**
   Here they are in ratio $1:2$.
   Forcing them equal gives $w^3 = 80$ instead of $40$, so $w = 4.3089$ instead of $3.4200$.

7. **Inventing a closed interval and comparing endpoints.**
   The course notes offer "check endpoints" as the verification step in five of their six optimization strategy blocks, and this question has none.
   Inventing $[1,10]$ happens to return the right minimum, which is the third consecutive category in which a wrong method is rewarded on the exam question, and it also invents a spurious maximum where none exists.

8. **Stopping at $C''(c) > 0$.**
   That proves a **local** minimum.
   On a closed interval a local minimum still has to be compared with the endpoints; on an open domain there is nothing to compare it with, so the words "for all $w>0$" are the entire difference between a complete argument and an incomplete one.

9. **Forgetting the second constraint.**
   There are two, $l = 2w$ and $wlh = 120$, and both must be eliminated.
   Using only the volume leaves a two-variable objective; using only $l = 2w$ leaves $h$ in the answer.

10. **Two wrong models that agree with each other.**
    For $C = Aw^2 + B/w$ the minimiser depends only on $B/A$ and the minimum depends only on $AB^2$, and these two quantities are independent.
    So the uniform-1.20 reading, the uniform-1.50 reading and the least-area problem all return the **same width** $3.5568933045$ with three different costs, and the no-lid reading and the count-the-sides-as-$4wh$ slip return the **same cost** $144.5975172177$ with two different widths.
    Getting the same answer twice by two routes is not evidence, if both routes share a modelling error.

11. **Reporting one dimension.**
    The stem says "dimensions", plural, and there are three of them plus a cost.
    Four boxes to fill.

12. **The unbracketed fractional exponent.**
    `40^1/3` is $13.3333$, not $3.4200$.

What the verification actually did, so the numbers above mean something.

The constraint and the cost objective were checked against the stem's own numbers **in exact rationals, before any calculus**, face by face at five separate widths, with three alternative rate readings explicitly rejected.
A **dimensional gate** was built as exponent vectors over centimetres and dollars and proved live against eight seeded rejections, because a units misreading is the one error in this question that no numeric arm can see.
$C'$ was recovered twice without ever seeing its closed form, by complex-step differentiation and by a bounded Richardson ladder whose fourth-order convergence was measured on a separate **curved** control, since a centred quotient differentiates the quadratic part exactly and would have measured nothing.
$C''$ got its own arm, a Richardson-extrapolated second difference, rather than being obtained by re-differentiating the claimed $C'$.
