# Final Exam

At the end of your course, you will write a three-hour supervised (invigilated) final exam to ensure that you have achieved the learning outcomes of the course.
You may bring a dictionary with you (and it may combine English and another language if English is not your first language).

The final exam counts for 20% of your course mark, and you must achieve a mark of 50% or better on the exam to pass the course.
If you do not pass the exam the first time, you may write one supplemental exam.
See the Athabasca University Undergraduate Calendar for more information on [supplemental exams](https://www.athabascau.ca/calendar/undergraduate/exams-grades/supplemental-exams.html).

The exam results and your final grade will be available to you approximately five business days after you write it.
The exam will not be returned to you, but you will receive a brief note from your tutor with your results.
Should you wish to appeal your exam grade, consult [Appeals](https://www.athabascau.ca/calendar/undergraduate/exams-grades/marks-and-grades.html#appeals) in the online Athabasca University Undergraduate Calendar for more information.

This is an [online exam](https://www.athabascau.ca/support-services/exam-services-support/writing-online-exams.html), and it will contain the following four sections:

## Part A: Essay (50 marks)

Write a 500- to 600-word essay on your choice of four topics.
The topics will be general enough to allow you to write based on your personal experience and knowledge.
Your essay should demonstrate proficiency in one of the following modes of organization: process, classification, comparison, or cause-effect.
Take time to create an outline or a plan, and be sure to focus the essay on a clear thesis.
The essay should include the following:

- a title
- an introductory paragraph that includes a strong thesis statement
- two or three body paragraphs, each beginning with a topic sentence
- a concluding paragraph that sums up and restates the thesis

Set aside time for both planning and proofreading.
The essay will be marked on content, organization, mechanics, grammar, and style.

Part A is worth 50% of the exam mark, so spend approximately 50% of your time (60 to 90 minutes) on it.

## Part B: Summary (20 marks)

For Part B, you will read an unfamiliar article (provided with the exam).
Then, you will write a one-paragraph summary of that article (approximately 150-200 words in length).
Begin the paragraph with the article title, the author's name, and a restatement of the author's thesis.
Then, follow the steps in "10 Steps to Writing a Successful Summary" (in Chapter 15 of *Read, Think Write*) to write a summary of the article.

Part B is worth 20% of the exam mark, and you will spend approximately 30 minutes on it.

## Part C: Excerpt Analysis (10 Marks)

For Part C, you will need to be familiar with **one** of the essays listed below.
Most likely, you will choose the essay you summarized for Assignment 2, but if you prefer, you may choose another.

- ["How to Write with Style"](https://kmh-lanl.hansonhub.com/pc-24-66-vonnegut.pdf) by Kurt Vonnegut
- ["I Wrote the Super Queer Novel My Younger Self Needed"](https://electricliterature.com/would-i-have-realized-i-was-gay-sooner-if-queer-books-were-mainstream/) by Celia Laskey
- ["Life Is Too Short for Someone Else's Shame"](https://disabilityvisibilityproject.com/2020/09/16/life-is-too-short-for-someone-elses-shame/) by Amanda Leduc
- ["No Place for Self-Pity, No Room for Fear"](https://www.thenation.com/article/archive/no-place-self-pity-no-room-fear/) by Toni Morrison
- ["On Fighting for Space in the Literary World as a Black Canadian Writer"](https://lithub.com/on-fighting-for-space-in-the-literary-world-as-a-black-canadian-writer/) by Cheryl Thompson
- ["On Keeping a Notebook"](https://sanmiguelwritersconference.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/406-706-Looking-Back-Moving-Forward-Jessica-Handler.pdf) by Joan Didion
- ["Politics and the English Language"](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) by George Orwell
- ["Shitty First Drafts"](https://wrd.as.uky.edu/sites/default/files/1-Shitty%20First%20Drafts.pdf) by Anne Lamott
- ["Why I Write"](https://www.nytimes.com/1976/12/05/archives/why-i-write-why-i-write.html) by Joan Didion
- ["Why I Write"](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/why-i-write/) by George Orwell
- ["Why I Write Indigenous Sci-Fi"](https://canadianart.ca/essays/why-i-write-indigenous-sci-fi/) by Drew Hayden Taylor
- ["Write Till You Drop"](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/books/99/03/28/specials/dillard-drop.html) by Annie Dillard

On the exam, you will see a list of twelve excerpts, one from each essay listed above.
You will choose one excerpt to work with.

You will write a paragraph that a) identifies the essay title and the author, and b) examines the role and significance of the excerpt and how it functions in the article in relation to the author's thesis.
(See Preparing for the Final Examination, below, for more information.)

Part C is worth 10% of the final exam mark, and you will likely spend 20 to 30 minutes on it.

## Part D: Grammar and Mechanics (20 marks)

Part D consists of 20 multiple-choice questions, each containing one of the errors included in "20 Common Errors" document at the end of the Study Guide, Activities, and Assessments.
For example, a question might contain a comma splice, a sentence fragment, a semicolon error, a parallelism error, a dangling participle, etc.
You do not need to be able to label/name the errors, but you do need to be able to identify a correct or incorrect sentence from a multiple-choice list.

Part D is worth 20% of the exam mark, and it will likely take only 15 to 20 minutes if you have prepared adequately.

## Preparing for the Final Exam

### 1. Prepare for Part A.

Review Chapters 12 and 16 in *Read, Think, Write*.
Review your tutor's feedback on Assignments 3 and 5.

Practice writing an essay, from start to finish, within a time limit.
Choose a topic from Practice 16.2, 16.3, 16.4, or 16.5 in *Read, Think, Write*.
Give yourself 75 minutes to write the practice essay, from planning to proofreading.

### 2. Prepare for Part B.

Review Chapter 15 in *Read, Think, Write*, paying particular attention to "10 Steps to Writing an Effective Summary" and "The Dos and Don'ts of Writing a Summary."
Review your tutor's feedback on Assignment 2.

Practice writing a summary.
Choose one of the articles in Unit 2 that you have not already written about.
Choose one page of the article to summarize.
Give yourself 40 minutes to read the page and to write a summary.

### 3. Prepare for Part C.

For Part C, you will work with **one** of 12 essays.
Do not try to memorize all 12 essays.
Do not even memorize one!
You do not need to read any of the other essays listed for Part C **except** for the one you wish to work with.

Shortly before the exam, review your notes from Learning Activity 5 in Unit 2.
Re-read your chosen essay.
Memorize the title and author.
Read the essay closely, using the active reading strategies you learned in Unit 2.
Write out a restatement of the author's thesis, in your own words.
Memorize it.
Identify the main supporting points.

To practice for Part C, choose an important sentence from your essay and pretend that it is the excerpt you see on the exam.
Determine the role of excerpt:

- Is it the author's thesis statement?
- Is it a key supporting idea?
- Is it a main argument or a key reason?
- Is it a piece of evidence?
- Is it a supporting detail? If it is a supporting detail, is it a statistic, a fact, an example, an anecdote, etc?
- Is it an analogy or a metaphor?
- Does it appeal to logic or emotion?
- Is it an introduction or a conclusion?
- Does it have any other roles, such as establishing tone?

Give yourself 20 minutes to write a paragraph that 1) identifies the title and author, 2) restates the article's thesis, and 3) thoroughly explains the role of excerpt by answering the questions above.

In the first sentence of your paragraph, identify the essay title and the author ("This excerpt is from . . . ).
Then, paraphrase the author's thesis.

The rest of the paragraph should be devoted to identifying the role and significance of the quotation.
How does the excerpt relate to the author's thesis?
What is the job of the quotation in supporting the thesis?
Which specific words or phrases in the quotation demonstrate its role?
In your paragraph, quote them and explain their significance.

### 4. Prepare for Part D.

To prepare for Part D, review the "20 Common Errors" document at the end of the Study Guide, Activities, and Assessments, and study the examples.
All 20 errors will be represented on the exam.
Pay particular attention to the errors that your tutor has flagged in your assignments.

> **Note:** You may take a dictionary into the exam, but you may not use any other books or sources.

### 5. On exam day, be as rested and relaxed as you can.

We recommend, above all, that you have a good night's rest and try to relax.
This exam should have no surprises.
We want to assess your essay-writing, summarizing, and critical thinking skills.
We also want to see your ability with grammar and have assurance that the tone and style of your writing matches that of your previous course submissions.
We do not expect your writing to be as polished as it might be if you had more time.

### 6. Manage the exam.

1. Look the whole exam over once.
   This will calm your nerves and prepare you to write.

> **Remember:** You have a maximum of three hours to complete the exam.
> Most students use the full three hours.
> Budget your time accordingly.

2. Proceed to Part A, and choose the essay topic you would like to write about.

3. Next, answer the following questions for yourself, and based on those answers, make a rough outline for the essay:

   - Who is my audience?
   - What is my purpose?
   - Therefore, what should be my style and type of writing (e.g., personal, expository, persuasive)?
   - What is my thesis?
   - What is the topic/topic sentence in each of my three or four body paragraphs?
   - What sort of supporting detail will I need to mention for each point made in each paragraph?

   Your essay should demonstrate a clear, sufficiently detailed thesis (usually placed at the end of your opening paragraph) and two or three solid body paragraphs of 6-12 sentences each.
   Use good paragraph craft so that each paragraph has focus, support, and a clear, effective relationship to your thesis.
   **Give yourself 90 minutes for planning and writing your essay.**

4. Use your dictionary.
   Remember that you may bring a dictionary with you, and it may combine English and another language if English is not your first language.
   Use your dictionary initially just to check the precise meanings of any key words in the essay assignment instructions.
   Once you reach the essay-revision stage, use your dictionary to check spellings, if you have time.

5. Manage your remaining time.
   Assuming you have used the full 90 minutes to prepare and write Part A, you will have 90 minutes left to complete your exam.
   We recommend using that time as follows:

   | Section | Time |
   | --- | --- |
   | Part B | 40 minutes |
   | Part C | 20 minutes |
   | Part D | 20 minutes |
   | Final Revision | 10 minutes |

   This timeline is just a recommendation.
   The important thing is to keep an eye on the clock so you do not spend too much time on any one section.

### 7. A few more tips

- Do not waste time typing out exam instructions or essay topics. Instead, get straight to your answer.
- While it is a good idea to jot notes or make an outline for your essay on a piece of paper, do not try to write out a complete first draft. If you do, you will likely run out of time and energy in your final draft.
- You will have limited ability to format your answers in the exam software. You do not need to double-space. Instead of indenting, hit the return key twice and leave a blank line to indicate a paragraph break. Instead of italics for book or film titles, use quotation marks.
- There is no spell-check in the exam software. Leave time to proofread.
- You do not need to provide any in-text citations or bibliographies.
- If you get stuck on a grammar question, guess, then move on. You can go back to it later if you have time. Each grammar question is worth only one mark, so do not waste time pondering an individual question. Your time is better spent proofreading your essay.
