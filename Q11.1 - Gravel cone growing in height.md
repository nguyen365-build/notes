# Q11.1 - Gravel cone growing in height

**The question (Midterm Exam 1, item 7, 5 points).**
"Gravel is being dumped from a conveyor belt at a rate of $0.5\text{ m}^3/\text{min}$. It forms a pile in shape of a cone whose base diameter and height are always equal. How fast is the height of the pile increasing when the pile is $4\text{ m}$ high?"

**The answer.** $\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0398$ metres per minute.

**What to type into Möbius:** `1/(8*pi)`

Provenance: parsed from `notes\MATH265\MATH265.md`, not typed.
MT1's ten printed values are 5, 6, 5, 16, 8, 4, 5, 5, 5, 5, summing to 64, and item 7 is worth 5 of them.
MT1 carries exactly one "no credit will be given" clause and it sits on item 3, not on this one.
Verification: 180 assertions pass, 0 fail, 15 of them seeded controls proving the gates can bite.

---

## 0. The 30-second version

```
NAME + WRITE THE RATES     h = height (m), r = base radius (m), V = volume (m^3)
                           GIVEN  dV/dt = +0.5 m^3/min      (a RATE, all the time)
                           WANT   dh/dt = ?  at the INSTANT h = 4 m
                                                   ^^^^^^^ a value, not a rate

RELATE                     V = (1/3) pi r^2 h              two variables, need one

ELIMINATE (ratio only)     "diameter = height" ==> 2r = h ==> r = h/2   TRUE FOR ALL t
                           V = (1/3) pi (h/2)^2 h = pi h^3 / 12

DIFFERENTIATE wrt t        dV/dt = (pi/4) h^2 * dh/dt      <- chain rule lives here

SUBSTITUTE, THEN SOLVE     0.5 = (pi/4)(4)^2 dh/dt  ==>  dh/dt = 1/(8 pi) m/min
```

The whole method is: name, relate, eliminate, differentiate, and only then substitute.
The single rule that keeps you out of trouble is that a **constant** may be substituted before you differentiate and a **variable** may not.

## 1. What this question is really testing

This is the first related-rates question in the bank, and related rates is a different kind of question from everything in categories 1 to 10.
The calculus in it is trivial - one power rule and one chain factor.
Every mark is in the setup and in the reading of the English.

| Reflex being tested | What it looks like here | Where it comes back |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Turn English into named variables with units | "how fast is the height increasing" is $dh/dt$ in m/min | every related-rates question, always |
| Separate a **rate** from an **instantaneous value** | $0.5$ is a rate; $4$ is a value | Q11.2, and the whole category |
| Choose the relating equation from geometry | volume of a cone | Q11.2 uses Pythagoras instead |
| Use a fixed ratio to eliminate a variable | $2r = h \Rightarrow r = h/2$ | any cone, any similar-triangle problem |
| Differentiate an implicit relation with respect to $t$ | $\frac{d}{dt}h^3 = 3h^2\,\frac{dh}{dt}$ | this is Q10.1's $y'$ wearing a different hat |
| Substitute **after** differentiating | putting $h = 4$ in early destroys the problem | the classic lost mark |
| Report with units and a sign | $+0.0398$ **m/min** | units are never carried by the algebra |

**Pair this with Q10.1.**
Both are on MT1, both are worth 5 points, and both are the same skill: differentiating something whose variable is itself a function of another variable, and remembering the chain factor.
In Q10.1 the chain factor is written $y'$.
Here it is written $dh/dt$.
It is the same $y'$.

**One provenance point that costs marks.**
The cone volume formula is **not printed on this question**.
The other cone question in the course, MT2 item 2, prints the hint "The volume of a cone is $V = \frac{\pi r^2 h}{3}$" - and `MATH265_Review.md` describes that formula as "given on exam" under its Q2 heading.
That description is true of MT2 item 2 and false of MT1 item 7.
You must have $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ memorised for this question.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2.1 The solid-geometry formulas

Related rates does not supply these.
The whole category is a memory test with a one-line derivative attached.

| Solid | Volume | Surface |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Cone | $V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | lateral $= \pi r \sqrt{r^2+h^2}$ |
| Sphere | $V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | $S = 4\pi r^2$ |
| Cylinder | $V = \pi r^2 h$ | lateral $= 2\pi r h$ |
| Cube | $V = s^3$ | $S = 6s^2$ |
| Circle | $A = \pi r^2$ | circumference $= 2\pi r$ |

### 2.2 The two English-to-symbol translations that decide everything

| English | Symbol | Is it constant in time? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "at a rate of 0.5 m³/min" | $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 0.5$ | yes, it holds at every instant |
| "base diameter and height are **always** equal" | $2r = h$ | yes, and the word "always" is the licence |
| "when the pile is 4 m high" | $h = 4$ | **no** - this is one instant only |
| "how fast is the height increasing" | $\dfrac{dh}{dt}$ | this is what you are solving for |

The word **always** is the whole permission slip for the substitution $r = h/2$.
The word **when** is the warning label on $h = 4$.

### 2.3 The chain rule, in its related-rates costume

$$\frac{d}{dt}\Big[h(t)^3\Big] = 3h^2 \cdot \frac{dh}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\Big[r(t)^2\Big] = 2r\cdot\frac{dr}{dt}$$

Every variable in a related-rates problem is secretly a function of $t$.
Differentiating it with respect to $t$ therefore always produces its own rate as a trailing factor.
If your differentiated equation does not contain the rate you want, you dropped a chain factor.

### 2.4 The units bookkeeping

Units do not travel through the algebra by themselves.
Carry them separately and reunite them at the end.

$$\left[\frac{dh}{dt}\right] = \frac{[dV/dt]}{[h]^2} = \frac{\mathrm{m}^3/\mathrm{min}}{\mathrm{m}^2} = \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{min}}$$

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1. Draw it and name everything.**
> Every quantity that changes gets a letter and a unit.
> Every quantity that does not change gets a number.
>
> **Step 2. Write GIVEN and WANT as rates, before any calculus.**
> `GIVEN: dV/dt = 0.5 m^3/min`
> `WANT:  dh/dt at the instant h = 4 m`
> If you cannot write these two lines, you have not read the question yet.
>
> **Step 3. Find the relating equation.**
> One equation connecting the given quantity to the wanted quantity.
> It comes from geometry, from Pythagoras, from similar triangles, or from trigonometry.
>
> **Step 4. Eliminate every variable you do not want, using a FIXED relationship only.**
> A relationship that holds at all times may be substituted now.
> A value that holds at one instant may not.
>
> **Step 5. Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$.**
> Chain rule on every variable. Every term gains a rate.
>
> **Step 6. NOW substitute the instantaneous values, and solve.**
>
> **Step 7. Report with units, with a sign, and in a sentence.**

### The classify table: which relating equation?

| Cue in the stem | Relating equation | Elimination tool |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A cone, "always" some shape | $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$ | fixed ratio $r = kh$ |
| A sphere, a balloon | $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ | none needed, one variable |
| A ladder, a right angle, two distances | $x^2 + y^2 = c^2$ | none needed |
| A shadow, a lamp post, "tall" and "away" | similar triangles | ratio of the two triangles |
| An angle of elevation, a radar dish | $\tan\theta$, $\csc\theta$ | none needed |
| Two things changing independently | keep both variables | none - the product rule survives |

## 4. Q11.1 worked three ways

### Route A - eliminate first, then differentiate (the intended route)

The stem says the base diameter and the height are **always** equal.
Diameter is $2r$, so $2r = h$, so $r = \tfrac{h}{2}$.
This is a fixed proportion, not a snapshot, so it may be substituted before differentiating.

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi\cdot\frac{h^2}{4}\cdot h = \frac{\pi h^3}{12}$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$.
The right-hand side needs the chain rule, because $h$ is a function of $t$.

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{12}\cdot 3h^2 \cdot \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{4}h^2\,\frac{dh}{dt}$$

Only **now** substitute the instant.

$$0.5 = \frac{\pi}{4}(4)^2\,\frac{dh}{dt} = 4\pi\,\frac{dh}{dt} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{0.5}{4\pi} = \frac{1}{8\pi}$$

$$\boxed{\ \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0398\ \text{m/min}\ }$$

### Route B - keep both variables, differentiate, eliminate afterwards

This route is worth doing once, because it proves that the early substitution in Route A was legitimate rather than lucky.

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(2rh\,\frac{dr}{dt} + r^2\,\frac{dh}{dt}\right)$$

The product rule is needed here, because both $r$ and $h$ vary.
Now use the fixed ratio in both of its forms: $r = h/2$ and, differentiating that same fixed ratio, $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{dh}{dt}$.

$$\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(2\cdot\frac{h}{2}\cdot h\cdot\frac{1}{2}\frac{dh}{dt} + \frac{h^2}{4}\frac{dh}{dt}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(\frac{h^2}{2}+\frac{h^2}{4}\right)\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{3h^2}{4}\cdot\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{4}h^2\frac{dh}{dt}$$

Identical.
This was checked over a grid of 45 $(h,\,dV/dt)$ pairs and the two routes agree everywhere.
Note that Route B needs the ratio **and** the ratio's own derivative; forgetting the second is a fresh way to lose the mark, which is why Route A is the one to use.

### Route C - the base-area shortcut

There is a genuinely useful fact hiding in this problem.
For a cone that grows self-similarly with $r = kh$,

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi k^2 h^3 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dV}{dh} = \pi k^2 h^2 = \pi (kh)^2 = \pi r^2 = \text{the base area}$$

So for **any** self-similar cone,

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{dV/dt}{\text{base area}}$$

Here the base area at $h = 4$ is $\pi r^2 = \pi(2)^2 = 4\pi$, so $dh/dt = 0.5/(4\pi) = 1/(8\pi)$ in one line.
This was verified for seven different ratios $k$ at fourteen heights each.
Use it as a **check**, not as your written method, because the marker wants to see the differentiation.

### Route comparison

| Route | Steps | Where it goes wrong | Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - eliminate, then differentiate | 4 | forgetting that $r = h/2$ needs the diameter halved | ~60 s |
| B - differentiate, then eliminate | 6 | product rule, plus forgetting $dr/dt = \tfrac12 dh/dt$ | ~120 s |
| C - base-area shortcut | 1 | no derivation shown, so no method marks | ~15 s |

**Use Route A under pressure, and check with Route C.**
Route A has the fewest chances to drop a factor, and it is the route the course's own notes take.
Route C takes eight seconds and catches a wrong constant immediately.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Nothing in the four course files states a marking scheme - there are zero occurrences of "rubric", "marking scheme" or "part marks" across all of them - so which sentences earn which marks is inference, not fact.
What follows is the shortest write-up that shows every step a marker could be looking for.

> Let $h$ be the height of the pile in metres, $r$ its base radius in metres, and $V$ its volume in cubic metres, each a function of time $t$ in minutes.
> **Given** $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 0.5\ \text{m}^3/\text{min}$. **Want** $\dfrac{dh}{dt}$ when $h = 4\ \text{m}$.
>
> The base diameter equals the height at all times, so $2r = h$ and $r = \dfrac{h}{2}$.
>
> $V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi\left(\dfrac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \dfrac{\pi h^3}{12}$.
>
> Differentiating with respect to $t$ and applying the chain rule to $h^3$:
> $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = \dfrac{\pi}{4}h^2\dfrac{dh}{dt}$.
>
> Substituting $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 0.5$ and $h = 4$:
> $0.5 = 4\pi\dfrac{dh}{dt}$, so $\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0398$.
>
> The height of the pile is increasing at $\dfrac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0398$ metres per minute.

Six sentences.
The three that are easiest to omit and most likely to be looked for are the naming sentence, the "at all times" justification for the substitution, and the final sentence with units.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The magnitude check

Half a cubic metre of gravel per minute, spread over a base of area $4\pi \approx 12.6$ square metres, must raise the pile by roughly $0.5/12.6 \approx 0.04$ m each minute.
Four centimetres a minute is the right order of magnitude for a gravel pile.
An answer of 4 metres per minute or of 0.0004 metres per minute is wrong on inspection.

### Cheap invariants

| Check | Value here | What a failure means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $dh/dt = (dV/dt)/(\pi r^2)$ | $0.5/(4\pi) = 1/(8\pi)$ | your constant is wrong |
| Sign of $dh/dt$ | positive, the pile grows | you inverted a rate |
| Units | m/min | you divided by $h$ instead of $h^2$ |
| Does the rate shrink as $h$ grows? | yes, $\propto 1/h^2$ | you put $h$ in the numerator |
| Doubling $h$ divides the rate by | exactly 4 | your exponent is wrong |

### Is the instant even reachable?

Do not assume it.
With a constant $dV/dt = 0.5$ from an empty start, $V(t) = 0.5t$ and $\frac{\pi h^3}{12} = 0.5t$, so

$$h(t) = \left(\frac{6t}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad h = 4 \text{ at } t = \frac{32\pi}{3} \approx 33.51\ \text{minutes}$$

Finite and positive, so the instant is attainable.
The same screen applied to a **shrinking** pile asked to reach $h = 6$ from $h = 4$ correctly returns "never".

### Numbers worth memorising

| Quantity | Exact | Decimal |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $dh/dt$ at $h=4$ | $\frac{1}{8\pi}$ | 0.0397887 m/min |
| $dr/dt$ at $h=4$ | $\frac{1}{16\pi}$ | 0.0198944 m/min |
| $dV/dh$ at $h=4$ (the base area) | $4\pi$ | 12.5664 m² |
| $V$ at $h=4$ | $\frac{16\pi}{3}$ | 16.7552 m³ |
| Time from empty to $h=4$ | $\frac{32\pi}{3}$ | 33.5103 min |
| Base area rate $dA/dt$ at $h=4$ | $\frac14$ | 0.25 m²/min exactly |
| Same answer in cm/min | $\frac{100}{8\pi}$ | 3.9789 cm/min |
| Same answer in m/h | $\frac{60}{8\pi}$ | 2.3873 m/h |

## 7. Answer-entry rules

| Do | Do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| `1/(8*pi)` | `1/8*pi` - this parses as $(1/8)\pi = 0.3927$, off by a factor of about 10 |
| `0.5/(4*pi)` | `0.5/4*pi` - same failure |
| `2/(16*pi)` | `1/8pi` - implicit multiplication is not guaranteed |
| `0.0398` if a decimal is demanded | `0.04` - that is $2\times10^{-4}$ away, outside a $10^{-4}$ tolerance |
| Type `*` between every factor, then **Preview** | assume the box knows what you meant |

**The single most expensive typo is the missing bracket in the denominator.**
`1/8*pi` evaluates to 0.392699 rather than 0.039789 - very nearly a factor of ten, which looks plausible enough that you will not notice it.

If the box asks for the unit separately, it is **m/min**, not m³/min.
The units were verified dimensionally rather than by eye: $[dV/dt]\,[h]^{-2} = \mathrm{m}^3\mathrm{min}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{m}^{-2} = \mathrm{m}\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$, and the same check explicitly rejects m³/min, m/min² and min/m.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
For the self-similar cone there is only ever one formula,

$$\frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{dV/dt}{\pi k^2 h^2}, \qquad r = kh$$

and every entry in Family 1 is that one formula with a different $k$.
The magnitudes that actually appear are generated by $k^{-2}$, so they run 4, 1, $\tfrac{4}{9}$, 9, $\tfrac{1}{4}$ against the base case - three distinct behaviours, not five facts.
Signs are generated entirely by whether the given rate is an addition or a removal.

Every value below was verified twice: once from the closed form, and once formula-blind, by building an explicit $h(t)$ consistent with the given rate and taking a numerical derivative of it.

### Family 0 - the same pile, a different unknown

**Trigger:** the same stem, with the question mark moved.
**Tool:** the same relating equation; only the unknown changes place.

| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $dh/dt$ at $h=4$ | the exam question | $\frac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0397887$ m/min |
| $dV/dt$ if $dh/dt = 0.02$ at $h=4$ | run it backwards | $0.08\pi \approx 0.2513274$ m³/min |
| At what $h$ is $dh/dt = 0.1$? | solve for $h$ | $2.5231$ m |
| $dr/dt$ at $h=4$ | $r = h/2 \Rightarrow \dot r = \dot h/2$ | $\frac{1}{16\pi} \approx 0.0198944$ m/min |

### Family 1 - the same cone, a different fixed ratio

**Trigger:** any phrase fixing the shape "always" or "at all times".
**Tool:** convert the phrase to $r = kh$, then $dh/dt = \dfrac{dV/dt}{\pi k^2 h^2}$.

The conversion is where marks are lost, so do it slowly.

| Phrasing | $r = kh$ | $dh/dt$ at $h=4$, $dV/dt = 0.5$ |

| "height is double the radius" | $k = \tfrac12$ - **the same constraint** | $\frac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0397887$ |

### Family 2 - a different solid, one variable

**Trigger:** a sphere, a cube, a balloon, a tank of fixed radius.
**Tool:** no elimination is needed. Differentiate the volume formula and solve.

| Setup | Relation | Answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sphere, $dV/dt = 0.5$, $r = 4$ | $\dot V = 4\pi r^2\dot r$ | $\dot r = \frac{1}{128\pi} \approx 0.0024868$ m/min |
| That sphere's surface rate | $\dot S = 8\pi r\dot r$ | $0.25$ m²/min |
| Cube, $dV/dt = 0.5$, $s = 4$ | $\dot V = 3s^2\dot s$ | $\dot s = \frac{1}{96} \approx 0.0104167$ m/min |
| That cube's surface rate | $\dot S = 12s\dot s$ | $0.5$ m²/min |
| Cylinder of fixed radius 2, $dV/dt = 0.5$ | $\dot V = 4\pi\dot h$ | $\dot h = \frac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0397887$ m/min |
| Inverted conical tank, radius 3 at depth 6 | the tank fixes $r = h/2$ | at $h=4$, $\frac{1}{8\pi}$ - identical algebra |

The cylinder row is a warning, not a coincidence to lean on: it agrees with the cone **only at $h = 4$**, because that is the one height where the cone's base area happens to be $4\pi$.
At $h = 2$ or $h = 6$ the two disagree completely.

The tank row is worth its own sentence.
A **pile** fixes its own ratio by growing self-similarly; a **tank** fixes the ratio by its walls.
The algebra is identical, but the sentence justifying it is different, and only one of them contains the word "always".

### Family 4 - similar triangles and shadows

**Trigger:** a lamp post, a person "tall", a wall, a projected image.
**Tool:** set up the proportion between the two similar triangles, then eliminate.

## 9. The trap list, ranked by how often it costs marks

**1. Substituting $h = 4$ before differentiating.**
This is the defining error of the whole category.
If you write $V = \frac{\pi(4)^3}{12} = \frac{16\pi}{3}$ and then differentiate, you get $\frac{dV}{dt} = 0$, because you differentiated a constant.
The equation $0.5 = 0$ has no solution and the question is destroyed.
A value that holds at **one instant** may only be substituted **after** differentiating.
A relationship that holds at **all instants** may be substituted before.
The word "always" in the stem is the only thing that distinguishes them.

**2. Using $r = h$ instead of $r = h/2$.**
The stem says the **diameter** equals the height.
This gives $0.5/(16\pi) = \frac{1}{32\pi} \approx 0.0099472$, a factor of 4 too small.
This is the single most common wrong answer on this question.

**3. Never substituting the ratio at all, and treating $r$ as a constant.**
This gives $\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{3}r^2\frac{dh}{dt}$ and $\frac{3}{8\pi} \approx 0.1193662$, a factor of 3 too large.
It looks like a complete solution, which is what makes it dangerous.

**4. Dropping the chain factor.**
Writing $\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{\pi}{4}h^2$ with no $\frac{dh}{dt}$ leaves $0.5 = 12.566$, a plainly false statement with no unknown left in it.
If the rate you are solving for is not in your differentiated equation, stop and re-differentiate.

**5. Dropping the $\frac13$ and using a cylinder.**
Gives $\frac{1}{24\pi} \approx 0.0132629$, a factor of 3 too small.

**6. Reporting the wrong units.**
The answer is a **length per time**, m/min.
Writing m³/min because the given rate was in m³/min is a lost mark that no numerical check will catch, which is why the units deserve their own line rather than being assumed.

**7. Answering the wrong question.**
"How fast is the height increasing" is $dh/dt$.
It is not $dr/dt$ (half as big), not $dV/dt$ (given), and not the base-area rate (0.25).

**8. Missing the bracket in the Möbius box.**
`1/8*pi` is 0.3927, not 0.0398.

### Where a wrong answer hides

Seven wrong formulas were scanned across a grid of nine heights and five volume rates, deliberately including the degenerate cases rather than excluding them.

- **At $dV/dt = 0$, every single one of the seven agrees with the truth**, because they all differ from it by a multiplicative factor. A spot-check performed on a stationary pile cannot fail, so it proves nothing.
- **At $h = 1$, exactly the two exponent slips hide** - writing $h^3$ or $h$ where $h^2$ belongs. Both are invisible at $h = 1$ and only there. Never sanity-check a related-rates formula at $h = 1$.
- The five constant-factor slips have no $h$-dependent hiding point at all; they are wrong at every height.
- **This question's own stem is a degenerate instance.** Because the diameter equals the height, $d = h = 4$ numerically at the instant asked. A student who reads the "width" as the height gets the right number here by luck. Change the ratio to $r = h$ and the same confusion costs a factor of 4.

## 10. Drill set

Score **sign** and **magnitude** separately.
A wrong sign means you mis-read the direction of a rate, which is a reading problem.
A wrong magnitude means you dropped or added a constant, which is an algebra problem.
The two need different remediation, so do not lump them together.

1. The same gravel pile: how fast is the height increasing when the pile is 2 m high?
2. The same pile at $h = 4$: how fast is the base **radius** increasing?
3. The same pile at $h = 4$: how fast is the **base area** increasing?
4. Gravel at 2 m³/min forms a cone whose base radius always equals its height. How fast is the height rising when $h = 3$?
5. Water flows into an inverted conical tank of top radius 3 m and depth 6 m at 0.8 m³/min. How fast is the depth rising when the depth is 2 m?
6. A spherical balloon is inflated at 10 cm³/s. How fast is the radius growing when $r = 5$ cm?
7. For that same balloon at that same instant, how fast is the surface area growing?
8. A 13 ft ladder leans on a wall. The foot is pulled away at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 5 ft from the wall?
9. A rocket rises vertically, tracked by a radar station 5 mi away. How fast is it rising when it is 4 mi high and its distance from the station grows at 2000 mi/h?
10. A cone has $r = 2$, $h = 4$, with $\dot r = 0.1$ and $\dot h = 0.05$ m/min, independently. How fast is the volume growing?
11. A 5 m lamp post, a 2 m person walking away at 1.5 m/s. How fast is the **tip** of the shadow moving?
12. For the original gravel pile, at what height is the height increasing at exactly 1 cm/min?

   |
   v

   every variable gains its own rate as a trailing factor
   |
   v

   no  -> you dropped a chain factor. Go back.
   yes -> continue
   |
   v
NOW SUBSTITUTE THE INSTANT, AND SOLVE
   |
   v
CHECK
   sign     : should it be growing or shrinking? does your sign say so?
