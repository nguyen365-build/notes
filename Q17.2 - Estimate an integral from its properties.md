# Q17.2 - Estimate an integral from its properties

**The question (Final Exam 2, item 3b; item 3 is worth 8 points and part (a) is the other half).**
"Use the properties of the definite integral to estimate the value of the integral
$$\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}\,dx.$$"

**The answer.**
$$1 \;\le\; \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \;\le\; 2$$
so the estimate is $\tfrac{1+2}{2} = 1.5$, and the true value is $\dfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5707963268$.
The floor is $0.5707963268$ below it and the ceiling is $0.4292036732$ above it.

**What to type into Möbius:** `1` for the lower bound and `2` for the upper bound.
If one box asks for a single estimate, `3/2`.
If a box asks for the exact value, `pi/2`.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2.1 The comparison property

> If $m \le f(x) \le M$ for every $x$ in $[a,b]$, then
> $$m\,(b-a) \;\le\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx \;\le\; M\,(b-a).$$

Read it as an area statement.
The integral is the area under the curve; $m(b-a)$ is the area of the rectangle that fits underneath, and $M(b-a)$ is the area of the rectangle that covers it.
The $(b-a)$ is the width of both rectangles, which is why leaving it out is not a small slip: it turns an area into a height.

### 2.2 What $m$ and $M$ are allowed to be

They are any numbers that sandwich $f$ on the whole interval.
They do **not** have to be the true extremes.
Using $m=0$ here is honest, legal, and gives $0 \le I \le 2$, which is correct and nearly useless.

The honest ones give the tightest correct answer, and the honest ones are exactly what part (a) computed.

### 2.3 Part (a)'s answers, which are this question's inputs

From `Q15.3`, on $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2+1}$ over $[-1,1]$:

| what | value | where |
| :--- | ---: | :--- |
| absolute maximum $M$ | $1$ | $x = 0$, an interior critical point |
| absolute minimum $m$ | $\tfrac12$ | $x = -1$ **and** $x = 1$, both endpoints |

Confirmed independently for this page rather than copied: a four-arm root census on $f'(x) = \dfrac{-2x}{(x^2+1)^2}$ finds exactly one critical point, at $x=0$; $f''(0) = -2 < 0$, so it is a maximum; $f'$ is never undefined because $(x^2+1)^2 \ge 1 > 0$; and a 400001-point grid scan reproduces $\max f = 1$ and $\min f = \tfrac12$.

### 2.4 The width

$$b - a = 1 - (-1) = 2.$$

Write it on its own line before you multiply.
Two minus signs in one subtraction is where the arithmetic dies.

## 3. The universal procedure

> 1. **Read the verb.** "Interval" wants two numbers presented as an interval; "estimate" wants a number; "bound" wants an inequality, possibly one-sided.
> 2. **Check the integrand is bounded on $[a,b]$.** Screen for poles and for points where the function blows up. If it is unbounded the property does not apply at all.
> 3. **Get $m$ and $M$.** If a part (a) on the same function and interval exists, they are already there. Otherwise run the closed-interval method: critical points, points where $f'$ is undefined, and both endpoints.
> 4. **Write $b-a$ down explicitly.**
> 5. **Multiply.** $m(b-a) \le I \le M(b-a)$.
> 6. **Answer the verb.** For "estimate", state the sandwich and then give the midpoint, or the exact value if you have it.
> 7. **Sanity-check.** The width of your interval is $(M-m)(b-a)$. If that is enormous relative to the bounds, say so and consider splitting.

Classifying the question in one line:

| what you see | which family | what it forces |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $f$ monotone on $[a,b]$ | Family 0 | $m$ and $M$ are the two endpoint values, no calculus |
| $f$ has an interior critical point | Family 1 (**this question**) | the closed-interval method, or part (a) |
| a part (a) on the same $f$ and $[a,b]$ | Family 2 (**also this question**) | two multiplications and nothing else |
| the interval is long and the bound is loose | Family 4 | split it |
| $f$ unbounded on $[a,b]$ | Family 6 | the property does not apply; say so |

## 4. Q17.2 worked four ways

### Route A - reuse part (a) (the intended route)

$m = \tfrac12$ and $M = 1$ come straight from item 3a.
$b - a = 2$.

$$\tfrac12 \cdot 2 \;\le\; \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \;\le\; 1 \cdot 2
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad 1 \le I \le 2.$$

Estimate: $I \approx \tfrac{1+2}{2} = 1.5$.
Two multiplications and one division.
About twenty seconds.

### Route B - the closed-interval method from scratch

$f'(x) = \dfrac{-2x}{(x^2+1)^2}$, which is zero only at $x=0$ and never undefined.
Candidates $-1, 0, 1$ give $f(-1) = \tfrac12$, $f(0) = 1$, $f(1) = \tfrac12$.
So $M = 1$ and $m = \tfrac12$, then as in Route A.

This is Route A with part (a) redone.
It is the route to use only if the question stands alone.

### Route C - sharpen with a non-constant comparison

For $u \ge 0$ the alternating expansion of $\dfrac{1}{1+u}$ gives
$$1 - u \;\le\; \frac{1}{1+u} \;\le\; 1 - u + u^2 .$$
Put $u = x^2$ and integrate term by term over $[-1,1]$:

$$\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^2)\,dx = \frac{4}{3} = 1.3333333333,
\qquad
\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^2+x^4)\,dx = \frac{26}{15} = 1.7333333333 .$$

So $1.3333333333 \le I \le 1.7333333333$, a width of $0.4$ against the constant sandwich's width of $1$.
Both comparisons were checked pointwise at 200001 samples, with zero violations in either direction.

This is a better answer and it is **not** what the question asked for.
It is worth knowing because it is what a "find a better estimate" follow-up wants.

### Route D - compute the integral exactly, then quote it

$$\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} = \Big[\arctan x\Big]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} = 1.5707963268 .$$

Correct, and it answers a different question.
See section 5 for what to do with it.

### Route comparison

| route | what it needs | time | risk | when to use it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - reuse part (a) | reading the page above | ~20 s | the $(b-a)$ slip | **always, on this item** |
| B - closed-interval method | one derivative, one table | ~2 min | arithmetic on three candidates | when the question stands alone |
| C - polynomial sandwich | recognising a geometric series | ~4 min | getting an inequality backwards | only if asked to sharpen |
| D - exact by antiderivative | knowing $\int \frac{dx}{1+x^2} = \arctan x$ | ~1 min | answering the wrong question | as a **check**, and as a bonus line |

**Under pressure, use Route A.**
It is the route the item was built for, and the eight points are shared with a part (a) you have already done.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

The verb is "estimate", so the answer has to contain a number.
Here is the full-credit shape, in the order a marker reads it.

> On $[-1,1]$ the function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2+1}$ is continuous, since $x^2+1 \ge 1 > 0$ for every real $x$.
> By part (a), its absolute maximum on $[-1,1]$ is $M = 1$ (at $x=0$) and its absolute minimum is $m = \tfrac12$ (at $x = \pm 1$).
> The interval has width $b - a = 1 - (-1) = 2$.
> By the comparison property of the definite integral,
> $$m(b-a) \le \int_{-1}^{1} f(x)\,dx \le M(b-a),
> \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
> 1 \le \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \le 2 .$$
> Taking the midpoint of these bounds gives the estimate $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1} \approx 1.5$.

That is the answer.
Then, on a separate line, if you have time:

> (For comparison, the exact value is $\big[\arctan x\big]_{-1}^{1} = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.571$, which lies inside the bounds.)

**Why the exact value goes on a separate line and not instead of the estimate.**
The question says "use the properties of the definite integral".
That phrase names the method, so the marks are for producing $m$, $M$, $b-a$ and the comparison property, not for producing a number that happens to be right.
A page reading only "$\pi/2$" has skipped the entire requested method.
This is an inference: no marking scheme for MATH 265 exists in any of the four course files.
But it is the safe inference, and it costs one extra line to satisfy both readings.

## 6. Verify before you commit

**The magnitude check.**
$f$ is between $\tfrac12$ and $1$, and the interval is $2$ wide, so the answer must be between $1$ and $2$.
Anything outside that is arithmetic, not method.

**The average-value check, which is the cheapest one that discriminates.**
$$\frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f = \frac{\pi/2}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4} = 0.7853981634 .$$
This number has to sit between $m$ and $M$.
It does: $0.5 < 0.7854 < 1$.
Any claimed $m$ above $0.7853981634$, or any claimed $M$ below it, is provably wrong.

**Numbers worth memorising for this item.**

| quantity | value |
| :--- | ---: |
| $m$, $M$ | $\tfrac12$, $1$ |
| $b-a$ | $2$ |
| bounds | $[1,\,2]$ |
| $\pi/2$ | $1.5707963268$ |
| $\pi/4$ (the average value) | $0.7853981634$ |
| midpoint estimate | $1.5$ |
| relative error of that estimate | $4.5070341449\%$ |

**The check that does NOT work, and this is the important one.**
"Does my interval contain the true value" cannot detect a bound that is too *loose*.
Over **201201** sampled loosenings ($m' \le \tfrac12$ in steps of $0.001$, $M' \ge 1$ in steps of $0.01$), **zero** failed to contain $\pi/2$.
So containment is a one-sided test: it catches over-tightening and never catches over-loosening.
If you want to know whether your $m$ and $M$ are right, you have to check the extremes themselves.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as a structure, not as a list of facts.
Every row is the same three-step calculation: find $m$ and $M$, find $b-a$, multiply.
What changes between families is only **where $m$ and $M$ come from**.

### Family 0 - monotone integrand: the endpoints are the extremes

**Trigger.** $f' $ has no zero inside $[a,b]$.
**Tool.** Evaluate $f(a)$ and $f(b)$; the smaller is $m$, the larger is $M$. No table needed.

| integral | $m$ | $M$ | $b-a$ | bounds | true value |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx$ | $0$ | $1$ | $1$ | $[0,\,1]$ | $0.3333333333$ |
| $\int_1^2 \frac{dx}{x}$ | $\tfrac12$ | $1$ | $1$ | $[0.5,\,1]$ | $0.6931471806$ |
| $\int_0^1 e^x\,dx$ | $1$ | $e$ | $1$ | $[1,\,2.7182818285]$ | $1.7182818285$ |
| $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\pi/2$ | $[0,\,1.5707963268]$ | $1$ |
| $\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec x\,dx$ (**Q17.1**) | $1$ | $2$ | $\pi/3$ | $[1.0471975512,\,2.0943951024]$ | $1.3169578969$ |

Notice that the last row is Q17.1, and that its bounds are the only ones in the table that are not round numbers.
That is because its $(b-a)$ is $\pi/3$ rather than an integer, and it is the reason Q17.1 hides the error that this question catches (section 9, trap 1).

### Family 1 - an interior critical point supplies one of the extremes

**Trigger.** $f'$ has a zero strictly inside $[a,b]$.
**Tool.** The full closed-interval method: critical points **and** both endpoints.

| integral | $m$ | $M$ | $b-a$ | bounds | true value |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ (**this question**) | $\tfrac12$ | $1$ | $2$ | $[1,\,2]$ | $1.5707963268$ |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+4}$ | $\tfrac15$ | $\tfrac14$ | $2$ | $[0.4,\,0.5]$ | $0.4636476090$ |
| $\int_{-2}^{2}\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ | $\tfrac15$ | $1$ | $4$ | $[0.8,\,4]$ | $2.2142974356$ |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}e^{-x^2}\,dx$ | $e^{-1}$ | $1$ | $2$ | $[0.7357588823,\,2]$ | $1.4936482656$ |
| $\int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx$ | $0$ | $1$ | $\pi$ | $[0,\,3.1415926536]$ | $2$ |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^2)\,dx$ | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $[0,\,2]$ | $1.3333333333$ |

Only three distinct shapes appear in that table.
The maximum sits at the interval midpoint in the first, second, fourth and sixth rows because each of those integrands is even on a symmetric interval; row three is the same function on a longer interval; row five is the one where the interior point supplies $M$ for a non-symmetric reason.

### Family 2 - part (a) hands you $m$ and $M$

**Trigger.** The item has two parts, on the same function over the same interval.
**Tool.** Copy the two numbers. Multiply by $b-a$. Stop.

| Final Exam 2 item | part (a) | $m$ | $M$ | $b-a$ | part (b) bounds | true value |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- | ---: |
| item 3 (8 pts) | Q15.3, $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ on $[-1,1]$ | $\tfrac12$ | $1$ | $2$ | $[1,\,2]$ | $1.5707963268$ |
| item 7 (8 pts) | Q15.5, $(x^2+2x)^{2/3}$ on $[-2,3]$ | $0$ | $15^{2/3}$ | $5$ | $[0,\,30.4110099779]$ | $10.6736300365$ |

**This is the most valuable single fact in category 17.**
On both of these items, part (b) is two multiplications, because part (a) already did the hard half.
Note also that item 7's floor is $0$, so its bound is genuinely one-sided in content even though it is written as two numbers.

### Family 3 - comparison against a non-constant dominating function

**Trigger.** The wording asks you to *improve* the estimate, or a constant sandwich is embarrassingly wide.
**Tool.** Find $g \le f \le h$ with $g$ and $h$ integrable in closed form, then integrate $g$ and $h$.

On this question, from $1-u \le \frac{1}{1+u} \le 1-u+u^2$ with $u = x^2$:

| sandwich | lower | upper | width | contains $\pi/2$ |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| constants $m=\tfrac12$, $M=1$ | $1$ | $2$ | $1$ | yes |
| polynomials $1-x^2$ and $1-x^2+x^4$ | $1.3333333333$ | $1.7333333333$ | $0.4$ | yes |

The polynomial sandwich is strictly inside the constant one, and the constant one is what the question asked for.

### Family 4 - additivity: splitting sharpens the bound

**Trigger.** The interval is long, or the question says "as accurately as you can".
**Tool.** $\int_a^b = \int_a^c + \int_c^b$, and bound each piece with its own $m$ and $M$.

| pieces $k$ | lower | upper | width |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $2$ | $1.0000000000$ | $2.0000000000$ | $1.0000000000$ |
| $4$ | $1.3000000000$ | $1.8000000000$ | $0.5000000000$ |
| $8$ | $1.4405882353$ | $1.6905882353$ | $0.2500000000$ |
| $20$ | $1.5199629945$ | $1.6199629945$ | $0.1000000000$ |

The width halves whenever $k$ doubles, which is the whole content of the family.

**A caution worth carrying.**
The usual formula for the width of a $k$-piece bound, $(M-m)(b-a)/k$, is derived by telescoping, and it telescopes **once per monotone branch**.
This integrand has two monotone branches on $[-1,1]$, so its width is $2(M-m)h$, twice the textbook value; at $k=20$ that is $0.1$, not $0.05$.
Verified against the monotone half $[0,1]$, where the textbook formula does hold.

### Family 5 - orientation and sign

**Trigger.** The limits are the wrong way round, or the integrand is negative or odd.
**Tool.** $\int_b^a = -\int_a^b$, and multiplying an inequality by $-1$ reverses it.

| integral | bounds | true value |
| :--- | :--- | ---: |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ | $[1,\,2]$ | $1.5707963268$ |
| $\int_{1}^{-1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ | $[-2,\,-1]$ | $-1.5707963268$ |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{-dx}{x^2+1}$ | $[-2,\,-1]$ | $-1.5707963268$ |
| $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x\,dx}{x^2+1}$ | $[-1,\,1]$ | $0$ |

The last row is the honest one: the property still applies to an odd integrand, gives $[-1,1]$, and tells you almost nothing.
Symmetry tells you the answer is exactly $0$ and the property cannot.

### Family 6 - when the property does NOT apply

**Trigger.** The integrand is unbounded on $[a,b]$.
**Tool.** Say so. There is no finite $M$, so there is no upper bound to write.

| integrand on $[-1,1]$ | bounded? | property applies? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ | yes, $[\tfrac12, 1]$ | yes |
| $1$ for $x<0$, $2$ for $x \ge 0$ | yes, $[1,2]$, but discontinuous | **yes** |
| $\frac{1}{x^2}$ | no, blows up at $0$ | no |

Row two is the one people get wrong.
The comparison property needs **boundedness and integrability**, not continuity.
That jump function integrates to exactly $3$, and $2 \le 3 \le 4$ holds.

### Family 7 - the three verbs

**Trigger.** Read the verb before you start.

| verb, and which question uses it | what to write | on that question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "find an interval where the value is located" (**Q17.1**) | both bounds, as an interval | $[\pi/3,\ 2\pi/3]$ |
| "estimate the value" (**Q17.2**) | the sandwich **and** a number | $1 \le I \le 2$, so $I \approx 1.5$ |
| "bound the value" (**Q17.3**) | an inequality; one side may suffice | $0 \le I \le 30.4110099779$ |

All three are the same calculation.
Only the last line differs.

### Family 8 - the estimate ladder (this question's own contribution)

**Trigger.** The verb is "estimate" and you have to commit to a number.

| estimate | formula | value | error against $1.5707963268$ |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: |
| lower bound alone | $m(b-a)$ | $1$ | $0.5707963268$ |
| upper bound alone | $M(b-a)$ | $2$ | $0.4292036732$ |
| **midpoint of the bounds** | $\tfrac{m+M}{2}(b-a)$ | $1.5$ | $0.0707963268$ |
| trapezoid on the endpoints | $\tfrac{f(a)+f(b)}{2}(b-a)$ | $1$ | $0.5707963268$ |
| midpoint rule | $f\!\left(\tfrac{a+b}{2}\right)(b-a)$ | $2$ | $0.4292036732$ |
| Simpson on three points | $\tfrac{b-a}{6}\big(f(a)+4f(\tfrac{a+b}{2})+f(b)\big)$ | $1.6666666667$ | $0.0958703399$ |
| exact | $\big[\arctan x\big]_{-1}^{1}$ | $1.5707963268$ | $0$ |

**The midpoint of the bounds is the best of the cheap estimates here, and on Q17.1 it is the worst.**

| question | midpoint of the bounds, error | midpoint rule, error | winner |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| Q17.1, $\int_0^{\pi/3}\sec$ | $0.2538384299$ | $0.1077583208$ | midpoint **rule** |
| Q17.2, $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2+1}$ | $0.0707963268$ | $0.4292036732$ | midpoint of the **bounds** |

The reason is structural rather than lucky.
On Q17.2 the maximum sits exactly at the interval midpoint, so the midpoint rule evaluates $f$ at its highest point and returns $M(b-a)$, which is the ceiling itself.
A rule that returns one end of your interval is not an estimate of anything.

So the answer to "which estimate should I write" is **the midpoint of the bounds**, because it is the only one of the cheap options that cannot degenerate to a bound you already wrote down.

### Family 9 - symmetry, and the trap inside it

**Trigger.** The integrand is even and the interval is symmetric about $0$.
**Tool.** $\int_{-a}^{a} f = 2\int_0^a f$ when $f$ is even, and $0$ when $f$ is odd.

| step | on $[0,1]$ | doubled |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| $m$, $M$ | $\tfrac12$, $1$ | unchanged |
| $b-a$ | $1$ | $2$ |
| bounds | $[0.5,\,1]$ | $[1,\,2]$ |
| true value | $\pi/4 = 0.7853981634$ | $\pi/2 = 1.5707963268$ |

Symmetry costs nothing and gains nothing on this question, and it opens a hole.

**The trap:** the un-doubled half-interval bound $[0.5,\,1]$ is numerically identical to the answer you get by dropping $(b-a)$ altogether.
Two completely different mistakes land on the same wrong interval, so seeing $[0.5,\,1]$ on your page does not tell you which one you made.
Neither contains $\pi/2$.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one costs marks.

**1. Dropping the $(b-a)$ factor and writing $\tfrac12 \le I \le 1$.**
This is the mistake category 17 exists to punish.
**On this question it is caught**, because $\pi/2 = 1.5708$ is above the mutilated ceiling of $1$.
**On Q17.1 the identical mistake is invisible**, because there the mutilated interval is $[1,2]$ and the true value $1.3170$ sits inside it.

The discriminator is not luck, it is the width:

| question | $b-a$ | correct | with $(b-a)$ dropped | true value | verdict |
| :--- | ---: | :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| Q17.1 | $1.0471975512$ | $[1.0471975512,\,2.0943951024]$ | $[1,\,2]$ | $1.3169578969$ | contains it, **hidden** |
| **Q17.2** | $2$ | $[1,\,2]$ | $[0.5,\,1]$ | $1.5707963268$ | outside it, **caught** |
| Q17.3 | $5$ | $[0,\,30.4110099779]$ | $[0,\,6.0822019956]$ | $10.6736300365$ | outside it, **caught** |

Dropping the factor divides your interval by $(b-a)$, so the slip survives a self-check exactly when $(b-a)$ is close to $1$.
Q17.1's width is $1.0472$, which is within $5\%$ of $1$; this question's is $2$.
For this integrand the largest width at which the slip would still hide is $M/\bar f = 4/\pi = 1.2732395447$, and $2$ is comfortably past it.

**This contrast is the strongest argument in the whole category against trusting a self-check.**
The same student, making the same mistake, on two questions from the same category, gets caught on one and not on the other, and nothing about the answer they wrote tells them which case they are in.

**2. Answering with an interval when the verb said "estimate".**
Costs the last mark, not the method marks.
One extra line fixes it.

**3. Answering with $\pi/2$ alone.**
The question named the method.
Give the sandwich first, then the exact value as a bonus.

**4. Reading the minimum at $x=0$.**
$x=0$ is where the *maximum* is.
The resulting bound $[2,2]$ does not contain $\pi/2$, so this one is caught.

**5. Reading the maximum somewhere other than $x=0$.**
Whether this is caught depends on where.
The dividing line is the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals point $c$, the place where $f(c)$ equals the average value $\pi/4$:
$$c = \sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi} - 1} = 0.5227232009 .$$
Reading $M$ anywhere in $[0, c)$ still produces an interval containing $\pi/2$, so the mistake **hides**; reading it beyond $c$ is caught.
That is $52.27\%$ of the half-interval, confirmed by an independent 1001-point scan rather than by re-deriving the formula.
The boundary is not the midpoint and it is not obvious.

**6. Sign slips on $b-a = 1-(-1)$.**
Write the substitution out.

**7. Using the property on an unbounded integrand.**
Screen first. See Family 6.

**8. Believing that "my interval contains the answer" is a check.**
It only catches over-tightening.
See section 6.

**How the true value was established, and to what precision.**
$\pi/2$ was checked against four equivalent closed forms and against two independent quadratures.
Tanh-sinh at level 9 and Gauss-Legendre at $n=40$ agree with the closed form and with each other to under $10^{-13}$.

Two precision findings were measured rather than assumed, and both bound how many digits this page is allowed to print.

First, **Simpson's observed order on this integrand is $6$, not the textbook $4$**: $5.9948605833$ at $n=64 \to 128$ and $6.1019065780$ at $n=128 \to 256$.
The reason is analytic and was verified: $f'''(x) = \dfrac{24x(1-x^2)}{(x^2+1)^4}$ vanishes at **both** endpoints $x = \pm 1$, which kills the $h^4$ term of the Euler-Maclaurin error expansion.
A control integrand whose third derivative does not vanish at the endpoints ($\int_0^1 e^x$) shows the textbook order $4$ on the same meter, so the $6$ is a property of this integrand and not of the instrument.

Second, **both quadrature ladders had to be bounded before they were read.**
Simpson reaches the roundoff floor at $n=256$ (absolute error $4\times10^{-15}$), and past that rung the measured order goes negative, which is float noise rather than a degraded method.
Tanh-sinh is best at levels 8 and 9 and gets *worse* from level 10 onward as summation error accumulates.
A digit-lock inherits the precision of the method that produced it, so every decimal on this page is printed at six to ten places and none of them was widened past what the computation actually resolved.

**The root census, and why its arms are reported separately.**
$f'(x) = -2x/(x^2+1)^2$ has exactly one zero on $[-1,1]$, at $x = 0$, and $0$ is the exact centre of the interval.
On a grid with an odd number of nodes, $x=0$ **is** a node, so a strict sign-change arm reports **zero** changes while an exact-grid-zero arm reports **one**.
On a grid with an even number of nodes the two swap.
Both grids were run and both arms reported; their sum is $1$ either way.
A census run only one way here would have printed `0` and looked exactly like a census that never ran.
Two further arms were run: a value-turn arm, which reports $2$ because $f'$ turns at the inflection points $x = \pm 1/\sqrt3 = \pm 0.5773502692$; and an undefined-point arm, which reports $0$ because $(x^2+1)^2 \ge 1 > 0$.
Controls: the same census finds the one critical point of $\cos$ on $[-1,1.3]$ where the root is off the grid, reproduces the on-node masking on $[-1,1]$, finds none for $\exp$, and detects a cusp on $x^{2/3}$ that a sign arm alone would misread.
