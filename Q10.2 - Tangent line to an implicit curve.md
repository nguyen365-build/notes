## What Q10.2 adds, and what it inherits

This question is a **join**.
It is the last question in the implicit-differentiation category, and it does not introduce a new differentiation technique.
It takes two things you already have and makes you use them in the same four minutes.

**Inherited from `Q10.1 - Find the derivative implicitly.md`:**

- the implicit method itself - differentiate both sides in $x$, attach $y'$ to every $y$ by the chain rule, collect the $y'$ terms, divide;
- the fact that the answer legitimately contains $y$ as well as $x$, which is the single biggest source of doubt in this topic;
- the whole verification pattern used below: prove the point is **on the curve** before you say anything about a slope, then check the slope against arms that never saw your formula.

**Inherited from `Q9.1 - Tangent line perpendicular to a given line.md`:**

- assembling a line once you have a slope and a point, via $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$;
- the geometry around it - normals, parallels, perpendiculars;
- the habit of reading the stem for *what object is being demanded*.

**What Q10.2 contributes that neither parent does:**

1. **An implicit $y'$ needs BOTH coordinates.** An explicit $f'(x)$ is a function of $x$ alone, so "evaluate at the point" means "substitute one number". An implicit $y'$ is a function of $x$ **and** $y$, so you must carry the point as a pair. This is the structural difference, and it is why the question hands you $(1,1)$ rather than just $x = 1$.
2. **The point must be verified.** Because $y'$ depends on $y$, feeding it a $y$ that does not belong to that $x$ produces a confident, wrong, unfalsifiable number. Nothing in the algebra complains.
3. **A slope is not a line.** Q9.1 asked for values of $x$. Q10.2 asks for an *equation*. Stopping at $m = 2$ answers a question that was not asked.

## 0. The 30-second version

```
GIVEN  y^3 + y x^2 + x^2 = 3 y^2   at (1,1)

  0) CHECK the point:  1 + 1 + 1 = 3  and  3(1) = 3.   ON the curve.  [1 line, always do it]

  1) d/dx both sides, y' on every y:
         3y^2 y' + (x^2 y' + 2xy) + 2x = 6y y'

  2) SUBSTITUTE (1,1) NOW, while it is still linear in y':
         3m + m + 2 + 2 = 6m      ->      4m + 4 = 6m      ->      m = 2

  3) BUILD THE LINE:  y - 1 = 2(x - 1)      ->      y = 2x - 1

  4) CHECK:  x=1 -> y=1 (through the point).       ANSWER: y = 2x - 1
```

Four steps, no fractions, no algebra with letters in it.
Step 2 is the whole trick and section 4 explains why it beats the textbook order.

## 2. The four things you must have cold

### 2a. The chain factor, by term

Every $y$ is secretly $y(x)$. Differentiating in $x$ therefore drops a $y'$ each time you pass through one.

| Term | $\dfrac{d}{dx}$ | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^3$ | $3y^2 y'$ | chain rule, outer power then inner $y$ |
| $y x^2$ | $x^2 y' + 2xy$ | **product** rule: both factors vary |
| $x^2$ | $2x$ | no $y$, no $y'$ |
| $3y^2$ | $6y y'$ | chain rule |
| $c$ (a constant) | $0$ | |

The term that costs people marks is $yx^2$.
It looks like "$x^2$ times a letter", and a tired reader differentiates only the $x^2$ or only the $y$.
It is a product of two varying things and it needs the product rule, both halves.

### 2b. The point-slope form

$$y - y_1 = m(x - x_1) \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad y = m(x - x_1) + y_1 = mx + (y_1 - m x_1)$$

The intercept is $y_1 - m x_1$.
Here $1 - 2(1) = -1$, so $y = 2x - 1$.
Get the sign of that subtraction wrong and you produce $y = 2x + 1$, which passes through $(1,3)$ - a point not on the curve at all ($F(1,3) = 4 \neq 0$, verified exactly).

### 2c. What "on the curve" means, and how to check it in one line

The point $(a,b)$ is on the relation $F(x,y) = 0$ when substituting **both** coordinates gives $0$.
Here, with $F(x,y) = y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 - 3y^2$:

$$F(1,1) = 1 + 1 + 1 - 3 = 0 \quad \checkmark$$

Do it in exact arithmetic, in your head, before you differentiate anything.
It costs one line and it is the only defence against the trap in section 9.

### 2d. When the slope form does not apply

Write the relation as $F(x,y) = 0$. Then

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y}, \qquad F_x = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}, \quad F_y = \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}$$

and the whole thing is legal only while $F_y \neq 0$.
For this curve $F_y = 3y^2 + x^2 - 6y$, and at $(1,1)$ that is $3 + 1 - 6 = -2$.
Comfortably non-zero, so the tangent has a finite slope and the point-slope form is the right tool.
If $F_y$ had been $0$ with $F_x \neq 0$, the tangent would be **vertical** and the answer would be $x = \text{constant}$, with no $m$ anywhere in it.
Section 8 Family 3 handles that case; on this curve it happens at $y = \sqrt{3}$, nowhere near $(1,1)$.

You do not have to use the $-F_x/F_y$ language on the exam - collecting $y'$ terms by hand gives the same thing.
But it is the fastest way to *screen* for a vertical tangent, and it is what the verification arms in section 6 use.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1. Verify the point.**
> Substitute both coordinates into the original relation. Both sides must agree exactly.
> If they do not, stop - either you misread the point or the question is a Family 9 trap.
>
> **Step 2. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$.**
> Every $y$ carries a $y'$. Every product of two varying factors gets the product rule.
> Do not simplify. Do not solve for $y'$ yet.
>
> **Step 3. Substitute the point into the differentiated equation.**
> You now have a linear equation in the single unknown $m = y'$, with numbers for coefficients.
>
> **Step 4. Solve for $m$.**
> Collect the $m$ terms on one side, the constants on the other, divide once.
>
> **Step 5. Screen the denominator.**
> If step 4 gave $0 \cdot m = (\text{nonzero})$, the tangent is vertical: answer $x = x_1$.
> If it gave $0 \cdot m = 0$, the point is singular and there is no unique tangent - say so.
>
> **Step 6. Build the line and write it down.**
> $y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$, then simplify to $y = mx + b$.
>
> **Step 7. Check.** Put $x = x_1$ into your line; you must get $y_1$ back.

Steps 3 and 4 are deliberately in that order.
The textbook order is to solve for $y'$ symbolically first and substitute afterwards, and section 4 measures what that costs.

### Classify the question in five seconds

| The stem says | You are in | You must produce |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "at the point $(a,b)$" | Family 0 - this question | a **line** |
| "at the point where $x = a$" | Family 1 | find $b$ first; possibly **several** lines |

## 4. Q10.2 worked three ways

### Route A - solve for $y'$ symbolically, then substitute (the textbook order)

Differentiate:

$$3y^2 y' + x^2 y' + 2xy + 2x = 6y y'$$

Move every $y'$ term left, everything else right:

$$3y^2 y' + x^2 y' - 6y y' = -2xy - 2x$$

Factor:

$$y'\left(3y^2 + x^2 - 6y\right) = -2x(y+1)$$

Divide:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-2x(y+1)}{3y^2 + x^2 - 6y}\;}$$

Now substitute $(1,1)$:

$$m = \frac{-2(1)(1+1)}{3(1) + 1 - 6} = \frac{-4}{-2} = 2$$

Then $y - 1 = 2(x-1)$, so $y = 2x - 1$.

**Cost.** Seven lines, one factorisation, one division of signed quantities, and a fraction with a negative numerator over a negative denominator - two sign opportunities in a single step.
**Value.** You end up holding the general formula, which you need if the question later asks for horizontal tangents, a second point, or $y''$.

### Route B - substitute first, then solve (recommended)

Differentiate exactly as in Route A and stop:

$$3y^2 y' + x^2 y' + 2xy + 2x = 6y y'$$

Now put $x = 1$, $y = 1$, $y' = m$ straight in:

$$3m + m + 2 + 2 = 6m$$

$$4m + 4 = 6m \quad \Longrightarrow \quad 4 = 2m \quad \Longrightarrow \quad m = 2$$

Then $y - 1 = 2(x-1)$, so $y = 2x - 1$.

**Cost.** Three lines. No factorisation, no letters, no fraction - the arithmetic is $3+1$ against $6$.
**Value.** Every sign error opportunity in Route A's factor-and-divide step is gone, because you never divide by a symbolic expression.

This is the route to use under pressure, and it is worth being explicit about *why it is legal*.
The differentiated equation is an identity that holds at every point of the curve.
$(1,1)$ is a point of the curve (you checked in step 1).
So the equation holds there, and it is linear in $y'$, so solving it there gives the value of $y'$ there.
Nothing is lost.

**The line that this is often confused with, and which is genuinely illegal:** substituting the point into the **original** relation before differentiating.
If you set $y = 1$ in $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$ you get $1 + x^2 + x^2 = 3$, an equation in $x$ alone with no $y$ left to differentiate, and $y'$ has vanished from the universe.
Differentiate **first**, substitute **second**. That is the whole rule.

### Route C - solve for $x$ explicitly and reciprocate

This relation happens to be quadratic in $x$, so you can make $x$ an explicit function of $y$:

$$x^2(y + 1) = 3y^2 - y^3 = y^2(3 - y) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = \pm\, y\sqrt{\frac{3-y}{1+y}}$$

valid for $-1 < y \le 3$.
At $y = 1$ the radicand is $2/2 = 1$, giving $x = \pm 1$, and the $+$ branch is our point.
Differentiate with respect to $y$, using $u = \frac{3-y}{1+y}$ so that $u' = \frac{-4}{(1+y)^2}$:

$$\frac{dx}{dy} = \sqrt{u} + \frac{y\,u'}{2\sqrt{u}} \;\Big|_{y=1} = 1 + \frac{1 \cdot (-1)}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Then $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{dx/dy} = 2$, and the line is $y = 2x - 1$ as before.

**Cost.** Much longer, needs a square root and a quotient inside it, and needs you to notice the relation is quadratic in $x$ - which it usually will not be.
**Value.** It is a genuinely independent confirmation, and it is one of the three verification arms in section 6. As an exam route it is a trap; as a check it is excellent.

### Route D - solve for $y$ explicitly

The relation is a **cubic** in $y$. There is a closed form, and you will not want it.
Not viable.

### Route comparison

| Route | Lines | Sign risk | Gives you the general $y'$? | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - solve symbolically, then substitute | 7 | two sign steps (factor, then divide two negatives) | yes | Use when a later part needs the formula |
| **B - substitute, then solve** | **3** | **one, and it is integer arithmetic** | no | **Use this one under pressure** |
| C - explicit $x(y)$, reciprocate | 10+ | high | no | Verification only |
| D - explicit $y(x)$ | - | - | - | Not viable, cubic in $y$ |

**Use Route B.**
It is the shortest, it removes the only genuinely error-prone step, and this stem does not demand a general formula.
If the question had said "find $dy/dx$", you would be in Q10.1 and Route A would be forced.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The eight-second check

Put $x = 1$ into your answer. If it does not return $y = 1$, the line does not pass through the given point and it is wrong before anything else is considered.

$$y = 2(1) - 1 = 1 \quad \checkmark$$

That single substitution kills the most common arithmetic slip in this question ($y = 2x + 1$).

### The magnitude check

$m = 2$ is a positive slope of modest size.
Sanity: at $(1,1)$, $F_x = 2xy + 2x = 4$ and $F_y = 3y^2 + x^2 - 6y = -2$.
A positive $F_x$ over a negative $F_y$ gives a positive $-F_x/F_y$.
If your slope came out negative, you dropped a sign somewhere in the collect step.

### The real check: a tangent line meets the curve in a DOUBLE root

This is what "tangent" means, and it is a check you can actually run.
Substitute the line $y = 2x - 1$ back into the relation:

$$F(x,\, 2x-1) = 10x^3 - 24x^2 + 18x - 4 = 2(x-1)^2(5x-2)$$

The factor $(x-1)^2$ is squared. That is the tangency, algebraically.
Verified in exact rational arithmetic: $P(1) = 0$, $P'(1) = 0$, and $P''(1) = 12 \neq 0$, so it is a double root and not a triple one.

A merely *secant* line through $(1,1)$ gives $(x-1)^1$ and nothing more - confirmed for slopes $1$, $3$ and $7/4$, each of which produces $P'(1) \neq 0$.

**Free bonus fact.** The remaining factor $5x - 2$ says the tangent line crosses the curve again at $x = 2/5$, that is at $\left(\tfrac{2}{5}, -\tfrac{1}{5}\right)$ - verified exactly on the curve.
A tangent line is only locally tangent; it is perfectly entitled to hit the curve elsewhere.

One methodological note, because it bit during this run.
The Richardson ladder was first run with a fixed step $h = 0.04$ at every sample point.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read this as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
Every family below is the same two-part machine - *get a slope from $-F_x/F_y$*, then *do something geometric with it*.
What changes from family to family is only which of the two parts the examiner hides.

Every entry in every table was put through the same gate as the headline answer: the point was proved on the curve, the slope was checked against a formula-blind $-F_x/F_y$, and the finished line was checked for a double root.

### Family 0 - the point is given and is on the curve

**Trigger:** "at the point $(a,b)$".
**Tool:** the seven-step procedure. Route B.
**Shape:** differentiate, substitute the pair, solve for $m$, build the line.

| Relation | Point | $m$ | Tangent line |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$ | $(1,1)$ | $2$ | $y = 2x - 1$ |
| $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ | $(3,4)$ | $-3/4$ | $y = -\tfrac34 x + \tfrac{25}{4}$ |
| $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 3$ | $(1,1)$ | $-1$ | $y = -x + 2$ |
| $x^3 + y^3 = 9xy$ | $(2,4)$ | $4/5$ | $y = \tfrac45 x + \tfrac{12}{5}$ |
| $y^2 = x^3(2-x)$ | $(1,1)$ | $1$ | $y = x$ |
| $\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1$ | $\left(\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}, \sqrt2\right)$ | $-2/3$ | $y = -\tfrac23 x + 2\sqrt2$ |

Read the structure: six curves, six slopes, and in every single case the slope is $-F_x/F_y$ evaluated at a pair.
There is one method here, not six.

### Family 1 - only one coordinate is given

**Trigger:** "at the point where $x = a$" (or "$y = b$").
**Tool:** solve the relation for the missing coordinate **first**, then Family 0.
**The catch:** there may be more than one such point, and then there is more than one tangent line. Give them all.

On the exam curve at $x = 0$: $F(0,y) = y^3 - 3y^2 = y^2(y-3)$, so $y = 0$ or $y = 3$.
Two points, and they behave completely differently - see Family 2 and the singular-point note below.

At $x = 1$: $y^3 - 3y^2 + y + 1 = 0$, which factors as $(y-1)(y^2 - 2y - 1) = 0$, so $y = 1$ or $y = 1 \pm \sqrt2$.
All three are inside the domain $-1 < y \le 3$ ($1-\sqrt2 \approx -0.414$ and $1+\sqrt2 \approx 2.414$), and all three were verified on the curve.
So the vertical line $x = 1$ meets this curve **three** times, and "the tangent at $x = 1$" would be ambiguous.
That ambiguity is exactly why the real Q10.2 hands you the full pair $(1,1)$.

### Family 2 - horizontal tangents

**Trigger:** "where is the tangent horizontal", "find the points with a horizontal tangent".
**Tool:** set the **numerator** $F_x = 0$ and solve simultaneously with $F = 0$. Then confirm $F_y \neq 0$ there.

For the exam curve, $F_x = 2x(y+1) = 0$ means $x = 0$ or $y = -1$.
And $y = -1$ is **not on the curve at all**: $F(x,-1) = -1 - x^2 + x^2 - 3 = -4$ for every $x$, verified exactly.
So only $x = 0$ survives, giving $(0,0)$ and $(0,3)$.

| Point | $F_x$ | $F_y$ | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(0,3)$ | $0$ | $9$ | genuine **horizontal** tangent, $y = 3$ |
| $(0,0)$ | $0$ | $0$ | **singular**: $0/0$, no unique tangent |

The $(0,0)$ row is the interesting one and it generalises.
When both partials vanish you have a singular point - a node, a cusp, or an isolated point - and $-F_x/F_y$ is meaningless there.
Do not report a slope of $0$; report that the point is singular.

### Family 3 - vertical tangents (the slope form does not apply)

**Trigger:** "prove the curve has a vertical tangent at ...", or a denominator that vanishes.
**Tool:** set the **denominator** $F_y = 0$, solve with $F = 0$, and confirm $F_x \neq 0$ so the ratio is $\infty$ and not $0/0$.
**The answer is a line of the form $x = c$.** There is no $m$, and point-slope form cannot be used.

`MATH265_Categorized.md` sets exactly this on the cardioid $x^2 + y^2 = (2x^2+2y^2-x)^2$ at $(1,0)$, so it is a live exam target.

On the exam curve: $F_y = 3y^2 + x^2 - 6y = 0$ together with $x^2 = \frac{y^2(3-y)}{1+y}$ reduces exactly to $2y^3 - 6y = 0$, i.e. $2y(y^2 - 3) = 0$.
Inside the domain $-1 < y \le 3$ that leaves $y = 0$ (the singular point again) and $y = \sqrt{3}$.

$$y = \sqrt3, \qquad x^2 = 6\sqrt3 - 9, \qquad x = \pm\sqrt{6\sqrt3 - 9} \approx \pm 1.1799596796$$

Two genuine vertical tangents, $x = \pm 1.17996$, with $F_x = 2x(y+1) \approx \pm 6.447 \neq 0$ at each (the sign follows $x$) - verified.
Neither is anywhere near $(1,1)$: the nearest is $0.732$ away in $y$.

### Family 4 - the normal line

**Trigger:** "normal line", "perpendicular to the curve".
**Tool:** Family 0, then flip: $m_{\text{normal}} = -1/m$.

At $(1,1)$ on the exam curve, $m = 2$, so the normal has slope $-\tfrac12$:

$$y - 1 = -\tfrac12(x-1) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad y = -\tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac32$$

Check: $2 \times \left(-\tfrac12\right) = -1$. This is Q9.1's condition, reused verbatim.
If the tangent is horizontal the normal is vertical ($x = c$), and if the tangent is vertical the normal is horizontal - the one case where Family 3 and Family 4 swap places.

### Family 5 - a prescribed slope (the Q9.1 join)

**Trigger:** "where is the tangent parallel to $\dots$", "perpendicular to $\dots$", "has slope $k$".
**Tool:** set $-F_x/F_y = k$ (for perpendicular, $k = -1/m_{\text{given}}$) and solve **simultaneously with $F = 0$**.

This is the family that most often catches people, because it is two equations in two unknowns rather than one substitution.
You cannot solve $-F_x/F_y = k$ on its own; it describes a curve of its own, and you need its intersection with the original relation.

For the exam curve, "tangent parallel to $y = 2x + 7$" means $-2x(y+1) = 2(3y^2+x^2-6y)$ together with $y^3+yx^2+x^2 = 3y^2$, and $(1,1)$ is one solution by construction.

### Family 6 - transcendental relations

**Trigger:** the relation contains $\sin$, $\cos$, $e$, $\ln$.
**Tool:** identical. The chain factor is still just $y'$; only the outer derivative changes.

| Relation | Point | $m$ | Tangent line |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\sin(x+y) = 2x - 2y$ | $(0,0)$ | $1/3$ | $y = \tfrac{x}{3}$ |
| $x e^{y} + y = 2$ | $(2,0)$ | $-1/3$ | $y = -\tfrac{x}{3} + \tfrac23$ |

Both verified against formula-blind complex-step partials and both give a genuine double root.
Note how the numbers stay small and rational-looking: examiners choose the point so that $\sin$, $\cos$ or $e^y$ evaluates to $0$ or $1$ there.
If your arithmetic produces $e^{2.7}$, you have probably substituted the wrong coordinate.

### Family 7 - the second derivative at the point

**Trigger:** "$y''$", "concavity", "is the curve concave up at ...".
**Tool:** differentiate $y'$ again with the quotient rule, then **substitute the already-known $y'$** wherever one appears.

$$y'' = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{-2x(y+1)}{3y^2+x^2-6y}\right], \qquad \text{with } N' = -2(y+1) - 2xy', \quad D' = 6yy' + 2x - 6y'$$

At $(1,1)$, with $y' = 2$: $N = -4$, $D = -2$, $N' = -8$, $D' = 2$, so

$$y'' = \frac{N'D - ND'}{D^2} = \frac{(-8)(-2) - (-4)(2)}{4} = \frac{16+8}{4} = 6$$

$y'' = 6 > 0$, so the curve is concave up at $(1,1)$ and the tangent line lies **below** it locally.
Verified against a Richardson-extrapolated second difference: $5.99999986$ at $h = 0.005$.

The mark-losing move here is re-deriving $y'$ symbolically inside the second differentiation instead of substituting the number you already have.

### Family 8 - where the tangent meets the curve again

**Trigger:** "find the other point where the tangent line intersects the curve".
**Tool:** substitute the line into the relation, divide out the known double factor $(x-x_1)^2$, solve what remains.

$$F(x, 2x-1) = 10x^3 - 24x^2 + 18x - 4 = 2(x-1)^2(5x-2) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x = \tfrac25, \; y = -\tfrac15$$

Because the curve is a cubic and the tangency already accounts for two of the three roots, there is exactly one other meeting point, and it is always rational when the tangent point is.
This family is also the cleanest *self-check* for Family 0: if $(x - x_1)$ does not divide out twice, your slope is wrong.

### Family 9 - the point is not on the curve

**Trigger:** nothing in the wording. That is the point.
**Tool:** step 1 of the procedure.

If a question offers $(1,2)$ on this curve, then $F(1,2) = 8 + 2 + 1 - 12 = -1 \neq 0$ and the point is off the curve.
Every subsequent line of algebra will still work.
$y'$ will evaluate to a finite number; the point-slope form will produce a tidy line.
Nothing will complain, and the answer will be worth nothing.

This family exists because implicit $y'$ takes two inputs and validates neither.
It cannot happen on an explicit $f'(x)$ question, which is precisely why Q10.2 needs a check that Q8 never did.

### Family 10 - the tangent line as an approximation

**Trigger:** "use the tangent line to estimate $y$ when $x = 1.05$".
**Tool:** Family 0, then evaluate the line.

$y \approx 2(1.05) - 1 = 1.1$.
This is linearization (Q12.1-Q12.3) with the derivative arriving implicitly instead of explicitly, and it is the natural bridge between the two categories.
Since $y'' = 6 > 0$ here, the curve is concave up and the tangent **underestimates** - a free sanity check on the direction of the error.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each actually costs marks on this specific item.

**1. Reporting the slope instead of the line.**
The stem says "find the equation of the tangent line".
$m = 2$ is not an equation.
On an answer-only platform this is a complete zero for a question you solved correctly, and it is the most common way to lose all four points.

**2. Substituting the point into the original relation before differentiating.**
Setting $y = 1$ first turns the relation into $1 + x^2 + x^2 = 3$, and $y'$ no longer exists.
Differentiate first, substitute second.
Note carefully that substituting into the **differentiated** equation before solving for $y'$ is not this trap - it is Route B, and it is the recommended method.
The difference is whether $y$ was still a function when you differentiated.

**3. Never checking that $(1,1)$ is on the curve.**
Here it is, so nothing goes wrong.
In Family 9 nothing goes wrong either, right up until the mark.
One line, always.

**4. Dropping the product rule on $yx^2$.**
This is the term the curve is built around.
Writing $\frac{d}{dx}[yx^2] = x^2y'$ (forgetting $2xy$) gives $m = 1$; writing $2xy$ only (forgetting $x^2y'$) gives a different wrong answer again.

**5. Forgetting $y'$ on one $y$-term but not the others.**
Forgetting it on $y^3$ gives $m = 1.4$; forgetting it on $3y^2$ gives $m = 0.5$.
Both are plausible-looking numbers.

**6. Sign error collecting the $6yy'$ term.**
Moving it across without flipping its sign gives $m = -2$ - the right magnitude, wrong direction.
The magnitude being right is what makes this one dangerous.

**7. The intercept subtraction.**
$b = y_1 - mx_1 = 1 - 2 = -1$. Writing $y = 2x + 1$ instead is a pure arithmetic slip that the eight-second check catches instantly.

**8. Using the slope form where it does not apply.**
Not an issue at $(1,1)$, but on a Family 3 variant the answer is $x = c$ and there is no $m$ at all.

### Where the wrong answers hide

Following the Q10.1 finding that *whether a wrong answer has a hiding point is a property of the curve, not of the topic*, all four slips were scanned over 120 points spanning both branches of this curve, with no convenience exclusions.

| Slip | $m$ at $(1,1)$ | Hides anywhere on the curve? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Forgot $y'$ on $y^3$ | $1.4$ | **no** |
| Missed the product rule on $yx^2$ | $1.0$ | **yes - at $(0,3)$** |
| Sign slip on the $6yy'$ term | $-2$ | **yes - at $(0,3)$** |
| Forgot $y'$ on $3y^2$ | $0.5$ | **no** |

Two of the four are exactly invisible at $(0,3)$, because both collapse to $0$ wherever the true slope is $0$, and $(0,3)$ is the horizontal tangent.
That point is fully reachable - $F(0,3) = 0$ exactly - and it is the *second* most convenient point on this curve, the one a student would naturally reach for as a spot-check.

The practical consequence: **do not verify an implicit derivative at a horizontal tangent.**
At a point where the true slope is zero, any error that multiplies the numerator disappears.
$(1,1)$ is a good check point precisely because none of the four slips survives there.

## 10. Drill set

Score **sign** and **magnitude** separately.
A wrong magnitude means you mis-differentiated a term; a wrong sign means you mishandled the collect-and-divide step.
They need different remediation, so do not lump them together.

1. Find the tangent line to $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ at $(3,4)$.
2. Find the tangent line to $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 3$ at $(1,1)$.
3. Find the tangent line to $x^3 + y^3 = 9xy$ at $(2,4)$.
4. Find the tangent line to $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$ at $(0,3)$.
5. Find every point on $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$ where the tangent is vertical.
6. Find the **normal** line to $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$ at $(1,1)$.
7. Find $y''$ at $(1,1)$ on $y^3 + yx^2 + x^2 = 3y^2$, and say whether the curve is concave up or down there.
8. The tangent at $(1,1)$ meets that curve at one other point. Find it.
9. Find the tangent line to $\sin(x+y) = 2x - 2y$ at $(0,0)$.
10. Find the tangent line to $x e^{y} + y = 2$ at $(2,0)$.
11. Find the tangent line to $\dfrac{x^2}{9} + \dfrac{y^2}{4} = 1$ at $\left(\dfrac{3}{\sqrt2}, \sqrt2\right)$.
12. Find the tangent line to $y^2 = x^3(2-x)$ at $(1,1)$.

### Answer key

| # | Answer | Decimal | Family and method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $y = -\tfrac34 x + \tfrac{25}{4}$ | $m = -0.75$, $b = 6.25$ | **F0** - $m = -x/y$; the classic |
| 2 | $y = -x + 2$ | $m = -1$, $b = 2$ | **F0** - product rule on $xy$ |
| 3 | $y = \tfrac45 x + \tfrac{12}{5}$ | $m = 0.8$, $b = 2.4$ | **F0** - folium; product rule on $9xy$ |
| 4 | $y = 3$ | $m = 0$ | **F2** - $F_x = 0$, $F_y = 9 \neq 0$ |
| 5 | $x = \pm\sqrt{6\sqrt3 - 9}$, $y = \sqrt3$ | $x \approx \pm 1.1799596796$ | **F3** - $F_y = 0$ reduces to $2y(y^2-3) = 0$ |
| 6 | $y = -\tfrac{x}{2} + \tfrac32$ | $m = -0.5$ | **F4** - flip $m = 2$ |
| 7 | $y'' = 6$, concave **up** | $6$ | **F7** - quotient rule, then substitute $y' = 2$ |
| 8 | $\left(\tfrac25, -\tfrac15\right)$ | $(0.4, -0.2)$ | **F8** - $F(x,2x-1) = 2(x-1)^2(5x-2)$ |
| 9 | $y = \tfrac{x}{3}$ | $m \approx 0.333333$ | **F6** - $F_x = \cos(x+y) - 2$, $F_y = \cos(x+y)+2$ |
| 10 | $y = -\tfrac{x}{3} + \tfrac23$ | $m \approx -0.333333$ | **F6** - $F_x = e^y = 1$, $F_y = xe^y + 1 = 3$ |
| 11 | $y = -\tfrac23 x + 2\sqrt2$ | $m \approx -0.666667$, $b \approx 2.828427$ | **F0** - ellipse, $m = -4x/(9y)$ |
| 12 | $y = x$ | $m = 1$ | **F0** - $F_x = -6x^2+4x^3$, $F_y = 2y$ |

Every line in this key was verified three ways: the point is on the curve, the slope matches a formula-blind $-F_x/F_y$, and substituting the line back into the relation produces a double root at the point.

If you missed **4 or 5**, reread Families 2 and 3 - those are the ones where the slope form breaks.
If you missed **7**, you probably re-derived $y'$ instead of substituting the number.
If you missed **anything in the last column of Family 0**, the error is in the collect-and-divide step, not in the differentiation.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
       "Find the equation of the tangent line to <relation> at <point>"
                                  |
                    [ CHECK the point is ON the curve ]
                    substitute BOTH coordinates into the relation
                                  |
                +-----------------+------------------+
                |                                    |
          not satisfied                          satisfied
                |                                    |
        STOP - misread, or                          |
        it is a Family 9 trap                       |
                                                    |
                    [ DIFFERENTIATE both sides in x ]
                    y' on every y   /   product rule on every xy-type term
                    do NOT simplify, do NOT solve yet
                                  |
                    [ SUBSTITUTE the point NOW ]      <- Route B
                    you now have a LINEAR equation in m
                                  |
                    [ COLLECT the m terms, divide once ]
                                  |
                +-----------------+------------------+
                |                 |                  |
       coefficient of m      coefficient 0,      coefficient 0,
        is nonzero           RHS nonzero          RHS also 0
                |                 |                  |
         m = <number>      VERTICAL tangent      SINGULAR point
                |            answer: x = x1      no unique tangent;
                |            (no m at all)       say so, do not
                |                                report m = 0
                |
        [ BUILD THE LINE ]   y - y1 = m(x - x1)  ->  y = m x + (y1 - m x1)
                |
        [ CHECK ]  put x = x1 into your line; you must get y1 back
                |
        [ PREVIEW ]  type the LINE, not the slope.   `2*x-1`
```

The three exits at the bottom of the middle branch are the whole point of the tree.
Most students only ever draw the leftmost one, and then meet a Family 3 question in the exam.
