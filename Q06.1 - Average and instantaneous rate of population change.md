# Q6.1 - Average and instantaneous rate of population change

**The question (Midterm Exam 2, item 3, 4 points).**
The population $P$ of a city in the year $y$ is $P(y)$.
Write a sentence (in layman's terms) that explains the meaning of the expressions

- a. $\dfrac{P(15) - P(2)}{13} = 1{,}431$
- b. $P'(10) = 14{,}000$.

**The answer.**

> **(a)** Between year 2 and year 15, the city's population grew by an average of **1,431 people per year**.
>
> **(b)** In year 10, the city's population was growing at an instantaneous rate of **14,000 people per year**.

> **The one sentence that decides this question.**
> A **difference** divided by a **difference** is an **average** rate over an **interval**; a **prime** attached to a single number is an **instantaneous** rate at an **instant**, and both of them are measured in output units per input unit.
> Everything else on this question is bookkeeping around those two readings.

## 0. The 30-second version

This is the only question on either midterm that asks you to compute nothing at all.
That is exactly why it is easy to lose all four points: there is no algebra to hide behind, so a vague sentence is the whole answer.

```
Look at the SHAPE of the expression.

  [ P(b) - P(a) ] / (b - a)   ->  AVERAGE rate, over the INTERVAL [a,b]
  P'(a)                       ->  INSTANTANEOUS rate, at the INSTANT y = a

Then fill in one template, in this order:

  1. WHEN      "Between year 2 and year 15"     or  "In year 10"
  2. WHAT      "the city's population"
  3. HOW       "grew by an average of"          or  "was growing at a rate of"
  4. NUMBER    "1,431"                          or  "14,000"
  5. UNITS     "people per year"                <- this is where the marks are
```

## 1. What this question is really testing

The examiner is not testing whether you can differentiate.
He is testing whether you know what a derivative *is*, in a setting where you cannot fake it with a rule.

| Reflex | Where it comes back on the same paper |
| :--- | :--- |
| Recognising a difference quotient by its shape | item 7's limits, where $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ is the definition being taken to a limit |
| Attaching units to a rate | item 8 (linearization), item 11 (related rates, mi/h) |
| Average over an interval versus value at a point | item 5's conditions on $f'$ over intervals versus $f$ at points |
| Reading a slope as a real-world sentence | item 10's tangent line, item 11's "how fast is the rocket rising" |
| Noticing that $13 = 15 - 2$ | anything where a difference quotient is disguised |

There is a second thing being tested that is easy to miss.
The phrase is "in layman's terms".
That is an instruction about vocabulary, not about rigour.
It means: no "derivative", no "difference quotient", no "limit", no "rate of change of the function $P$ with respect to $y$".
Say it the way you would say it to someone who runs the city.

## 2. The five things you must have cold

### 2.1 The two objects, side by side

This table is the whole question.
Read it down the columns, not across the rows: every row is one way the two objects differ, and there is no row on which they agree except the last.

| | **average** rate of change | **instantaneous** rate of change |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| written | $\dfrac{P(b)-P(a)}{b-a}$ | $P'(a)$, or $\dfrac{dP}{dy}\Big\vert_{y=a}$ |
| lives on | an **interval** $[a,b]$ | a single **instant** $y=a$ |
| geometry | the slope of the **secant** line through the two endpoints | the slope of the **tangent** line at the one point |
| needs | only the two endpoint values | the function near $a$, and a limit |
| plain-English verb | "grew **by an average of**" | "**was growing at**" |
| what it hides | everything that happened in between | everything outside that instant |
| exists when | $P(a)$ and $P(b)$ both exist | $P$ is differentiable at $a$ |
| units | output units per input unit | output units per input unit |

The last row is the one students throw away.
Both are rates, so both carry the same units, and both need those units stated.

### 2.2 The units rule, which is worth more marks than anything else here

> **The units of any rate of change are (units of the output) per (one unit of the input).**

That is the whole rule, and it never has an exception.

| the function | output unit | input unit | units of BOTH rates |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $P(y)$, population in year $y$ | people | years | people per year |
| $C(x)$, cost of $x$ chairs | dollars | chairs | dollars per chair |
| $V(t)$, litres in a tank at minute $t$ | litres | minutes | litres per minute |
| $T(t)$, temperature at hour $t$ | degrees C | hours | degrees C per hour |
| $s(t)$, distance at second $t$ | metres | seconds | metres per second |
| $N(t)$, bacteria at hour $t$ | cells | hours | cells per hour |
| $R(x)$, revenue from $x$ units | dollars | units | dollars per unit |

Note the fourth column of row two: **dollars per chair**, not dollars per year.
The input variable does not have to be time.
If you say "per year" out of habit when the input is chairs, you have shown the marker that you memorised a sentence instead of a rule.

The second derivative applies the rule twice: $P''$ is the rate of change of $P'$, so its units are **people per year, per year**.

### 2.3 The three spellings of the same quotient

These are one object, not three, and the exam uses whichever one hides the structure best.

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(b)-P(a)}{b-a}}_{\text{two endpoints}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{P(a+h)-P(a)}{h}}_{\text{a start and a step}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta y}}_{\text{the shorthand}}$$

Q6.1 uses the first, with the denominator already simplified to 13.
That simplification is the small trap: the number 13 does not appear anywhere in the question except as the width of the interval, so if you do not spot $13 = 15 - 2$ you may read the expression as some rate "per 13 years" and give the wrong sentence.

**Always reconstruct the interval from the denominator before writing anything.**

### 2.4 The bridge: the derivative IS the limit of the average rate

$$P'(a)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{P(a+h)-P(a)}{h}$$

Part (a) and part (b) are not two different topics.
Part (b) is part (a) with the interval squeezed to a point.
Saying that out loud in your answer is free credit, and it is the sentence that shows you understand rather than recognise.

> The course's own quick-reference card lists "DIFFERENCE QUOTIENT" and "DERIVATIVE DEFINITION" on two separate lines as if they were two separate formulas.
> They are the same expression.
> One of them has a limit sign in front.
> See section 12 for the other places the course files are imprecise here.

### 2.5 What each fact does NOT say

This is where the fourth mark usually goes, because a confident sentence that overclaims is worse than a plain one that does not.

| The given fact | It does NOT mean |
| :--- | :--- |
| average rate $= 1{,}431$ | the population rose by 1,431 in every one of those years |
| average rate $= 1{,}431$ | the population rose at all in any particular year |
| average rate $= 1{,}431$ | the population is 1,431 anything; it is a **rate**, not a count |
| average rate $= 1{,}431$ | anything about what happened before year 2 or after year 15 |
| $P'(10) = 14{,}000$ | the population went up by exactly 14,000 during year 10 |
| $P'(10) = 14{,}000$ | the rate stayed at 14,000 for any length of time |
| both together | the two numbers contradict each other |

The last row matters, and section 8 Family 10 proves it with two explicit cities.

## 3. The universal procedure

> **Step 1 - Read the shape, not the numbers.**
> Is there a subtraction over a subtraction? That is an average rate.
> Is there a prime, a $\frac{d}{dy}$, or the word "instantaneous"? That is a derivative.
>
> **Step 2 - Recover the interval or the instant.**
> For an average rate, name $a$ and $b$ explicitly, and check that the denominator really is $b-a$.
> For a derivative, name the single input value.
>
> **Step 3 - Name the two quantities in words.**
> Not "$P$" and "$y$" but "the city's population" and "the year".
> This is the "layman's terms" instruction being obeyed.
>
> **Step 4 - Build the units.**
> Output unit, the word "per", one input unit. Write them down before the sentence.
>
> **Step 5 - Fill the template, and read the sign.**
> Positive means growing, rising, increasing.
> Negative means shrinking, falling, cooling, draining.
>
> **Step 6 - Say what it is not, if the question invites it.**
> One clause of the form "this is an average over the whole stretch, so it says nothing about any single year" is often the difference between three marks and four.

**The classify table.** One glance at the expression picks your sentence.

| The expression looks like | It is | The sentence starts |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$ | average rate on $[a,b]$ | "Between ... and ..., on average, ..." |
| $\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ | average rate on $[a,a+h]$ | same, with the interval reconstructed |
| $f'(a)$ | instantaneous rate at $a$ | "At ..., ... was changing at ..." |
| $\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h)-f(a)}{h}$ | that is $f'(a)$, written out | same as $f'(a)$ |
| $f(b)-f(a)$ | a **net change**, not a rate | "Between ... and ..., it changed by ..." with NO "per" |
| $f''(a)$ | rate of change of the rate | "At ..., the rate itself was changing at ..." |
| $\frac{f'(a)}{f(a)}$ | relative rate, a percentage | "At ..., it was growing by ...% per ..." |

Row five is the sharpest distinction on the table.
$P(15)-P(2)=18{,}603$ is a number of people.
$\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=1{,}431$ is a number of people **per year**.
Dividing by the width is what turns a count into a rate, and that is the only difference between the two.

## 4. Q6.1 worked five ways

All five routes produce a correct answer.
Route A is the one to write under pressure; the others exist because the examiner can ask for any of them by rewording.

### Route A - the template (use this one)

**Part (a).**
The shape is a difference over a difference, so it is an average rate.
The interval: the numerator uses years 2 and 15, and $15-2=13$, which is exactly the denominator, so the interval is $[2,15]$ and the quotient is a genuine average rate rather than a rate "per 13 years".
The quantities: population, and years.
The units: people per year.
The sign: positive, so it grew.

> **Between year 2 and year 15, the city's population grew by an average of 1,431 people per year.**

**Part (b).**
There is a prime on a single input, so it is an instantaneous rate at $y=10$.
Same units, same sign.

> **In year 10, the city's population was growing at an instantaneous rate of 14,000 people per year.**

That is the answer, and it took four lines of thought.

### Route B - the geometric reading

Part (a) is the slope of the **secant** line joining the two points $(2,P(2))$ and $(15,P(15))$ on the graph.
Part (b) is the slope of the **tangent** line at the single point $(10,P(10))$.

Here is one city that satisfies both facts exactly, with both lines drawn on it.
The dotted line is the secant, slope 1,431.
The steep line is the tangent at year 10, slope 14,000.

```
       |                                                                  
       |                                           /                      
70,000 |                                          //                   ...
       |                                          /******************O****
       |                                         /**          .....       
65,000 |                                         **      .....            
       |                                        /*  ......                
       |                                        *....                     
       |                                    ...**                         
60,000 |                               .....   O                          
       |                          ......       *                          
       |                      .....           *                           
55,000 |                 ......              **                           
       |             .....                   *                            
       |        ......                     **/                            
50,000 |****O*******************************/                             
       |   .                                /                             
       |                                   /                              
       |                                   /                              
       +------------------------------------------------------------------
            2        4        6       8        10       12           15
```

`*` is the population curve, `.` is the secant, `/` is the tangent, `O` marks years 2, 10 and 15.

Look at how much steeper the tangent is.
That picture is the fastest way to see that the two given numbers are not in conflict: a curve can be nearly flat for years and then very steep for one of them, and the secant only sees the two endpoints.

The geometric reading is worth having because a reworded version of this question ("which is larger, the slope of the secant or the slope of the tangent at year 10?") is answered instantly from the picture and slowly from the algebra.

### Route C - the units-first reading

Do the units before the words and the sentence writes itself.

$$\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}:\quad \frac{\text{people}}{\text{years}} = \text{people per year}$$

Now you know the sentence must end in "people per year", so it cannot end in "people", and it cannot be a total.
That single constraint kills the two most common wrong answers before you have written a word.

### Route D - the prediction reading of part (b)

The most useful layman's gloss on a derivative is a forecast.

> $P'(10) = 14{,}000$ means that **if the growth held steady at the year-10 pace, the city would add about 14,000 people over the next year.**

That is a legitimate and often-preferred second sentence for part (b), because it explains the number to somebody with no calculus at all.
Notice the two hedges: "if it held steady" and "about".
Both are required, because a derivative is a rate at an instant and the rate does not hold steady.
Drop the hedges and the sentence becomes false: in the city drawn above, $P(10)=59{,}302$ and the linear forecast gives $P(11)\approx 73{,}302$, while the true $P(11)=67{,}729$.
The forecast overshoots by 5,573 people.
Shorten the step to a quarter of a year and the same forecast overshoots by only about 156, which is the whole idea behind linearization on item 8 of the same paper.

### Route E - the "what must be true" reading

Both facts can be turned into a consequence rather than a translation, which is what a follow-up part usually asks for.

From (a): the population's **net** change over those 13 years was $13\times1{,}431=18{,}603$ people.
From (a) plus the Mean Value Theorem: if $P$ is continuous on $[2,15]$ and differentiable on $(2,15)$, then **there is some year $c$ between 2 and 15 at which the population was growing at exactly 1,431 people per year.**
From (b): over a short window around year 10, the population added roughly 14,000 people per year of window.

### Route W - the natural wrong answers

These are the answers that get written when the shape is not read first.

| Wrong answer | Why it is wrong |
| :--- | :--- |
| "The population was 1,431 in year 13." | Reads the quotient as a value and the denominator as a year. |
| "The population grew by 1,431 people." | Drops "per year" and "average", turning a rate into a count. |
| "The population grows by 1,431 people every year." | Turns an average into a guarantee about every year. |
| "The population grew by 1,431 people per year over 13 years." | Correct in substance but never names the interval $[2,15]$. |
| "The derivative of $P$ at 10 is 14,000." | Restates the symbols, which is exactly what "in layman's terms" forbids. |
| "In year 10 the population increased by 14,000." | Turns an instantaneous rate into a whole year's change. |
| "The population is growing at 14,000 people per year." | Drops "in year 10", so it claims a rate that holds always. |

### The route comparison

| | A (template) | B (geometry) | C (units first) | D (prediction) | E (consequence) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Time to write | 30 seconds | 1 minute, needs a sketch | 30 seconds | 45 seconds | 1 minute |
| Answers the question as asked | yes | partly | yes | yes for (b) | no, it goes further |
| Risk | forgetting the units | drawing a graph you were not asked for | none | forgetting the hedges | asserting the MVT without its hypotheses |
| Best for | the exam | a "which is steeper" reword | checking a sentence | a "explain to the mayor" reword | a follow-up part |
| Verdict | **use this one under pressure** | keep in your head | use as a pre-check | add as a second sentence if there is room | only if asked |

**Under exam pressure, use Route A, and pre-check it with Route C.**
Say the units to yourself first, write the template, then re-read your sentence and confirm the words "average" and "between" appear in (a) and the words "in year 10" appear in (b).

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Four points, two parts, so budget two marks each.
On a hand-marked paper, the marks fall roughly as: naming the object correctly (average versus instantaneous), and giving the number with correct units in a sentence about the city.

**The short form, which is what to write if time is tight.**

> (a) Between year 2 and year 15, the city's population grew by an average of 1,431 people per year.
>
> (b) In year 10, the city's population was growing at a rate of 14,000 people per year.

**The full-credit form, which adds the one clause that shows understanding.**

> (a) The expression $\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}$ is the average rate of change of the population over the 13 years from year 2 to year 15, since $13 = 15 - 2$.
> Its value of 1,431 means that over that stretch the city gained an average of 1,431 people per year, a total of $13 \times 1{,}431 = 18{,}603$ people.
> It is an average over the whole period, so it does not say that the population rose by 1,431 in any particular year.
>
> (b) $P'(10)$ is the instantaneous rate of change of the population at year 10, that is, how fast the population was changing at that moment rather than over a stretch of time.
> Its value of 14,000 means the population was growing at 14,000 people per year at that instant; if that pace had held, the city would have added about 14,000 people over the following year.

Three things a marker looks for that students routinely skip:

- the word **average** in (a) and the word **instantaneous** (or "at that moment") in (b), because those two words are the entire distinction being examined;
- the **units**, written out as "people per year" rather than left implied;
- naming the **interval** in (a), which is the only thing that shows you noticed $13 = 15 - 2$.

## 6. Verify before you commit

This takes eight seconds and it catches every common failure on this question.

**The units check.**
Read your own sentence back and find the phrase "per year".
If it is not there, the sentence describes a count, not a rate, and it is wrong.
If the input variable had been chairs or litres, "per year" being there would also be wrong.

**The interval check.**
Find the two endpoints in your sentence.
"Between year 2 and year 15" must appear in (a).
If your sentence names only one year, you have written a derivative sentence for a difference quotient.

**The instant check.**
"In year 10" must appear in (b), and nothing about a stretch of time may appear.
The words "over", "during the year" and "between" are all wrong in (b).

**The three cheap invariants.**

1. **Sign matches verb.** A positive rate pairs with grew, rose, increased. A negative rate pairs with fell, shrank, drained, cooled. Get this backwards and the sentence is simply false.
2. **Magnitude is plausible.** 1,431 people a year in a city is unremarkable. 14,000 a year is a boom. Neither is absurd, so neither number is a transcription error.
3. **Net change is recoverable.** $13\times1{,}431=18{,}603$. If you cannot multiply back to a sensible total, you have misread the interval.

**The consistency check, which is the one people get wrong.**
$14{,}000$ is 9.78 times $1{,}431$, which feels like a contradiction and is not.
An average over 13 years and a rate at one instant are different quantities, and nothing forces them to be close.
See section 8 Family 10 for two cities that satisfy both, one of which never shrinks and one of which shrinks for six years.

**The numbers worth memorising for this question.**

$$15-2 = 13,\qquad 13\times1{,}431 = 18{,}603,\qquad \frac{14{,}000}{1{,}431}\approx 9.78$$

## 8. The variation catalog

This is the part that makes the question repeatable.
Read each table as a **structure**, not as a list of facts: nearly every variation below is the same two readings with one element swapped, and the tables are arranged so you can see which element moved.

### Family 0 - the two sentences, phrase by phrase

Every acceptable answer is built from the same five slots.
There are only two settings for each slot, so there are effectively two sentences in this whole family.

| slot | for a difference quotient | for a derivative |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| WHEN | "Between year $a$ and year $b$" | "In year $a$" / "At year $a$" |
| WHAT | "the city's population" | "the city's population" |
| HOW | "grew by an average of" | "was growing at a rate of" |
| NUMBER | the quotient's value | the derivative's value |
| UNITS | "people per year" | "people per year" |

Swap "grew" for "fell" when the number is negative, and that is the whole family.

### Family 1 - the same question in a different costume

The trigger is that the story changed and the mathematics did not.
The tool is the units rule from section 2.2.

| Given | Reading | Units |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{C(80)-C(50)}{30}=42$, $C(x)$ dollars for $x$ chairs | between 50 and 80 chairs, each extra chair added about $\$42$ on average | dollars per chair |
| $C'(80)=35$ | at a production level of 80 chairs, the cost is rising by about $\$35$ per extra chair | dollars per chair |
| $\frac{V(20)-V(5)}{15}=-12$, $V(t)$ litres at minute $t$ | between minute 5 and minute 20 the tank lost an average of 12 litres per minute | litres per minute |
| $T'(6)=-1.5$, $T$ in degrees C at hour $t$ | at 6 a.m. the temperature was falling at $1.5^\circ$C per hour | degrees C per hour |
| $s'(4)=23$, $s$ metres at second $t$ | four seconds in, the object was moving at 23 metres per second | metres per second |
| $N'(3)=850$, $N$ bacteria at hour $t$ | three hours in, the culture was growing at 850 cells per hour | cells per hour |
| $R'(200)=18$, $R$ dollars from $x$ units | at 200 units, each extra unit brings in about $\$18$ | dollars per unit |

Two of those seven have a **non-time** input.
$C'(80)$ and $R'(200)$ are measured per chair and per unit, and their standard names are **marginal cost** and **marginal revenue**.
If you write "per year" on either of them you have applied a memorised sentence rather than the rule.

### Family 2 - the quotient written some other way

The trigger is that the denominator no longer looks like $b-a$.
The tool is to reconstruct the interval before reading anything.

| Given | The interval it describes | Sentence |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}$ | $[2,15]$, width 13 | Q6.1 itself |
| $\frac{P(2+h)-P(2)}{h}$ with $h=13$ | $[2,15]$ again | the identical statement |
| $\frac{P(10+h)-P(10)}{h}$ as $h\to0$ | shrinks to the instant $y=10$ | this *is* $P'(10)$ |
| $\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta y}=1431$ | whichever interval $\Delta y$ names | needs the interval stated |
| $\frac{P(9)-P(4)}{5}$ | $[4,9]$, width **5**, not 9 | the width is $9-4$ |
| $P(15)-P(2)=18603$ | not a rate at all | "the population grew by 18,603 people", no "per" |

The fifth row is the reword that catches people: "between year 4 and year 9" has width 5, and writing 9 in the denominator is the single most common arithmetic slip in this family.

### Family 3 - negative rates

The trigger is a minus sign; the tool is the verb.

| Given | Sentence |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=-1431$ | between year 2 and year 15 the city lost an average of 1,431 people per year |
| $P'(10)=-14000$ | in year 10 the population was falling at 14,000 people per year |
| $P'(10)=0$ | in year 10 the population was momentarily neither growing nor shrinking |

The third row is worth practising.
$P'(10)=0$ does **not** mean the population was zero, and it does not mean the population was constant.
It means the graph had a horizontal tangent at that instant, which is what a peak, a trough or a flat spot looks like.

### Family 4 - the second derivative

The trigger is a double prime; the tool is the units rule applied twice.

| Given | Sentence | Units |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $P''(10)=900$ | in year 10, the growth rate itself was rising by 900 people per year, each year | people per year per year |
| $P''(10)=-900$ | in year 10 the population was still growing, but the growth was slowing | people per year per year |
| $P'(10)>0$ and $P''(10)<0$ | growing, but decelerating: the graph is rising and concave down | |
| $P'(10)<0$ and $P''(10)<0$ | shrinking, and the decline is accelerating | |

The two-sign combination table is the same one used on item 5 of this paper for sketching, so this family is directly connected to Q5.2 and Q5.3.

### Family 5 - estimate the rate from a table

The trigger is that you are given data instead of a formula, and asked for $P'(10)$.
The tool is a difference quotient used as an **approximation**, which is a genuinely different job from reading one as a sentence.

Take this table, where the underlying function is $Q(y)=40{,}000+900y+60y^2$, so the true $Q'(10)=2{,}100$.

| year $y$ | 8 | 9 | 10 | 11 | 12 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $Q(y)$ | 51,040 | 52,960 | 55,000 | 57,160 | 59,440 |

| estimate | formula | value | error |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | ---: |
| forward | $\frac{Q(11)-Q(10)}{1}$ | 2,160 | $+60$ |
| backward | $\frac{Q(10)-Q(9)}{1}$ | 2,040 | $-60$ |
| **centred** | $\frac{Q(11)-Q(9)}{2}$ | **2,100** | **0** |
| average over $[8,12]$ | $\frac{Q(12)-Q(8)}{4}$ | 2,100 | 0 |

Read the structure, not the four numbers.
The forward and backward errors are $+60$ and $-60$: **equal and opposite**, which is exactly why averaging them (the centred estimate) is exact here.
And the fourth row is exact for the same reason: on a quadratic, the average rate over an interval equals the derivative at that interval's **midpoint**, and 10 is the midpoint of $[8,12]$.

**Both of those exactnesses are quadratic-only accidents.**
On the city drawn in Route B, the average rate over $[8,12]$ is 4,628, and $P'(10)$ is 14,000.
Do not carry the midpoint identity into a general question.

There are three more things in this family that a well-set exam will probe, and all three are counter-intuitive.

**First: annual data can miss the answer badly.**
The city in Route B, tabulated year by year, reads 50,000 / 50,045 / 50,874 / 59,302 / 67,729 / 68,558 at years 2, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 12.
Its centred estimate of $P'(10)$ is $\frac{67{,}729-50{,}874}{2}=8{,}427.5$, which understates the true 14,000 by **39.8 percent**, because the whole boom is narrower than the one-year sampling step.
A difference quotient can only see what the sampling interval resolves.

**Second: which estimate is better is a question about ORDER, not about one number.**
Halve the step and look at what happens to the error.

| method | on a cubic, errors as $h$ halves | ratio | order |
| :--- | :--- | ---: | :--- |
| forward $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ | 31, 15.25, 7.56, 3.77 | $\approx 2$ | first order |
| centred $\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}$ | 1, 0.25, 0.0625, 0.0156 | $\approx 4$ | second order |

Errors halving means first order.
Errors quartering means second order.
**A single accurate estimate proves nothing**, and here is the proof: on the Route B city, the forward estimate at year 9 has ratios 2.08, 2.04, 2.02 (first order), while at year 10 the *same method on the same function* has ratios 3.97, 3.99, 4.00 (second order).
The reason is that year 10 is that model's inflection point, where $P''(10)=0$ kills the first-order error term; the model is odd-symmetric about that point, so the forward quotient is literally equal to the centred quotient there.
If you had tested only at year 10 you would have concluded that forward differences are second order, and you would be wrong.

**Third: smaller $h$ is not always better.**
For the same centred estimate at year 10, the error is $2.5\times10^{-3}$ at $h=10^{-6}$ but $2.6$ at $h=10^{-12}$.
Past a point, subtracting two nearly equal large numbers and dividing by a tiny one loses more to rounding than it gains in truncation.
Test convergence on a **bounded** ladder, and stop before the arithmetic falls apart.

### Family 6 - read the rates off a graph

The trigger is a picture with no formula.
The tool is Route B: average rate is a secant slope, instantaneous rate is a tangent slope.

| Asked | How to answer from the picture |
| :--- | :--- |
| which is bigger, the average rate on $[a,b]$ or $f'(c)$? | draw both lines and compare steepness |
| where on $[a,b]$ is $f'$ largest? | where the curve is steepest |
| is $f'(c)$ positive or negative? | is the curve going up or down at $c$ |
| is there a $c$ with $f'(c)$ equal to the average rate? | yes if $f$ is smooth: slide the secant until it just touches |
| where is the average rate over $[a,b]$ attained? | where the tangent is parallel to the secant |

The last two rows are the Mean Value Theorem drawn instead of stated, and that picture is the reason the theorem is believable.

### Family 7 - a formula is given, compute both

The trigger is that $P$ is handed to you.
The tool is arithmetic plus one derivative rule; the interpretation is unchanged.

Take $P(y)=40y^{2}+500y+30{,}000$.

$$P(2)=31{,}160,\qquad P(15)=46{,}500$$

$$\text{average rate on }[2,15]=\frac{46{,}500-31{,}160}{13}=\frac{15{,}340}{13}=1{,}180$$

$$P'(y)=80y+500 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad P'(10)=1{,}300$$

There is a shortcut worth knowing for a quadratic $P(y)=Ay^{2}+By+C$: the average rate over $[a,b]$ is exactly $A(a+b)+B$, because the $y^2$ difference factors as $(b-a)(b+a)$.
Here $40(2+15)+500=1{,}180$, with no evaluation of $P$ at all.

Notice that the instantaneous rate at year 10 exceeds the average rate over $[2,15]$.
That always happens past the interval's midpoint when the rate is increasing, and $10 > \frac{2+15}{2}=8.5$.

### Family 8 - use the derivative as a forecast

The trigger is "estimate", "approximately", or a nearby input value.
The tool is $P(a+\Delta)\approx P(a)+P'(a)\,\Delta$, which is item 8 of this same paper wearing different clothes.

| Asked | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| estimate $P(11)$ from $P(10)$ | $P(10)+14{,}000$ |
| estimate $P(10.5)$ | $P(10)+7{,}000$ |
| estimate $P(10.25)$ | $P(10)+3{,}500$ |
| estimate $P(9)$ | $P(10)-14{,}000$ |
| how long to add 3,500 people, at the year-10 pace | about a quarter of a year |

Every one of these is the same multiplication, and every one of them is an **estimate**.
On the Route B city the one-year forecast overshoots by 5,573 people and the quarter-year forecast overshoots by about 156, which is a 36-fold smaller error for a 4-fold shorter step.
That ratio is not a coincidence: the error of a linear forecast grows like the square of the step.

### Family 9 - the Mean Value Theorem reading

The trigger is a question of the form "must there be a year when ...".
The tool is the MVT, and its hypotheses have to be stated.

> If $P$ is continuous on $[2,15]$ and differentiable on $(2,15)$, then there is at least one year $c$ strictly between 2 and 15 with $P'(c)=1{,}431$.

In plain words: **at some moment during those thirteen years, the city really was growing at exactly the average pace.**
Both cities in Family 10 do this, one near year 8.79 and one near year 8.43.

Two warnings.
The theorem gives you existence, not a location, so "at year 8.5" is not an answer.
And it needs differentiability: a city whose population jumped overnight through an annexation has no such year at all.

### Family 10 - what the two facts together do NOT force

This is the deepest variation, and the one most likely to be asked as "is this possible?".

Here are two cities.
Both satisfy $\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=1{,}431$ exactly and $P'(10)=14{,}000$ exactly, and both were built and checked numerically.

| year | **City B, the boom** | **City D, the dip** |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2 | 50,000 | 58,400 |
| 8 | 50,045 | 54,081 |
| 10 | 59,302 | 66,502 |
| 12 | 68,558 | 78,923 |
| 15 | 68,603 | 77,003 |

City B **never shrinks**: it sits at about 50,000 for six years, adds nearly 19,000 people in a boom around year 10, then flattens out.
City D **shrinks for six years**, bottoming out at about 54,074 people near year 7.9, booms past year 10, and is then falling again by year 15.

So, from the two given facts alone:

| Claim | Verdict |
| :--- | :--- |
| the population grew in every year between 2 and 15 | **not forced** - City D refutes it |
| the population shrank at some point | **not forced** - City B refutes it |
| the population at year 15 exceeded that at year 2 | **forced**, since the average rate is positive |
| the population was still growing at year 15 | **not forced** - City D was falling |
| some year had a growth rate of exactly 1,431 | **forced**, by the MVT |
| the two facts contradict each other | **false** |

That last row is the point of the whole family.
A ratio of 9.78 between an instantaneous rate and an average rate feels wrong and is completely ordinary.

### Family 11 - relative, or percentage, rate of change

The trigger is the word "percent" or "relative".
The tool is to divide the rate by the level.

$$\text{relative rate at } y=a \;=\; \frac{P'(a)}{P(a)}$$

For City B, $\frac{14{,}000}{59{,}302}=0.2361$, so the population was growing at about **23.6 percent per year** at year 10.
The average rate as a percentage of the starting population is $\frac{1{,}431}{50{,}000}=2.862$ percent per year.

The units are the reason this matters: a relative rate is a **pure number per year**, because the people cancel.
That is why demographers quote percentages instead of headcounts, and it is why "14,000 people per year" means something very different for a city of 50,000 than for a city of 5,000,000.

### Family 12 - when the derivative does not exist

The trigger is a kink, a jump or a piecewise definition at the very point being asked about.
The tool is the one-sided difference quotients.

| Situation | Average rate on an interval | $P'$ at the bad point |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| corner, e.g. $P(y)=\lvert y-10\rvert$ | fine: $\frac{P(15)-P(8)}{7}=\frac37$ | does **not** exist: left slope $-1$, right slope $+1$ |
| jump at year 10, e.g. an annexation of 9,000 people | fine: $\frac{9{,}000}{13}\approx 692.3$ | does **not** exist; $P$ is not even continuous |
| vertical tangent | fine | does not exist; the slope is infinite |

Part (a) can be perfectly meaningful at a place where part (b) is meaningless, because an average rate needs only two values while a derivative needs a limit.

**And here is the trap that a numerical check will not catch.**
For the corner $P(y)=\lvert y-10\rvert$, the centred difference quotient at year 10 is

$$\frac{P(10+h)-P(10-h)}{2h}=\frac{h-h}{2h}=0$$

for **every** $h$.
It reports a derivative of zero at a point where no derivative exists, and it does so no matter how small you make $h$, so no amount of refining will reveal the problem.
The one-sided quotients, $+1$ from the right and $-1$ from the left, are the only ones that see the corner.
When you are asked whether a derivative exists at a point, check the two sides separately; never rely on a symmetric estimate.

### Family 13 - the question run backwards

The trigger is that you are given the sentence and asked for the expression.
The tool is the same table, read right to left.

| Sentence | Expression |
| :--- | :--- |
| between year 4 and year 9 the population grew on average by 2,000 people per year | $\frac{P(9)-P(4)}{5}=2000$ |
| in year 7 the population was falling at 300 people per year | $P'(7)=-300$ |
| the population rose by 18,603 people between years 2 and 15 | $P(15)-P(2)=18603$ |
| at 50 chairs the cost is rising by $\$40$ per chair | $C'(50)=40$ |
| the growth rate was itself increasing in year 6 | $P''(6)>0$ |

Row one is the one to get right: the denominator is $9-4=5$, not 9.
Row three has no denominator at all, because "rose by" with no "per" is a net change.

### Family 14 - the average of the rate

The trigger is wording like "on average, how fast was the population growing".
The tool is recognising that this is the same question as (a), not a new one.

The average **value** of $P'$ over $[2,15]$ equals the average **rate** of $P$ over $[2,15]$:

$$\frac{1}{15-2}\int_{2}^{15}P'(y)\,dy=\frac{P(15)-P(2)}{13}=1{,}431$$

Both cities in Family 10 were checked against this numerically and both give exactly 1,431.
This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus reading of part (a), and it is worth knowing because it explains *why* an average rate deserves the word "average" at all: it is the mean of the instantaneous rates.

## 9. The trap list

Ranked by how often each one actually costs marks.

1. **Dropping the units.** "Grew by an average of 1,431" is not an answer; "1,431 people per year" is. This costs more marks on this question than everything else combined.
2. **Turning a rate into a count.** "The population grew by 1,431 people" describes $P(15)-P(2)$ divided by nothing. The division by 13 is what makes it a rate.
3. **Losing the word "average" in (a) or "instantaneous" in (b).** Those two words are what the question is examining. A sentence without them cannot show that you know the difference.
4. **Not naming the interval.** "Over 13 years" is weaker than "between year 2 and year 15", and only the second shows you spotted $13 = 15 - 2$.
5. **Treating an average as a guarantee.** "The population grew by 1,431 every year" is a stronger claim than the data supports, and City D in Family 10 refutes it outright.
6. **Reading $P'(10)=14{,}000$ as "the population rose by 14,000 during year 10".** An instantaneous rate is not a year's worth of change. Add "if the pace held" if you want to phrase it as a forecast.
7. **Restating the symbols.** "The derivative of $P$ at $y=10$ equals 14,000" is a translation into the same language. "In layman's terms" is an explicit instruction against it.
8. **Assuming "per year" when the input is not time.** $C'(80)$ is dollars per **chair**.
9. **Getting the interval width wrong on a reword.** "Between year 4 and year 9" has width 5.
10. **Declaring the two numbers inconsistent.** They are not, and section 8 Family 10 exhibits two cities that satisfy both.
11. **Trusting a centred difference at a corner.** It returns 0 for every $h$ and hides the non-differentiability completely.
12. **Concluding a method is accurate from one good estimate.** Order is a statement about how the error behaves as $h$ shrinks, not about a single value.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
An expression about a changing quantity. What does it MEAN?
|
+-- Does it have a DIVISION by a difference of two inputs?
|     YES -> AVERAGE RATE over an INTERVAL.
|            1. Recover a and b. Confirm the denominator is b - a.
|               (13 in Q6.1 is 15 - 2, not "per 13 years".)
|            2. "Between <a> and <b>, <thing> changed by an average of
|                <value> <output unit> per <input unit>."
|            3. Geometry: it is the SECANT slope through the two endpoints.
|
+-- Does it have a PRIME, a d/dy, or the word "instantaneous"?
|     ONE prime  -> INSTANTANEOUS RATE at an INSTANT.
|            1. Name the single input value.
|            2. "In/at <a>, <thing> was changing at <value>
|                <output unit> per <input unit>."
|            3. Geometry: it is the TANGENT slope at that one point.
|            4. Optional gloss: "if that pace held, about <value> over
|               the next <one input unit>."  Keep BOTH hedges.
|     TWO primes -> the rate OF THE RATE. Units get "per <input unit>" twice.
|
+-- Is there a difference with NO division?
|     YES -> a NET CHANGE, not a rate. No "per" in the sentence.
|            f(b) - f(a) = (average rate) x (b - a).
|
+-- Is the rate divided by the LEVEL, f'(a)/f(a)?
|     YES -> a RELATIVE rate: a percentage per input unit. The units cancel.
|
+-- Being asked to ESTIMATE a derivative from a table?
|     -> centred (f(a+h) - f(a-h)) / 2h  beats one-sided, normally.
|     -> BUT at a corner the centred quotient is 0 for every h and lies.
|        Check the two sides SEPARATELY when existence is the question.
|     -> Judge a method by the ORDER: halve h, and see whether the error
|        halves (first order) or quarters (second order). One good
|        estimate proves nothing.
|     -> Do NOT shrink h without limit. Past about 1e-6 the rounding
|        error grows faster than the truncation error shrinks.
|
+-- Asked "must there be a moment when ..."?
      -> MEAN VALUE THEOREM. State continuity on [a,b] and
         differentiability on (a,b), then conclude existence only.
         It never tells you WHERE.

Before you write the final sentence, check three things:
  UNITS    output unit "per" one input unit, written out in full.
  SIGN     positive -> grew/rose;  negative -> fell/shrank.
  SCOPE    (a) names an interval;  (b) names one instant. Never swap them.
```
