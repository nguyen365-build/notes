# Q11.2 - Rocket tracked by a radar station

**The question (Midterm Exam 2, item 11, 6 points).**
"A rocket, rising vertically, is tracked by a radar station that is on the ground $5 \text{ mi}$ from the launch pad. How fast is the rocket rising when it is $4 \text{ mi}$ high and its distance from the radar station is increasing at a rate of $2000 \text{ mi/h}$?"

**The answer.** $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.5621$ miles per hour.

**What to type into Möbius:** `500*sqrt(41)`

Provenance: parsed from `notes\MATH265\MATH265.md`, not typed.
MT2's eleven printed values are 4, 4, 4, 6, 10, 10, 24, 6, 16, 4, 6, summing to 94, and item 11 is worth 6 of them.
MT2 carries exactly two "no credit will be given" clauses, on items 4 and 7, and neither is this one.
Verification: 443 assertions pass, 0 fail, 54 of them seeded controls proving the gates can bite.

This question **closes category 11**.
It is the second of the two related-rates questions in the bank, and it exists to change one thing about Q11.1 while holding everything else fixed.
Read [Q11.1](Q11.1%20-%20Gravel%20cone%20growing%20in%20height.md) first if you have not.
Sections 0 to 3 of that document - the four-line setup, the "always versus when" substitution licence, and where the chain rule enters - are assumed here rather than repeated.

## 0. The 30-second version

```
NAME + WRITE THE RATES     x = radar-to-pad distance = 5 mi        CONSTANT, always
                           y = rocket height (mi)                  varies
                           z = radar-to-rocket distance (mi)       varies
                           GIVEN  dz/dt = +2000 mi/h   (a RATE, all the time)
                           WANT   dy/dt = ?  at the INSTANT y = 4 mi

RELATE                     z^2 = x^2 + y^2 = 25 + y^2      <- Pythagoras, not a volume

DIFFERENTIATE wrt t        2z dz/dt = 2y dy/dt   ==>   z dz/dt = y dy/dt
                           (x is constant, so its term is 0 and simply vanishes)

FIND z AT THE INSTANT      z = sqrt(25 + 16) = sqrt(41)    <- computed NOW, not eliminated

SUBSTITUTE, THEN SOLVE     sqrt(41)(2000) = 4 dy/dt
                           dy/dt = 500 sqrt(41) ~ 3201.56 mi/h
```

The one new idea is on the fourth line.
In Q11.1 you got rid of the unwanted variable **symbolically, before differentiating**, using a ratio that held at all times.
Here you keep it, differentiate, and then **compute its value at the instant**.
Knowing which of those two moves a question wants is the whole of section 3.

## 1. What this question is really testing

Q11.1 and Q11.2 are a matched pair.
The examiner kept the setup discipline identical and changed the geometry, so that a student who learned Q11.1 as a recipe fails and a student who learned it as a procedure passes.

| Held fixed from Q11.1 | Changed in Q11.2 |
| :--- | :--- |
| Name everything, write GIVEN and WANT before any calculus | - |
| A rate is given, a value is given, they must not be confused | - |
| Differentiate with respect to $t$; every variable gains its own rate | - |
| Substitute the instantaneous value only after differentiating | - |
| Report with units, a sign, and a sentence | - |
| The relating equation is a **volume formula** | it is now **Pythagoras** |
| The unwanted variable is removed by a **fixed ratio, symbolically** | it is now **computed at the instant, numerically** |
| The constant in the problem is a **rate** ($dV/dt$) | there is now also a constant **length** ($x = 5$) |
| The answer is smaller than you expect | the answer is **larger** than the given rate |

| Reflex being tested | What it looks like here |
| :--- | :--- |
| Read the geometry out of the English | "on the ground 5 mi from the launch pad" plus "rising vertically" fixes a right angle at the pad |
| Recognise which side is which | 5 and 4 are both **legs**; the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{41}$, not 3 and not 5 |
| Know that a constant length differentiates to nothing | $\frac{d}{dt}(25) = 0$, which is why $x$ disappears |
| Compute a missing side at the instant | $z = \sqrt{41}$ is found from the relating equation, after differentiating |
| Keep a rate and a value apart | $2000$ is a rate; $4$ is a value |
| Sanity-check against physics | the rocket must be rising **faster** than the radar distance grows |

**Where it sits on the paper.**
Q11.2 is the **last item on MT2**, and it sits directly after Q10.2, the implicit-differentiation tangent-line question.
Those two are the closing pair of that paper, and they test the same underlying move: differentiating a relation in which the variable you want appears only as a chain factor.
In Q10.2 that factor is written $y'$; here it is written $dy/dt$.

## 2. The three things you must have cold

Q11.1's section 2 listed the solid-geometry formulas, which this question does not need at all.
What it needs instead is smaller and is listed here in full.

### 2.1 Pythagoras, and which side is the hypotenuse

$$z^2 = x^2 + y^2 \qquad\text{where } z \text{ is the side OPPOSITE the right angle}$$

The right angle is at the launch pad, because the rocket rises vertically from ground level and the radar sits on the ground.
So the two legs are the ground distance and the height, and the hypotenuse is the radar-to-rocket line.

This was checked from three-dimensional coordinates rather than assumed: with the pad at the origin, the radar at $(5,0,0)$ and the rocket at $(0,0,y)$, the straight-line distance is $\sqrt{25+y^2}$ at every height tested.

### 2.2 The English-to-symbol translations

| English | Symbol | Is it constant in time? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| "on the ground 5 mi from the launch pad" | $x = 5$ | **yes** - the station does not move |
| "rising vertically" | the angle at the pad is $90^\circ$ | yes, and this is what licenses Pythagoras |
| "its distance from the radar station is increasing at a rate of 2000 mi/h" | $\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2000$ | yes, it is a rate |
| "when it is 4 mi high" | $y = 4$ | **no** - this is one instant only |
| "how fast is the rocket rising" | $\dfrac{dy}{dt}$ | this is the unknown |

Read the third row twice.
The 2000 belongs to the **radar distance**, not to the rocket.
A student who attaches it to the height has answered a question that was not asked, and 2000 is one of the wrong answers this question is built to catch.

### 2.3 Differentiating a constant, and differentiating a square

$$\frac{d}{dt}\Big[x^2\Big] = 2x\,\frac{dx}{dt} = 2(5)(0) = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\Big[y(t)^2\Big] = 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

Both are the chain rule.
The first one produces zero not because you are allowed to ignore the 5, but because the 5 has a rate and that rate is zero.
Writing it out once is worth the four seconds, because it is the same reasoning that tells you $y = 4$ may **not** be substituted early - there, the rate is $dy/dt$, which is precisely what you are solving for and is certainly not zero.

## 3. The universal procedure, and the one decision it turns on

Q11.1's seven steps are unchanged.
What Q11.2 adds is a fork inside step 4.

> **Step 1. Draw it and name everything.**
> **Step 2. Write GIVEN and WANT as rates, before any calculus.**
> **Step 3. Find the relating equation.**
>
> **Step 4. Decide what to do with each unwanted quantity.** There are now three cases, not two.
>
> **Step 5. Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$.**
> **Step 6. NOW substitute the instantaneous values, and solve.**
> **Step 7. Report with units, with a sign, and in a sentence.**

### The three-way substitution table

This is the sharpened form of Q11.1's "always versus when", and it is Q11.2's main contribution.

| The quantity | Example | When may you substitute it? | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A constant **value** | $x = 5$ here | **before** differentiating | its rate is zero, so nothing is lost |
| A constant **relation** | $r = h/2$ in Q11.1 | **before** differentiating | it holds at every instant, so it survives differentiation |
| An **instantaneous value** | $y = 4$ here, $h = 4$ in Q11.1 | **after** differentiating, never before | its rate is nonzero and is what you are solving for |
| A **derived** instantaneous value | $z = \sqrt{41}$ here | **after** differentiating | it is computed *from* an instantaneous value, so it inherits the restriction |

Notice what makes Q11.2 harder than Q11.1 to read.
In Q11.1 the two substitutable things looked different on the page: one was an equation with letters in it, the other a bare number.
In Q11.2 **both $x = 5$ and $y = 4$ are bare numbers**, and only the sentence they came from tells you that one may go in early and the other may not.
There is no visual cue at all.

Proof that it matters: substitute $y = 4$ into $z^2 = 25 + y^2$ before differentiating and you get $z^2 = 41$, a constant, whose derivative is $dz/dt = 0$.
The given says $dz/dt = 2000$.
You have written $0 = 2000$ and destroyed the problem, exactly as Q11.1's early substitution did.

### Eliminate early, or compute at the instant?

Both Q11.1 and Q11.2 have one equation and one variable too many.
The difference is what you do about it.

| | Q11.1 (cone) | Q11.2 (radar) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Relating equation | $V = \frac13\pi r^2 h$ | $z^2 = x^2 + y^2$ |
| Unwanted quantity | $r$ | $z$ |
| Is there a fixed ratio to it? | **yes**, $r = h/2$, stated by the stem | **no** - $z/y$ changes at every height |
| So the move is | eliminate $r$ **symbolically**, before differentiating | keep $z$, differentiate, then **compute** $z$ at the instant |
| What elimination costs | nothing; it turns $V$ into a clean $\frac{\pi h^3}{12}$ | a square root, $z = \sqrt{25+y^2}$, which then needs a chain rule |

**The deciding rule, in one sentence: eliminate a variable early when doing so makes the equation simpler, and compute it at the instant when eliminating it would introduce a radical.**

You *can* eliminate $z$ here - route B in section 4 does it, and it gets the same answer.
It is simply the worse route, because differentiating $\sqrt{25+y^2}$ is a chain rule you do not have to do.
This is the opposite verdict from Q11.1, where eliminating was clearly right, and that is the point of running both questions.

### The classify table, extended

| Cue in the stem | Relating equation | What to do with the extra variable |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A cone, "always" some shape | $V = \frac13\pi r^2 h$ | eliminate early by the fixed ratio |
| A sphere, a balloon | $V = \frac43\pi r^3$ | nothing - one variable already |
| **A right angle with one FIXED side** | $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$ | **compute the missing side at the instant** |
| A ladder against a wall | $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$, $L$ fixed | compute the other leg at the instant |
| Two vehicles leaving one point | $x^2 + y^2 = z^2$, nothing fixed | nothing - keep both rate terms |
| A shadow, a lamp post | similar triangles | eliminate early by the ratio |
| An angle of elevation | $\tan\theta = y/x$ | nothing - one variable already |

## 4. Q11.2 worked three ways

### Route A - keep the implicit form (the intended route)

The radar station does not move, so $x = 5$ at all times and Pythagoras gives

$$z^2 = x^2 + y^2 = 25 + y^2$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $t$.
Every variable is a function of $t$, so every squared term produces its own rate.

$$2z\,\frac{dz}{dt} = 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z\,\frac{dz}{dt} = y\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

The 25 contributed nothing, because the derivative of a constant is zero.

Now, and only now, evaluate at the instant.
At $y = 4$,

$$z = \sqrt{25 + 4^2} = \sqrt{41}$$

Substitute $z = \sqrt{41}$, $dz/dt = 2000$, $y = 4$:

$$\sqrt{41}\,(2000) = 4\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

$$\boxed{\ \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{2000\sqrt{41}}{4} = 500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.5621\ \text{mi/h}\ }$$

### Route B - eliminate $z$ symbolically first

Worth doing once, to see why it is the worse route rather than to use it.

$$z = \sqrt{25+y^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{25+y^2}}\cdot 2y\,\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{y}{\sqrt{25+y^2}}\,\frac{dy}{dt}$$

Solve and substitute:

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt{25+y^2}}{y}\,\frac{dz}{dt} = \frac{\sqrt{41}}{4}(2000) = 500\sqrt{41}$$

Identical, and checked against route A over a grid of 40 $(y,\,dz/dt)$ pairs.
The cost is visible: route A differentiates a polynomial, route B differentiates a radical.
That extra chain rule is a place to drop a factor of 2 for no gain.

### Route C - eliminate $y$ instead

You can also make $y$ the derived quantity, $y = \sqrt{z^2 - 25}$, giving $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \dfrac{z}{\sqrt{z^2-25}}\,\dfrac{dz}{dt}$.
At $z = \sqrt{41}$ this is $\dfrac{\sqrt{41}}{\sqrt{16}}(2000) = 500\sqrt{41}$.
This route is the one to use if the stem gives you the **radar distance** rather than the height, which is a variation the examiner can ask and section 8 covers.

### Route comparison

| Route | Steps | Where it goes wrong | Time |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A - implicit, compute $z$ at the instant | 4 | forgetting to compute $z$ and using 5 instead | ~45 s |
| B - eliminate $z$, then differentiate | 6 | the square-root chain rule | ~90 s |
| C - eliminate $y$ | 5 | only natural when $z$ is the given value | ~60 s |

**Use Route A under pressure.**
It is the shortest, it differentiates nothing harder than a square, and it is the form the course's own notes use.
The single step it must not skip is computing $z = \sqrt{41}$ **before** substituting.

## 5. The exam-ready write-up

Nothing in the four course files states a marking scheme - there are zero occurrences of "rubric", "marking scheme" or "part marks" across all of them - so which sentences earn which marks is inference, not fact.
What follows is the shortest write-up that shows every step a marker could be looking for.

> Let $y$ be the rocket's height in miles, $z$ its distance from the radar station in miles, and $t$ the time in hours.
> The station is on the ground $5$ mi from the pad and the rocket rises vertically, so the pad, the station and the rocket form a right triangle with legs $5$ and $y$ and hypotenuse $z$.
> **Given** $\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2000\ \text{mi/h}$. **Want** $\dfrac{dy}{dt}$ when $y = 4\ \text{mi}$.
>
> $z^2 = 5^2 + y^2 = 25 + y^2$.
>
> Differentiating with respect to $t$, and noting that $5$ is constant:
> $2z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt}$, so $z\dfrac{dz}{dt} = y\dfrac{dy}{dt}$.
>
> At the instant $y = 4$: $z = \sqrt{25+16} = \sqrt{41}$.
>
> Substituting: $\sqrt{41}(2000) = 4\dfrac{dy}{dt}$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.56$.
>
> The rocket is rising at $500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.56$ miles per hour.

Seven sentences.
The three easiest to omit and most likely to be looked for are the sentence identifying the right triangle, the line computing $z = \sqrt{41}$ at the instant, and the final sentence with units.

## 6. Verify before you commit

### The physical check, which is the best one this question has

The radar measures only the component of the rocket's velocity **along its own line of sight**.
The rocket is going straight up, and the line of sight is slanted, so the radar always sees *less* than the true speed.

$$\frac{dy/dt}{dz/dt} = \frac{z}{y} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{25}{y^2}} > 1 \quad\text{for every finite height}$$

So the answer **must** be greater than 2000, at every height, always.
Here the factor is $\sqrt{41}/4 \approx 1.6008$, giving $3201.56$.

This is a strong check because it kills four of the eight plausible wrong answers outright.
Anything at or below 2000 mi/h is wrong before you look at the algebra.

| Wrong answer | Value | Killed by the "must exceed 2000" check? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Ratio inverted, $\frac{y}{z}\dot z$ | 1249.39 | yes |
| The 3-4-5 decoy, $z = 3$ | 1500 | yes |
| A stray factor of 2 | 1600.78 | yes |
| Answered the given rate | 2000 | yes (it is not *greater* than 2000) |
| Read 5 as the hypotenuse | 2500 | no |
| Divided by $x$ instead of $y$ | 2561.25 | no |
| Forgot to divide by $y$ | 12806.25 | no |

### Cheap invariants

| Check | Value here | What a failure means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Is the answer bigger than 2000? | yes, $1.6008\times$ | you inverted the ratio |
| Is $z$ bigger than both 4 and 5? | $6.4031$ | you took 5 as the hypotenuse |
| Does $z^2 - y^2$ give back 25? | $41 - 16 = 25$ | your triangle is wrong |
| Sign of $dy/dt$ | positive, it is rising | you flipped a rate |
| Units | mi/h | you divided by the wrong thing |
| Round trip: does $\frac{y}{z}(500\sqrt{41})$ give 2000? | yes, exactly | your algebra is self-consistent |

That last row is the best eight-second check on this question.
Take your answer, multiply by $y/z = 4/\sqrt{41}$, and you must land back on the given 2000.

### Is the instant even reachable?

Do not assume it.
With $dz/dt$ constant at 2000 from the moment of launch ($y = 0$, so $z = 5$), we have $z(t) = 5 + 2000t$ and $y(t) = \sqrt{z(t)^2 - 25}$.
Then

$$y = 4 \iff z = \sqrt{41} \iff t = \frac{\sqrt{41}-5}{2000} \approx 0.00070156\ \text{h} \approx 2.53\ \text{s}$$

Finite and positive, so the instant is attainable.
The same screen correctly returns "never" for the question "at what height is the rocket rising exactly as fast as the radar distance grows", because that needs $y = z$ and the hypotenuse is strictly longer than a leg at every height.

## 7. Answer-entry rules

| Do | Do not |
| :--- | :--- |
| `500*sqrt(41)` | `500sqrt(41)` - implicit multiplication is not guaranteed |
| `2000*sqrt(41)/4` | `2000*sqrt(41/4)` - this is $6403.12$, exactly **twice** the answer |
| `sqrt(41)*2000/4` | `sqrt41*2000/4` - the bracket after `sqrt` is not optional |
| `500*41^(1/2)` | `500*41^1/2` - this parses as $(500 \cdot 41)/2 = 10250$ |
| `3201.5621` if a decimal is demanded | `3201.6` - that is $0.0379$ away, outside a $10^{-4}$ tolerance |
| Type `*` between every factor, then **Preview** | assume the box knows what you meant |

**The single most expensive typo is `sqrt(41/4)` in place of `sqrt(41)/4`.**
It returns 6403.12, exactly double the right answer, and 6403 looks every bit as plausible as 3201 for a rocket.
Nothing about the number will warn you.

If the box asks for the unit separately, it is **mi/h**, not mi.
The units were verified dimensionally rather than by eye: $[z][\dot z][y]^{-1} = \mathrm{mi}\cdot\mathrm{mi}\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{mi}^{-1} = \mathrm{mi}\,\mathrm{h}^{-1}$, and the same check explicitly rejects mi, mi/h², h/mi, mi²/h and a dimensionless answer.

## 8. The variation catalog

Read each table as **structure**, not as a list of facts.
For the fixed-leg right triangle there is only ever one formula,

$$\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dz}{dt}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}{y} = \frac{dz}{dt}\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^2}$$

and every entry in Family 1 is that one formula with a different $x/y$.
Only the **ratio** $x/y$ matters, never the two numbers separately - which is why $(x,y) = (5,4)$ and $(50,40)$ give the same amplification.
The magnitudes are generated by that single ratio, and the signs are generated entirely by the sign of the given rate.

Every value below was verified twice: once from the closed form, and once formula-blind, by building an explicit time parametrisation and taking a complex-step derivative of it.

### Family 0 - the same rocket, a different unknown

**Trigger:** the same stem, with the question mark moved.
**Tool:** the same relating equation; only the unknown changes place.

| Asked | Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $dy/dt$ at $y = 4$ | the exam question | $500\sqrt{41} \approx 3201.5621$ mi/h |
| $dz/dt$ if the rocket rises at 3000 at $y=4$ | run it backwards | $\frac{12000}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 1874.0851$ mi/h |
| $z$ at $y = 4$ | Pythagoras | $\sqrt{41} \approx 6.4031242$ mi |
| $dz/dy$ at $y = 4$ | $\frac{y}{z}$ | $\frac{4}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 0.6246950$ |
| $d\theta/dt$, $\theta$ the elevation angle | $\tan\theta = y/5$ | $\frac{2500}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 390.4344$ rad/h |

This is Q11.2's own family and the one to be fluent in.

Read the last column as one shape: as $y$ grows past $x$ the answer squeezes down toward the given 2000 and never reaches it, and as $y$ shrinks below $x$ it runs away to infinity.

### Family 2 - a fixed leg, the LEG rate given, find the hypotenuse rate

**Trigger:** the same picture, with the question and the given swapped.
**Tool:** $\dot z = \dot y\,\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}$. The answer is now always **smaller** than the given rate.

| Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| Softball diamond 18 m, runner 3 m from second at 7.5 m/s, distance from **home** | $\frac{112.5}{\sqrt{549}} \approx 4.8014$ m/s |
| The rocket, rising at 3000 at $y=4$: how fast does $z$ grow? | $\frac{12000}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 1874.0851$ mi/h |
| Kite 40 m out horizontally, 30 m up, rising at 5 m/s | $3$ m/s exactly |

The softball question in `MATH265_Categorized.md` is exactly this family, and its "fixed leg" is the 18 m baseline from home to first.
The runner moves along the first-to-second line, which is perpendicular to it.

**Family 1 and Family 2 are the same picture run in opposite directions**, and the sanity check is that they undo each other: feed Family 1's answer into Family 2 and you get the original given back.

### Family 3 - a fixed HYPOTENUSE (the ladder). The sign flips.

**Trigger:** a ladder, a rod, a rope of fixed length, anything that cannot stretch.
**Tool:** $x^2 + y^2 = L^2$ with $L$ constant, so $x\dot x + y\dot y = 0$ and $\dot y = -\dfrac{x\dot x}{y}$.

| Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| $L=10$ ft, foot 6 ft out, moving out at 1 ft/s | $-0.75$ ft/s |
| $L=13$ ft, foot 5 ft out, moving out at 2 ft/s | $-\frac56 \approx -0.8333$ ft/s |
| $L=13$ ft, foot 12 ft out, moving out at 2 ft/s | $-\frac{24}{5} = -4.8$ ft/s |
| $L=25$ ft, foot 15 ft out, moving out at 3 ft/s | $-2.25$ ft/s |

**This is the family most often confused with Q11.2, and the confusion is worth naming.**
Both are right triangles. Both use Pythagoras.
But in Q11.2 a **leg** is fixed and the hypotenuse varies, while in the ladder the **hypotenuse** is fixed and both legs vary.
The differentiated equations are therefore different shapes - $z\dot z = y\dot y$ against $x\dot x + y\dot y = 0$ - and the answers have opposite signs.
Ask yourself which side cannot change; that one question sorts the two families every time.

The two $L=13$ rows also teach something real: the top of a ladder accelerates as it falls, from $-0.83$ ft/s to $-4.8$ ft/s for the same steady foot speed, and it approaches infinite speed as the ladder goes flat.

### Family 4 - nothing fixed, both legs vary

**Trigger:** two things leaving the same point in perpendicular directions, each with its own given rate.
**Tool:** do **not** eliminate. Differentiate to $z\dot z = x\dot x + y\dot y$ and keep both terms.

| Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| One car east at 60 mph, one north at 80 mph, one hour later | $100$ mph exactly |
| Ships 16 km east and 12 km south, at 20 and 15 km/h | $25$ km/h exactly |
| $x=9$, $y=12$, $\dot x = 3$, $\dot y = -4$ | $-1.4$ units/s |

The third row is the point of the family: the separation **shrinks** even though one leg is growing.
When two rates fight, the sign of the answer is not the sign of either input and you cannot guess it.
This is Q11.1's Family 6 in Pythagorean clothing, and setting either rate to zero collapses it back into Q11.2's own shape.

### Family 5 - a trig relation on the same triangle

**Trigger:** an angle, an angle of elevation, a rotating camera or searchlight.
**Tool:** $\tan\theta = y/x$, differentiated to $\sec^2\theta\,\dot\theta = \dot y/x$, so $\dot\theta = \dfrac{x\dot y}{x^2+y^2}$.

| Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| The rocket's elevation angle at the exam instant | $\frac{2500}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 390.4344$ rad/h |
| Balloon rising 2 m/s, observer 50 m away, at height 50 m | $0.02$ rad/s exactly |
| Satellite, $h = r(\csc\theta - 1)$, $r = 6378$, at $\theta = \pi/6$ | $\frac{dh}{d\theta} = -12756\sqrt3 \approx -22094.04$ km/rad |

The rocket row is the most likely reworded version of this exam question, because it uses the identical triangle and the identical instant.
Note that its answer is in **radians per hour**, so the dimensional check in section 7 has to be redone from scratch rather than reused.

### Family 6 - the Q11.1 contrast: elimination by a fixed ratio

**Trigger:** the stem contains "always", "at all times", or a tank whose walls fix the shape.
**Tool:** eliminate the unwanted variable **symbolically, before differentiating**.

| Setup | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| Gravel cone, base diameter equals height, $\dot V = 0.5$, $h = 4$ | $\frac{1}{8\pi} \approx 0.0397887$ m/min |
| Same cone with $r = h$ | $\frac{1}{32\pi} \approx 0.0099472$ m/min |
| Lamp post 5 m, person 2 m walking away at 1.5 m/s, **tip** of shadow | $2.5$ m/s exactly |

This family is here to mark the boundary, not to be re-taught - it is Q11.1's whole content.
Put it beside Family 1 and read the two triggers together: **"always" means eliminate, a fixed number means compute at the instant.**

### Family 7 - inverse and asymptotic questions on the same rocket

**Trigger:** "at what height", "how long until", "what happens as it climbs".
**Tool:** the same formula, solved for a different letter.

| Asked | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| At what height is the rocket rising at 2500 mi/h? | $\frac{20}{3} \approx 6.6667$ mi |
| $dy/dt$ at $y = 8$ | $250\sqrt{89} \approx 2358.4953$ mi/h |
| Doubling the height from 4 to 8 divides the answer by | $\approx 1.3575$, **not** 4 |
| What happens to $dy/dt$ as $y \to \infty$? | it decreases toward 2000 and never reaches it |
| What happens as $y \to 0^+$? | it blows up; near the pad the radar barely notices a climb |

The doubling row is a deliberate trap for anyone who memorised Q11.1.
There, doubling the height divided the answer by exactly 4, because the relationship was a clean power law.
Here it divides it by about 1.36, because $\sqrt{1 + 25/y^2}$ is not a power of $y$ at all.
**Q11.1's scaling intuition does not transfer, and assuming it does is the most sophisticated way to get this question wrong.**

## 9. The trap list, ranked by how often it costs marks

**1. Reading 5 as the hypotenuse.**
The radar is 5 mi from the **launch pad**, not from the rocket.
Using $z = 5$ gives $\frac{5}{4}(2000) = 2500$ mi/h, about 22 percent low.
This is the single most common wrong answer on the question, and it survives every sanity check except "is $z$ bigger than both legs".

**2. The 3-4-5 decoy.**
The stem prints a 5 and a 4, and every student has 3-4-5 burned in.
But here **4 and 5 are both legs**, so the third side is $\sqrt{41} \approx 6.403$, not 3.
Writing $z = 3$ gives 1500 mi/h, 53 percent low.
Had the radar been 3 mi away instead of 5, the answer really would have been 2500, so this is a reachable mistake rather than an absurd one - which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

**3. Substituting $y = 4$ before differentiating.**
The defining error of the whole category, carried over unchanged from Q11.1.
Here it makes $z^2 = 41$, a constant, so $dz/dt = 0$ and you have written $0 = 2000$.
Note the cruelty of this question's design: $x = 5$ **may** be substituted early and $y = 4$ may not, and both look like bare numbers on the page.

**4. Inverting the ratio.**
Writing $\dot y = \frac{y}{z}\dot z$ instead of $\frac{z}{y}\dot z$ gives $\frac{8000}{\sqrt{41}} \approx 1249.39$ mi/h.
The physical check kills this instantly: the answer must be larger than 2000, never smaller.

**5. Dividing by $x$ instead of $y$.**
Writing $\dot y = \frac{z}{5}\dot z$ gives $400\sqrt{41} \approx 2561.25$, exactly 20 percent low.
This one has a hiding point - see below - and it is the most under-detected slip on the question.

**6. Answering the given rate.**
"How fast is the rocket rising" is $dy/dt$.
It is not $dz/dt = 2000$, which was handed to you, and it is not $d\theta/dt$.

**7. Dropping the chain factor.**
Writing $2z\dot z = 2y$ with no $\dot y$ leaves an equation with no unknown in it.
If the rate you are solving for is not in your differentiated equation, stop and re-differentiate.

**8. Reporting the wrong units.**
The answer is a length per time, **mi/h**.
No numerical check will catch a unit error, which is why the dimensional check gets its own line in section 7.

**9. The `sqrt(41/4)` typo in the Möbius box.**
Returns 6403.12, exactly double, and looks entirely plausible.

### Where a wrong answer hides

Eight wrong formulas were scanned across a grid of eleven heights and five rates, deliberately **including** the degenerate points rather than excluding them.

- **At $dz/dt = 0$, every single one of the eight agrees with the truth**, because they all differ from it by a multiplicative factor. A spot-check on a rocket that is not moving cannot fail, so it proves nothing. This is the identical finding to Q11.1's zero-rate blind spot, and it is now confirmed across both questions in the category.
- **At $y = 5$, the "divided by $x$ instead of $y$" slip is exactly invisible**, because that is the one height where $x$ and $y$ are the same number. And 5 is *the number printed in the stem*, so it is the single most tempting height to spot-check at. Check somewhere else.
- **At $y = 1$, the "forgot to divide by $y$" slip is exactly invisible**, for the same reason it was at $h = 1$ in Q11.1: the hiding point is wherever the erroneous factor equals 1.
- **As $y \to 0$, reading 5 as the hypotenuse becomes invisible**, because $\sqrt{25+y^2} \to 5$. At $y = 0.001$ the two agree to better than one part in a million. A rocket 5 feet off the pad cannot distinguish them; a rocket 4 miles up separates them by 22 percent.
- **The other six slips have no height-dependent hiding point at all** and are wrong everywhere the rate is nonzero. Six of eight, measured rather than assumed.

The general rule, now confirmed on both questions in this category: **a hiding point exists wherever the factor your error touches happens to equal 1, or wherever two of the stem's own numbers coincide.** Compute where that is before choosing a check point.

## 10. Drill set

Score **sign** and **magnitude** separately.
A wrong sign means you mis-read which side was fixed, which is a reading problem.
A wrong magnitude means you dropped or inverted a factor, which is an algebra problem.
The two need different remediation, so do not lump them together.

1. The same rocket and radar: how fast is it rising when it is 12 mi high?
2. The same rocket at 4 mi, but the radar distance is growing at only 500 mi/h.
3. At the exam instant, how fast is the **angle of elevation** from the station changing?
4. A radar station 12 mi from the pad. How fast is the rocket rising at 5 mi high with $\dot z = 2000$?
5. A radar station 3 mi from the pad. How fast is the rocket rising at 4 mi high with $\dot z = 2000$?
6. The rocket is at 4 mi and rising at 3000 mi/h. How fast is its distance from the station growing?
7. A 13 ft ladder leans on a wall. The foot is pulled out at 2 ft/s. How fast is the top sliding down when the foot is 5 ft from the wall?
8. A winch on a dock sits 5 ft above a boat's bow and hauls rope in at 2 ft/s. How fast does the boat approach the dock when it is 12 ft out?
9. A softball diamond is 18 m square. A runner going first to second is 3 m from second at 7.5 m/s. At what rate is her distance from home changing?
10. One car drives east at 60 mph, another north at 80 mph, from the same point at the same time. How fast are they separating one hour later?
11. For the exam's rocket, at what height is it rising exactly twice as fast as its radar distance grows?
12. Gravel at 0.5 m³/min forms a cone whose base diameter equals its height. How fast is the height rising at $h = 4$?

### Answer key

| # | Exact | Decimal | Family and method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | $\frac{6500}{3}$ mi/h | 2166.6667 | F1 - $z = 13$, a real 5-12-13 |
| 2 | $125\sqrt{41}$ mi/h | 800.3905 | F1 - same triangle, quarter the rate |
| 3 | $\frac{2500}{\sqrt{41}}$ rad/h | 390.4344 | F5 - $\dot\theta = x\dot y/(x^2+y^2)$; note the units |
| 4 | $5200$ mi/h | 5200 exactly | F1 - $z = 13$ again, legs swapped |
| 5 | $2500$ mi/h | 2500 exactly | F1 - the genuine 3-4-5 |
| 6 | $\frac{12000}{\sqrt{41}}$ mi/h | 1874.0851 | F2 - run backwards; must be **less** than 3000 |
| 7 | $-\frac56$ ft/s | -0.8333333 | F3 - fixed **hypotenuse**; sign must be negative |

Drills 7 and 8 are the discrimination pair, and they are the most valuable two minutes in this set.
Both are right triangles with a 5, a 12 and a 13.
In drill 7 the **13 is fixed** and the answer is $-5/6$; in drill 8 the **5 is fixed** and the answer is $-13/6$.
Same numbers, different fixed side, answers differing by a factor of 2.6.
If you can sort those two on sight you have the whole family.

Drill 3 is the units drill: the answer is not in miles per hour.
Drill 12 is the elimination drill: it is the one question here where you substitute before differentiating.

## 11. The one-page decision tree

```
READ THE STEM
   |
   +-- underline every RATE   ("at a rate of", "per hour", "how fast")
   +-- circle every VALUE     ("when", "at the instant", "is 4 mi high")
   +-- box every FIXED LENGTH ("on the ground 5 mi from", "a 13 ft ladder")
   |
   v
WRITE TWO LINES BEFORE ANY CALCULUS
   GIVEN: d?/dt = ____ units
   WANT:  d?/dt = ?    at the instant ____ = ____
   |
   v
PICK THE RELATING EQUATION
   |
   +-- cone / sphere / cube ................ a volume formula
   +-- a right angle, two distances ........ x^2 + y^2 = z^2
   +-- "tall" + "away" + a lamp ............ similar triangles
   +-- an angle ............................ tan / csc
   |
   v
FOR EACH QUANTITY YOU DO NOT WANT, ASK: CAN ITS RATE BE ZERO?
   |
   +-- it is a CONSTANT NUMBER (the radar's 5, the ladder's 13)
   |        ==> substitute NOW. Its rate is 0 and its term vanishes.
   |
   +-- it is tied by a FIXED RATIO the stem calls "always" (r = h/2)
   |        ==> substitute NOW, symbolically. It holds at every instant.
   |
   +-- it is an INSTANTANEOUS VALUE ("when y = 4")
   |        ==> DO NOT substitute. Its rate is what you are solving for.
   |
   +-- it is DERIVED from an instantaneous value (z at that moment)
            ==> differentiate first, THEN compute it from the relation.
   |
   v
   IF you could eliminate but it would create a SQUARE ROOT, don't.
   Keep the implicit form. z^2 = 25 + y^2 beats z = sqrt(25 + y^2).
   |
   v
DIFFERENTIATE WITH RESPECT TO t
   every variable gains its own rate as a trailing factor
   constants contribute nothing, because their rate is zero
   |
   v
IS THE RATE YOU WANT ACTUALLY IN THE EQUATION?
   no  -> you dropped a chain factor. Go back.
   yes -> continue
   |
   v
COMPUTE THE MISSING SIDE AT THE INSTANT, THEN SUBSTITUTE, THEN SOLVE
   |
   v
CHECK
   which side was fixed?  a fixed LEG  -> z zdot = y ydot,  answer > given
                          a fixed HYP  -> x xdot + y ydot = 0, answer NEGATIVE
                          nothing fixed-> z zdot = x xdot + y ydot
   round trip: multiply your answer by y/z - do you get the given back?
   units     : work them out, do not guess
   not at y = 5, not at y = 1, not at a zero rate - wrong answers hide there
   |
   v
PREVIEW THE MÖBIUS BOX
   sqrt(41)/4   not   sqrt(41/4)      500*sqrt(41)   not   500sqrt(41)
```

## 12. Closing the category: one procedure, two questions

Category 11 is now complete, and the two questions divide the work cleanly.
Neither is redundant.

| | Q11.1 - gravel cone | Q11.2 - rocket and radar |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Paper, item, points | MT1 item 7, 5 pts | MT2 item 11, 6 pts |
| Relating equation | $V = \frac13\pi r^2h$ - **must be memorised** | $z^2 = x^2+y^2$ - **must be recognised** |
| The hard reading | converting "diameter equals height" to $r = h/2$ | seeing that 5 and 4 are both legs |
| The extra variable | $r$, removed **symbolically** by a fixed ratio | $z$, **computed** at the instant |
| Which substitution is fatal | $h = 4$ early | $y = 4$ early |
| Which substitution is safe | $r = h/2$ (a relation holding always) | $x = 5$ (a constant) |
| How the answer scales | a clean power law; doubling $h$ divides by 4 | not a power law; doubling $y$ divides by 1.36 |
| Sanity anchor | $dh/dt = \dot V / (\text{base area})$ | the answer must **exceed** the given rate |
| Sign | always positive here | positive here, negative in the ladder variant |
| It teaches | **when you may substitute early** | **what to do when you may not** |

**The single procedure that covers both**, and everything in section 8:

> 1. Name every changing quantity and give it units. Give every unchanging quantity a number.
> 2. Write GIVEN and WANT as rates, before touching any calculus.
> 3. Write one equation relating them, from geometry.
> 4. Sort every unwanted quantity into one of four boxes: constant number, always-true relation, instantaneous value, or derived-from-an-instantaneous-value. The first two go in now; the last two wait.
> 5. Differentiate with respect to $t$. Every variable contributes its own rate.
> 6. Compute whatever the instant requires, substitute, and solve.
> 7. Check the sign against physics, check the units dimensionally, and answer in a sentence.

Step 4 is the whole category.
Q11.1 teaches its first two boxes and Q11.2 teaches its last two, which is why doing only one of them leaves you half-prepared.
